AWhat Q26 asks you to do

Read this first. It explains the question in plain English.

On the exam paper, one of the 15 poems is printed in full. The question asks you to compare how poets present an idea (a theme) in that poem and in one other poem that you choose from the 15 Power and Conflict poems. The second poem is not printed, so you write about it from memory.

The question always looks like this:

Compare how poets present [a theme, for example “ideas about power”] in [the printed poem] and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’. [30 marks]

You must write about two poems

If you only write about the printed poem, AQA's mark scheme says your answer can be “capped” at the top of Level 2. That means a maximum of 10 out of 30, however good it is. So always choose a second poem and write about both.

Time and marks

Section B is worth 30 of the 96 marks on Paper 2. AQA doesn't set a time for each section; about 45 minutes is a sensible guide (5 minutes planning, 40 minutes writing). timing is advice, not an AQA rule

Which poems could come up?

All 15 are listed on the exam paper, and any of them can be the printed poem. AQA's specification says students “should study all 15 poems in their chosen cluster and be prepared to write about any of them in the examination.” The paper also lists two other clusters (Love and relationships, and Worlds and lives): you only answer the Power and conflict question.

BWhere the 30 marks come from

AQA's mark scheme splits the marks into three “assessment objectives” (AOs).
AO1 · Ideas and argument
12

A clear argument that answers the question, compares the two poems, and uses well-chosen references.

AO2 · How the poets write
12

Analysis of the poets' words, structure and form, and the effect of their choices.

AO3 · Context
6

The ideas, times and situations behind the poems, linked to the question.

What the top level (26–30 marks) looks like, in AQA's words

“a critical, exploratory, well-structured argument. It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task supported by a range of judicious references to the text. There will be a fine-grained and insightful analysis of methods supported by judicious use of subject terminology. Convincing exploration of themes/ideas/perspectives/contextual factors/interpretations.” AQA mark scheme, June 2025

In plain English: have one big idea that answers the question and keep proving it; choose short, precise quotations; zoom in on individual words and explain their effect; use technical terms only where they help; and link context to the poems' ideas.

Good news: AQA says examiners should “reward any valid interpretations”. The readings in this guide are strong options, not the only right answers.

How to get the AO2 marks (language, structure, form)

  • Explain effects, don't just name techniques. AQA's 2022 report: spotting techniques “will not achieve above Level 2 AO2 if it is not tied to meaning.”
  • Zoom in on single words. “You don't need to label parts of speech in order to do well. If you want to talk about the effect of a particular word choice, just use the word ‘word’!” (2023 report)
  • For structure, use openings, endings and turning points. AQA's 2023 report says helpful structure points are “openings/endings, plot development, changes of pace / scene”.
  • You don't have to cover everything. The mark scheme rewards “language and/or structure and/or form”, and the 2023 report says structure is not “a requirement”.
  • Avoid listing rhyme schemes (“the observation of a rhyme scheme [ABAB] is best avoided”, 2022) and empty form comments such as “sonnets are love poems”.
  • A realistic target: 3–4 short quotations analysed closely from each poem, plus one or two structure or form points linked to meaning. our advice

EWhat examiners say

Copied word for word from AQA's examiners' reports (Nov 2020, Nov 2021, June 2022, June 2023).

Do this

  • Start with a thesis. “Students who began their answer with a thesis statement that they could develop / explain / illustrate during their response, and perhaps return to at the end, tended to be more successful with AO1.” (2020)
  • Simple structure is fine. “You can get just as many marks by offering an introduction that shows your understanding of the connection between the named poem and the one you have chosen to use, then dealing with each poem separately, then perhaps drawing the two poems together at the end of your response.” (2023)
  • Short quotations. “A well-selected word or phrase from the text is often much more effective than a long direct quotation.” (2023)
  • Context = the question's ideas. “The focus for AO3 will be in the question – just talk about that. Know the what, the how and the why.” (2023)
  • Think wider than the story. “some of the best responses took a wider approach to the task focus” (2020).

Avoid this

  • Only writing about the printed poem. In 2020 some students “only responded to the named poem”.
  • Formula paragraphs. They “tended to become self-contained ‘mini-essays’” and “encourage bolted-on context” (2020).
  • Bolted-on history. “paragraphs about, for example, Owen's war experiences, Hughes' father as a soldier or extraneous information about Guardsman Tromans were not useful if they meant the student lost sight of the poem.” (2022)
  • Misreading My Last Duchess. Some thought the Duke “is grieving the loss of his first wife, which means they missed the point of the poem entirely.” (2023)
Reassurance: “Don't worry if you can't remember exact quotations. It's fine to pinpoint parts of a poem and it's also understandable if you slightly misquote because the examiner will get the idea.” (AQA examiners' report, 2022)

Ozymandias

Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792–1822) · published 1818

The story in short

A traveller describes a giant statue of an ancient king, now broken and lying in the empty desert.

The message

However powerful people think they are, their power doesn't last. Time and nature outlive everyone.

Why it was written

Shelley was a radical (someone who wants big changes to society) who rejected kings and the powerful. This poem quietly mocks a tyrant.

The title

“Ozymandias” is the Greek name for the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II. By naming the poem after a real, famous king, Shelley makes it about all-powerful rulers, not just one statue. (University of Toronto; Britannica)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe poet tells us what a traveller told him about a trip to an “antique land” (an ancient country). So we hear the story second-hand.
  • Lines 1–3In the desert stand two huge stone legs. The rest of the statue's body has gone.
  • Lines 4–5Nearby, the statue's broken face lies half-buried in the sand. It still has a frown and a cruel “sneer of cold command”.
  • Lines 6–8The sculptor understood exactly what this king was like and carved his arrogance into the stone. The king is long dead, but the carving still shows his character.
  • Lines 9–11Words on the base boast that he is “king of kings”. They tell other powerful rulers to look at his achievements and “despair”, because they'll never match him.
  • Lines 12–14But there are no achievements left to see. Only empty sand, stretching as far as the eye can see.
The turning point: The killer moment (line 12): “Nothing beside remains.” Straight after the boast, three blunt words show it was empty. How it ends: On the desert, not the king. Nature and time get the last word. The tone: Calm and quietly mocking. Shelley doesn't need to attack the king; the ruins do it for him.

2The poem, with what it means

Highlighted words are the ones worth quoting in the exam.
The poem
What it means, in plain English
I met a traveller from an antique land
Who said: Two vast and trunkless legs of stone
Stand in the desert. Near them on the sand,
Someone tells the poet about a trip to an ancient country. All that's left of a giant statue is two legs. “trunkless” = the body (trunk) has gone.
Half sunk, a shatter’d visage lies, whose frown
And wrinkled lip and sneer of cold command
Near the legs, the broken face (“visage” = face) is half-buried. It still shows a cruel, bossy sneer. This king looked down on everyone.
Tell that its sculptor well those passions read
Which yet survive, stamp’d on these lifeless things,
The hand that mock’d them and the heart that fed;
The sculptor “read” the king's feelings well and carved them in stone. “mock’d” means copied, and also made fun of. The art has lasted; the king hasn't.
And on the pedestal these words appear:
‘My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:
Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!’
The king's boast, carved on the base, tells other rulers to look at what he built and give up, because they can never match him. Irony: there's nothing left to look at.
Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare,
The lone and level sands stretch far away.
Just empty desert all around the huge (“colossal”) ruin. Time and nature have beaten the “king of kings”.

3Key quotations to learn

Learn these word for word. Short quotations score better than long ones.
LINE 1
“I met a traveller from an antique land”
Means: the story is second-hand and very old. Why it matters: the king is pushed far into the past.
LINE 2
“Two vast and trunkless legs of stone”
Means: huge, but broken. Why it matters: his ambition is now just fragments.
LINE 4
“Half sunk, a shatter’d visage lies”
Means: his face is sinking into the sand. Why it matters: words like “sunk” and “shatter’d” show collapse.
LINE 5
“sneer of cold command”
Means: he was a cruel, bossy ruler. Technique: alliteration; the hard “c” sounds feel harsh.
LINE 8
“The hand that mock’d them and the heart that fed”
Means: the artist copied, and mocked, the king. Why it matters: art judges the tyrant.
LINE 10
“‘My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:”
Means: he thought he was the greatest ruler ever. Technique: a boastful title, full of pride.
LINE 11
“Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!’”
Means: give up: you'll never beat me. Technique: a command (imperative), now ironic.
LINES 12 AND 14
“Nothing beside remains.” / “The lone and level sands stretch far away.”
Means: nothing is left but desert. Technique: a short blunt sentence, then alliteration.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: what a sonnet is, and what Shelley does with it
About the form
  • A 14-line poem, usually in iambic pentameter: about ten syllables a line, in a da-DUM, da-DUM rhythm.
  • It began in Italy and was perfected by the poet Petrarch in the 14th century. Britannica; EBSCO
  • Traditionally used for love poems, but later poets used it for other subjects, including politics. Britannica; EBSCO politics: one source
  • Why poets use it: it is built like an argument. It sets up a problem, then a volta (Italian for “turn”) changes direction to answer it. Britannica; poets.org; Poetry Archive
  • Petrarchan (Italian): 8 lines (octave) + 6 lines (sestet); rhyme ABBAABBA, then CDECDE or similar; the turn comes between lines 8 and 9: the octave sets up a problem, the sestet answers it. Britannica; poets.org; EBSCO
  • Shakespearean (English): three 4-line quatrains + a 2-line couplet; rhyme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG; the final couplet often sums up or twists the argument. Britannica; poets.org; Poetry Archive
What this poem does with it
  • So which type is Ozymandias? Neither: Shelley breaks the rules.
  • It has 14 lines of iambic pentameter, the traditional elements. Gale, Poetry for Students
  • But its rhyme scheme is ABABACDCEDEFEF, which matches neither type: “Rather than adhere to the English or Petrarchan rhyme schemes, Shelley does something different”. Gale; University of Toronto
  • There is no neat rhyming couplet at the end, so the poem refuses a tidy ending.
  • Its sense splits like a Petrarchan sonnet, 8 lines then 6, but the rhymes run across the split (“things” in line 7 rhymes with “kings” in line 10). EBSCO; our check
  • Why it matters: one critic suggests the rhyme scheme “gradually changes over time, just as the subject matter (Ozymandias's statue) does.” Gale A broken, shifting form for a broken, crumbling statue.
  • The turn: the octave describes the ruined statue; line 9, “And on the pedestal these words appear:”, turns to the king's own boast, and the sestet shows how hollow it is. our reading AQA's specimen mark scheme invites comments on the “use / effect of volta”.
A sentence you could use: Shelley writes a sonnet, a tightly controlled form, but breaks its rules: the rhyme scheme fits neither the Petrarchan nor the Shakespearean pattern, and there is no closing couplet. Like the statue, the form itself is half-ruined, so even the shape of the poem shows that power cannot hold together.
Three voices, one inside another

The poet, then the traveller, then the king's words on the stone. Each layer pushes the king further into the past.

Frame narrative: a story told inside another story.
Irony

The boast “Look on my works” is followed straight away by “Nothing beside remains.” The poem itself proves the boast wrong.

Irony: when the result is the opposite of what someone meant or expected.
Ending on the sand

The last image is the desert, not the king. AQA's 2018 mark scheme suggests writing about the sand as a “metaphor for the passing of time and impermanence of human constructions” (impermanence = not lasting).

Metaphor: describing one thing as if it were another.
A sudden stop

Line 12 breaks off after “Nothing beside remains.” The full stop in the middle of the line is a sudden silence straight after the king's shouting.

Caesura: a pause or break in the middle of a line of poetry.
Examiners warn: don't write “he uses a sonnet because sonnets are love poems”. AQA's 2023 report says this argument goes nowhere. Link the form to the poem's meaning instead.

Who Shelley was

  • Born in 1792 into a rich Sussex family; his father was an MP. Went to Eton, then Oxford. Britannica
  • Britannica calls him the “most politically radical” of the Romantic poets: “He rejected the monarchy, religion, war, and commerce.” Britannica power · tyranny
  • Oxford expelled him in 1811 for refusing to say whether he wrote a pamphlet called The Necessity of Atheism. Britannica; University College Oxford power · rebellion
  • He believed poets could change society. In a later essay (published 1840) he wrote: “Poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world.” Britannica art outlasts power
  • He married Mary Godwin, who wrote Frankenstein (1818). He drowned off Italy in 1822, aged 29. Britannica

Britain when he wrote it

  • King George III had been declared insane in 1811, so his unpopular son ruled as Prince Regent. Britannica power · rulers
  • Criticising the ruler was dangerous: the editors of The Examiner, the paper that printed Ozymandias, were jailed in 1813 for attacking the Prince Regent. Britannica; University of Iowa tyranny
  • Napoleon, whose army had invaded Egypt in 1798, was finally defeated at Waterloo in 1815. Britannica power doesn't last link is our reading
  • After the poem: at Peterloo (1819), soldiers attacked a peaceful protest in Manchester, killing about 11 people. Shelley answered with The Mask of Anarchy. Britannica; University of Manchester power vs the people

The real king and the real statue

  • “Ozymandias” is the Greek name for Ramesses II, a pharaoh of Egypt in the 13th century BC. University of Toronto; Britannica pride · power
  • An ancient Greek historian, Diodorus, recorded the statue's boast: “King of Kings am I, Osymandyas. If anyone would know how great I am and where I lie, let him surpass one of my works.” Shelley's line 10 echoes it. Diodorus (University of Chicago); University of Toronto pride
  • Members of Napoleon's expedition to Egypt are said to have tried and failed to remove a huge stone bust of Ramesses; a hole in it is said to be theirs. Belzoni removed it in 1816 and it reached the British Museum in 1818, after the poem was published. British Museum (via Google Arts & Culture); Britannica power · empire

How the poem came about

  • Shelley wrote it in a friendly competition with his friend Horace Smith; both wrote a sonnet called “Ozymandias”. Britannica; University of Toronto
  • It appeared in The Examiner, a radical newspaper, on 11 January 1818, under the made-up name “Glirastes”. Britannica; University of Toronto tyranny
  • Shelley was a second-generation Romantic, with Keats and Byron. The Romantics valued imagination, feeling and nature. Britannica nature · time
  • AQA's 2023 examiners' report praised essays that saw the Romantics “as disrupters / advocates for social change”. AQA 2023 examiners' report power
Putting it into an answer (AO3): Shelley published the poem in January 1818, when Britain was ruled by an unpopular Prince Regent and newspaper editors could be jailed for criticising him. By showing a king who called himself “king of kings” reduced to a broken statue, he suggests that every ruler's power will fade too.

Use for: NaturePower · AQA: specimen and 2018 mark schemes
Similar
  • Both show human power is weaker than nature: the king's empire is now “lone and level sands”; the islanders are helpless as the wind “strafes invisibly.”
  • Both show confidence that turns out to be hollow: “king of kings” and “We are prepared:”
Different
  • Speed: in Ozymandias nature wins slowly, over thousands of years. In Storm it attacks right now: “Space is a salvo,”
  • Voice: Ozymandias is told second-hand by a traveller, so it feels distant. Storm is spoken by a “we” living through it, so it feels close and frightening.
  • Ending: Shelley ends calmly mocking (“Nothing beside remains.”); Heaney ends in fear (“a huge nothing that we fear.”)
B
Ozymandias + My Last Duchess
Use for: PowerPride · AQA: specimen mark scheme (“despotism”); one of the two most popular partners in 2023, with London (examiners' report)
Similar
  • Both show an arrogant ruler, proud of his status: “king of kings” and “My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name”
  • Both rulers are judged through a work of art: a statue and a portrait.
  • Both rulers give themselves away through their own words.
Different
  • Whose power: Ozymandias ruled a kingdom; the Duke controls one woman, and brought about her death: “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.”
  • Does the power last? Ozymandias's power has crumbled to nothing. The Duke's power is still alive: he is arranging his next marriage.
  • Form: a short sonnet told second-hand, against a long dramatic monologue in the Duke's own voice.
C
Ozymandias + London
Use for: Power · AQA: 2018 mark scheme; “an excellent choice” (2021 examiners' report)
Similar
  • Both poets attack those in power: a tyrant king, and the Church and palace.
  • Both show the marks power leaves behind: the “sneer of cold command” carved in stone, and “Marks of weakness, marks of woe” on people's faces.
Different
  • Who suffers: Shelley focuses on the ruler; Blake focuses on the ordinary people who suffer: “Runs in blood down palace walls.”
  • Does the power last? Shelley shows power fading. Blake shows it everywhere and still crushing people: “The mind-forged manacles I hear”
  • Voice: second-hand and distant, against Blake's first-person “I”, walking the streets now.

7In the exam

Compare how poets present ideas about power in ‘Ozymandias’ and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’. (AQA, June 2018)
A strong opening (thesis): Both Shelley and Heaney show that human power is weaker than it looks, but Shelley shows it fading slowly over time, while Heaney shows nature attacking it head-on.
A model comparison paragraph:
Both poets present human power as fragile when set against nature. Shelley's ruler calls himself “king of kings”, yet the poem replies with the blunt sentence “Nothing beside remains.” His empire has become “lone and level sands”: nature has defeated him slowly, over thousands of years. Heaney's islanders also begin confidently, claiming “We are prepared”, but the storm attacks them right now. The wind “strafes invisibly” and “Space is a salvo”, military words that make nature an army they cannot fight. Where Shelley ends calmly mocking the tyrant's pride, Heaney ends in fear of “a huge nothing”. Together they suggest that human power, whether a king's or a community's, is weaker than it looks.
Why it scores:
  • Opens with a clear point that answers the question about power
  • Short quotations from both poems, each one explained
  • Compares all the way through (“also”, “Where…”) and names methods

The story in short

A man walks through the streets of London. Everywhere he looks and listens, he finds people who are suffering.

The message

The people with power (the Church, the king and the rich) are crushing ordinary people, and people's own minds keep them trapped.

Why it was written

Blake was a Londoner and a radical (someone who wants big changes to society) who opposed the monarchy. He wrote this in the years of the French Revolution, when questioning power was dangerous our reading. poets.org; Tate; British Library

The title

Just one word: the name of Britain's capital, where Blake was born and died. By naming a real city, Blake makes the poem a direct attack on his own society, not a made-up place. Britannica calls the poem “a critical commentary on the social and political conditions of London during Blake's time.” (Britannica; poets.org)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker (“I”) walks through London's streets. He tells us what he sees, and then, more and more, what he hears.
  • Lines 1–4He wanders through streets, and near a river, that are “chartered”: owned and controlled. In every face he meets he sees signs of weakness and sadness.
  • Lines 5–8He hears the same misery in every cry, from grown men to frightened babies. Behind it all he hears “mind-forged manacles”: chains made by the mind.
  • Lines 9–10He hears the cry of child chimney sweeps. The soot-blackened churches should be horrified by it, but they do nothing.
  • Lines 11–12He hears the sigh of an unlucky soldier, and pictures it as blood running down the walls of the royal palace.
  • Lines 13–16Worst of all, at midnight he hears a young prostitute cursing. Her curse (and her disease) harms new-born babies and turns marriage into a funeral.
The turning point (line 13): “But most”. The poem has built up from faces to cries, and now Blake tells us the very worst sound of all comes at night. How it ends: On death and disease spreading into marriage and new life. The poem ends with no hope and no way out. The tone: Angry and bitter, but tightly controlled. The steady, song-like rhythm makes the horror feel endless.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: a simple song with a terrible message
About the form
  • London has four quatrains (4-line stanzas) with an ABAB rhyme scheme: street / flow / meet / woe. our count
  • One critic calls them “ballad quatrains”: the plain, regular stanza of songs and ballads. Cash, English Association one source
  • The rhythm is mostly iambic tetrameter: four beats a line, da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM. Cash, English Association one source
  • It comes from Songs of Innocence and of Experience. Blake modelled the songs on simple moral songs written for children, like Isaac Watts's Divine Songs (1715), then turned them upside down. British Library
What this poem does with it
  • Why use a nursery-song shape for horror? The neat, regular form clashes with what it describes. Cash (“sardonic” means grimly mocking) calls it a “sardonic conflict between the alternate rhymes of his ballad quatrains and the grotesque experience which they describe.” Cash one source
  • The beat is like walking. The steady four-beat lines match the speaker's footsteps, and the misery never stops. our reading The Poetry Archive introduction praises its “pounding rhythm”. Poetry Archive
  • The rhythm breaks in stanzas 3–4. Lines like “How the chimney-sweeper’s cry” have only seven syllables and start on a strong beat, so they hit harder. our count
  • No escape in the structure. Each stanza moves to a darker place: faces, cries, Church and palace, then midnight. The last line ends on a “hearse”. our reading
  • Seeing, then hearing. The poem starts with “mark” (seeing) but switches to “I hear” (lines 8 and 13). Cash notes that the experience of the city is “auditory”. Cash; our check one source
A sentence you could use: Blake uses regular ABAB quatrains, the simple shape of a children's song, to describe child labour, war and disease; the clash between the neat form and the ugly content makes the suffering more shocking, and the steady beat suggests it never ends.
Relentless repetition

“every” comes seven times, and “cry” three times. Blake piles them up so the suffering seems to be everywhere and to affect everyone. AQA's 2021 mark scheme rewards comments on “repetition”.

Repetition: using the same word or phrase again and again for effect.
A mind in chains

“mind-forged manacles” is the poem's most famous image. Do people accept their misery because they have been taught to? AQA's 2021 report noted students “offering different interpretations of what Blake might have been saying”.

Metaphor: describing one thing as if it were another.
Blaming the powerful

Blake names the institutions: the “church” and the “palace”. The suffering of the weak (a child sweep, a soldier) is pinned on them, as dirt and blood on their walls.

Symbolism: when an object stands for a bigger idea (the palace stands for the king and the state).
A shocking final image

“marriage hearse” joins a wedding and a funeral. Cash says this “powerful oxymoron ‘marriage hearse’ suggests that nobody is immune” Cash, English Association one source. Even new life and love are poisoned.

Oxymoron: two opposite ideas placed side by side.
Examiners warn: in 2023, answers that discussed the “responsibilities / abuse of power” did much better than those “who stayed within the particulars of the poems in ‘My Last Duchess’, ‘Ozymandias’ or ‘London’”. Don't just retell the street scenes: say what Blake is arguing about power.

Who Blake was

  • Born in 1757 above his father's hosiery (stocking) shop in Soho, London. He died in London too, though he lived by the sea at Felpham, Sussex, from 1800 to 1803. Britannica; poets.org place

  • “William Blake composed his poem ‘London’ in the summer of 1792.” His handwritten draft survives in his notebook, now in the British Library. British Library one source

By blaming the “church” and “palace” for the suffering of child sweeps and soldiers, Blake argues that those in power have abused it, and by calling the chains “mind-forged” he challenges ordinary people to stop accepting it.

The story in short

A rich Italian Duke shows a visitor a painting of his dead wife. Bit by bit he hints that he had her killed because she smiled at everyone, not just him.

The message

Power without limits turns into cruelty. The Duke treats women as possessions, and he is about to marry again.

Why it was written

Browning was a Victorian poet famous for the dramatic monologue: he lets a powerful man condemn himself in his own words. Britannica; Baylor University

The title

“last” means most recent, and it hints there will be another: as one editor notes, the Duke “is now negotiating for his next Duchess.” The subtitle “Ferrara” names a real city in northern Italy, ruled by dukes in the Renaissance. editor's note: one source (RPO (University of Toronto); Victorian Web)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe whole poem is the Duke talking. His listener is an envoy (a messenger) sent by a Count to arrange the Duke’s next marriage. The listener never speaks.
  • Lines 1–4The Duke points to a painting of his “last Duchess”. She looks “as if she were alive”, so we know she is dead.
  • Lines 5–13He asks the visitor to sit and look. Only he opens the curtain over the painting. Visitors always wonder about her expression.
  • Lines 14–21He says her blush (that “spot / Of joy”) did not come only from him. Perhaps even the painter’s compliments made her blush, he suggests.
  • Lines 22–34She was “Too easily impressed”: sunsets, cherries and her mule pleased her as much as his “nine-hundred-years-old name”. This insults his pride.
  • Lines 35–43He would not lower himself to tell her what annoyed him: “I choose / Never to stoop.”
  • Lines 44–47She smiled at everyone. “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” He had her silenced: probably killed, though Browning once added that she might have been shut in a convent. Then he calmly asks the visitor to stand.
  • Lines 48–56Business: he expects a large dowry from the Count, though the daughter is his “object”. Walking downstairs, he shows off a bronze statue of Neptune “Taming a sea-horse”.
The turning point (lines 45–46): “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” Six short words hide a murder, and he carries on talking as if nothing happened. How it ends: On another possession, a statue made “for me!” He moves from his dead wife to his next bride to his art collection without a pause. The tone: Polite, proud and chillingly calm. He is not grieving; he is showing off. The politeness makes the cruelty worse.

The form: what a dramatic monologue is, and what Browning does with it
About the form
  • A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by one character who is not the poet, “in a specific situation at a critical moment.” Baylor University (M. H. Abrams)
  • Someone is listening, but “we know of the auditors’ presence, and what they say or do, only from clues in the discourse of the single speaker.” (Auditors = listeners; discourse = speech.) Baylor University (M. H. Abrams)
  • Why poets use it: to reveal the speaker’s character, often more than the speaker means to. So we have to read between the lines. Baylor University (M. H. Abrams)
  • The form had its “heyday in the Victorian Period”, and Browning is “considered to be the perfecter” of it. Baylor University; Britannica
  • A heroic couplet is a pair of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter (about ten syllables, da-DUM da-DUM). Britannica
What this poem does with it
  • One voice only: the Duke talks for all 56 lines. The Duchess is dead and the envoy never speaks. We hear only his side. our reading
  • We learn the listener is there only through the Duke’s words: “Will’t please you sit and look at her?” and “Will’t please you rise?” He decides when the visitor sits and stands, just as he controlled his wife. our reading
  • Rhyming couplets, 56 lines in one unbroken block. Britannica; our count The lines are iambic pentameter, so these are heroic couplets. EBSCO; Britannica pentameter: one source + our count
  • But enjambment (a sentence running on past the end of a line) hides the rhymes, so it sounds like casual chat. EBSCO Example: “I call / That piece a wonder”.
  • Why it matters: under the relaxed, chatty surface is a tight, controlled rhyme pattern, like the Duke: charming on top, controlling underneath. our reading
  • Openings and endings (AQA’s 2023 report suggests writing about these rather than rhyme labels): it opens on a dead wife turned into a painting and ends on a statue of “Taming”, made “for me!” Both are possessions. AQA praised essays on “the curtain”, Neptune and the dramatic monologue “to depict megalomania” (a hunger for power). AQA 2023 examiners' report
A sentence you could use: Browning uses a dramatic monologue so that the Duke condemns himself in his own words; the silent envoy, like the dead Duchess, is never allowed to speak, so the form itself shows how completely the Duke controls everyone around him.
Letting him give himself away

The Duke thinks he is showing off his art and his family name. Instead he reveals his jealousy and cruelty. We judge him by what he lets slip.

Dramatic irony: when the audience understands more than the speaker does.

He never says he killed her. “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” The calm, short phrasing is more frightening than a confession.

Euphemism: a mild phrase used instead of a harsh or shocking one.

Symbol: an object that stands for a bigger idea.
Cracks in the polish

Caesura: a pause in the middle of a line, often marked by punctuation.

The story in short

The speaker looks at thin paper that light shines through: old family records, maps, shop receipts. She imagines building with paper instead of brick, and ends with human skin.

The message

The things people fight over, like borders, buildings and power, are less solid than they look. Human life is fragile but it is what really matters.

Why it was written

Dharker was born in Pakistan and grew up in Glasgow, and calls herself a “cultural mongrel”. The poem comes from a 2006 book that her publisher says shows “a world stricken by fundamentalism” (extreme, rigid religious belief). Poetry Archive; Big Issue North; Bloodaxe

The title

“Tissue” has two main meanings: very thin, soft paper, and the cells that make up a living body. The whole poem slides between the two, until the paper becomes “your skin”. A tissue can also mean a web or network, as in “a tissue of lies”, which fits a poem about how lives are woven together. (Merriam-Webster; our reading of the poem)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThere is no story and no named place. A first-person speaker (“I”) thinks aloud about thin paper, and lets one idea lead to the next. The poem never says who the speaker is; these notes say “she” for short.
  • Lines 1–4The speaker starts with paper so thin that light shines through it, and claims this kind of paper “could alter things”. It has been worn thin by age and by people touching it.
  • Lines 5–12Her example: the back pages of an old family Koran, where someone has handwritten the family's history. Births, heights, weights, deaths and dates are all recorded. The pages have been handled so lovingly that they have gone see-through.
  • Lines 13–16She imagines buildings made of paper. Then she would feel them move, and see how easily they could fall, with just a sigh or a change in the wind.
  • Lines 17–20Maps are like this too. Hold one up and the sun shines through its borders, and the lines of rivers, roads, railways and mountains.
  • Lines 21–24Even little shop receipts, recording what we bought and paid by card, might lift our lives up like kites.
  • Lines 25–32An architect could build with all this paper, layering writing, numbers and lines. They would give up brick and block, and let daylight break through capital cities, giant stones and “the shapes that pride can make”.
  • Lines 33–37The new design would be made of living tissue: a structure never meant to last, paper smoothed and thinned until it is see-through. In the last line it becomes “your skin”.
The turning points: at line 25 (“An architect could use all this,”) she stops looking at paper and starts imagining a new way to build. Then the final line turns the paper into human skin. How it ends: On one short line, set apart: “turned into your skin.” The poem speaks straight to the reader, so the fragile thing is now you. The tone: Thoughtful, gentle and hopeful. The speaker is exploring an idea, not lecturing. The words “If”, “might” and “could” keep it a daydream rather than an argument.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: free verse, held in loose four-line stanzas
About the form
  • Free verse is “poetry organized to the cadences of speech and image patterns rather than according to a regular metrical scheme.” In plain English: no fixed rhythm and no rhyme pattern. Britannica; poets.org
  • Poets use it to sound like natural speech or thought, and to follow ideas and images wherever they lead. Britannica; poets.org
  • Enjambment is “the continuation of the sense of a phrase beyond the end of a line of verse.” Britannica
What this poem does with it
  • Shape: nine four-line stanzas (quatrains), then one last line on its own: 37 lines. No regular metre and no rhyme scheme, though a few words chime (“break”, “make”). our count
  • Loose, not rigid: the stanzas look neat, but sentences run on across lines and even across stanza gaps (lines 4–5, 8–9, 28–29, 32–33). The structure is there, but light gets through the gaps, like the paper she describes. our reading
  • One long sentence: lines 25–37 are a single sentence that keeps building, layer over layer, like the architect's paper. our count
  • The last line stands alone: after all that building, “turned into your skin.” is set apart, so the reader stops and feels the point land on them.
A sentence you could use: Dharker writes in free verse, but keeps it in loose four-line stanzas that run into each other; the form is a structure with gaps in it, just like the paper “that lets the light / shine through”, so the shape of the poem matches its message that rigid structures should give way.

Soft words (smoothed, stroked, thinned, sigh, kites) are set against hard ones (brick, block, monoliths). The poem seems to prefer the fragile side, which turns the usual idea of strength upside down.

Juxtaposition: placing two contrasting things side by side to show the difference.

Nanny’s section is a chain of images. She could see far ahead, she had a “mountain dream”, she fought with fire, and her hope flowed like a stream “to freedom river”. Nature imagery makes her almost a force of nature.
Lines 32–35Shaka de great Zulu … what happen to de Caribs and de Arawaks too
British war heroes (Nelson, Waterloo) are set against Shaka, the Zulu leader. Then Columbus and 1492, set against the peoples who already lived in the Caribbean. The question “what happen to” hints at something terrible that was hidden.
Lines 36–39Florence Nightingale and she lamp … Mary Seacole
Florence Nightingale, the famous British nurse, then Robin Hood and Old King Cole. The last name is Mary Seacole, the Jamaican nurse he was never taught about.
Lines 40–49even when de British said no … a yellow sunrise
The longest hero section. Seacole travelled from Jamaica to the Crimean War. The British said no, but she went anyway and faced the Russian snow. She was “a healing star” to the wounded and “a yellow sunrise” to the dying: images of light and hope.
Lines 50–53I checking out me own history … I carving out me identity
The chant returns, then the change: “But now”. The speaker stops being told and starts finding out. “Carving” suggests hard work, making something lasting, like carving in stone or wood.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: dialect, two voices and a refrain
About the form
  • Dialect is the way a language is spoken in one place or community, with its own words, spellings and grammar. Agard is known for using Caribbean dialect and Creole in his poems. Poetry Archive; Books for Keeps
  • Creole here means the language of the Caribbean that mixes English with other influences. Agard said his love of “the language, Creole, and the rich culture of Guyana” grew after he moved away. Books for Keeps, 1993 one source
  • A refrain is a line or phrase that keeps coming back, like the chorus of a song.
  • The Children’s Poetry Archive lists the poem’s key devices as “Dialect, Half Rhyme, Pace”. Children’s Poetry Archive one source
What this poem does with it
  • Phonetic spelling: words are spelled as they sound: “dem”, “dat”, “de”, “bout”. Verbs lose their endings (“she travel”, “I checking”). The poem is written in the speaker’s own voice, not the standard English of the schoolbooks. our reading
  • No punctuation: there are no full stops or commas in the whole poem. It reads like speech or performance, and it ignores the rules of standard written English, just as it ignores the official history. our check and reading
  • Two kinds of section: the “Dem tell me” stanzas have long lines and rhyme like nursery rhymes (balloon / moon / spoon / maroon). The hero sections are indented, in very short lines, and in italics in the anthology. The layout separates the two histories. our check
  • The rhyme links the two histories: in most “Dem tell me” stanzas a hero is rhymed with the British list: “Waterloo” with “Zulu”, “soul” with “Seacole”. The hero is placed where the rhyme lands, so we hear them clearly. our reading
  • The refrain: “Dem tell me” opens the poem and returns at the end. It sounds like a chant or rote learning. At the end it is answered by “But now I”, showing that he has broken the pattern. our reading
A sentence you could use: Agard writes in Caribbean dialect with no punctuation at all, so the poem itself refuses the standard English of the colonial classroom; by setting the heroes apart in short, indented sections, he gives them the space that his history lessons never did.
Repetition: “Dem tell me”

The phrase comes back again and again, like rote learning in a classroom. “Dem” is never named, which makes the power faceless and everywhere.

Refrain: a line or phrase repeated through a poem, like a chorus.
Mockery by juxtaposition

Real British heroes sit next to nursery rhymes: “Dem tell me bout 1066 and all dat” then Dick Whittington’s cat. This suggests the history he was taught was as useless to him as a children’s rhyme.

Juxtaposition: placing two things side by side so we compare them.
Light and nature imagery for the heroes

Toussaint is a “beacon”, Seacole “a healing star” and “a yellow sunrise”, Nanny a “hopeful stream”. Light answers the blindness of line 5.

Imagery: descriptive language that creates a picture in the reader’s mind.
Passive to active

For most of the poem things are done to the speaker (“Dem tell me”). In the last two lines he does things himself: “I checking out”, “I carving out”. He takes back control.

Volta: a turning point where a poem changes direction.
Examiners warn: “a mere observation of the techniques or even a vague comment about ‘flow’ and ‘pace’ will not achieve above Level 2 AO2 if it is not tied to meaning.” (AQA June 2022 report). So don’t just say “there’s no punctuation so it flows”; say what that choice shows about power and identity.

The story in short

An islander, speaking for the whole community (“we”), explains how they prepare for storms. Then a storm hits, and they can only sit and wait.

The message

However well people prepare, nature is stronger. The most frightening enemy is one you cannot see or fight.

Why it was written

Heaney grew up on a farm in Northern Ireland. In his early poems, nature is “violent as well as bounteous” (generous). Heaney estate; The Conversation (Oxford Brookes University)

The title

The title is plain: a storm, and an island. But it says “the Island”, not a real place name. The poem never tells us which island, so it could be any exposed place, cut off from help. Some readers link it to the Aran Islands off the west of Ireland, but only a revision guide says so, and we could not confirm it. Aran link: unconfirmed (AQA anthology (our check of the text))

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupOne islander speaks for everyone (“we”). The speaker talks to a listener (“you”) in a calm, chatty way, explaining how island life works.
  • Lines 1–2They are ready. They build low, solid (“squat”) houses, sink the walls into rock and use strong slate roofs.
  • Lines 3–5The land is poor and dried up (“wizened”). It grows no hay, so there are no haystacks or bundles of crops to lose.
  • Lines 5–11There are no trees either. Trees might seem like company in a gale, because their noise lets you hear what you fear. But that noise would distract you while the storm “pummels” your house. In any case, there is “no natural shelter”.
  • Lines 12–16You might think the sea is friendly, crashing “comfortably” on the cliffs. “But no”: in a storm the spray hits the windows and “spits” like a pet cat that has suddenly turned wild.
  • Lines 16–18The people just “sit tight”. The wind dives and attacks like a fighter plane. The empty air itself feels like gunfire, and they are “bombarded”.
  • Line 19The speaker finds it strange: what they fear is “a huge nothing”, the invisible air.
The turning point (line 14): “But no:” The calm explaining stops. The sea, which seemed friendly, attacks, and the language becomes violent and military. How it ends: on a paradox. A “huge nothing” sounds impossible, but it is exactly right: wind is invisible, yet it can wreck a house. The tone: starts confident and practical, like someone giving a tour. By the end it is uneasy and afraid. The certainty of line 1 has gone.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: blank verse, and a voice that sounds like talking
About the form
  • Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter: lines of about ten syllables in a da-DUM, da-DUM rhythm, with no rhyme. Britannica calls it “the preeminent dramatic and narrative verse form in English”. Britannica; poets.org
  • Because it does not rhyme, it can sound close to real speech. Britannica describes Shakespeare's blank verse as “approximating the rhythms of speech”, and notes its range reaches a “low-keyed, conversational tone”. Britannica
  • A dramatic monologue is “a poem written in the form of a speech of an individual character”. The reader works out the speaker's situation from what they say. Britannica
  • Wordsworth's Prelude is also in blank verse, which makes it a good partner for comparing form. Britannica
What this poem does with it
  • Blank verse: 19 unrhymed lines, most with ten syllables. Line 1 is a regular da-DUM line: “We are prepared: we build our houses squat,”. The steady rhythm sounds controlled, like the islanders' preparations. our check
  • One single stanza: there are no breaks, no gaps, nowhere to pause. Like the island, the poem has “no natural shelter.” our reading
  • The control breaks down: lines run on (enjambment) and are cut by sudden stops (caesura). “Turned savage.” starts a line and ends a sentence at once, so the shock lands hard. our reading
  • A talking voice: the speaker chats to a listener: “as you see”, “you know what I mean”, “You might think”. It reads like a dramatic monologue, but the speaker is a whole community (“we”), not one named character. our reading
  • Why it matters: the chatty, ordinary voice makes the storm feel like a normal part of life. That makes the fear at the end more unsettling: even people used to storms are afraid.
A sentence you could use: Heaney writes in blank verse, whose steady, controlled rhythm mirrors the islanders' confident preparations; but as the storm builds, enjambment and sudden mid-line stops (caesuras) break that control, so the form itself shows human order giving way to nature.
War imagery

The storm is described like a military attack: “Exploding”, “strafes”, “salvo”, “bombarded”. Nature becomes an enemy army, and the islanders are civilians under fire. AQA mark schemes name the “imagery of war in ‘Storm on the Island’”.

Semantic field: a group of words linked to the same topic (here, war) spread through a text.
Something tame turns savage

You “might think that the sea is company”, but in a storm it “spits like a tame cat / Turned savage.” The comparison to a pet shows that people only thought nature was under control.

Simile: comparing one thing to another using “like” or “as”.
Talking to the reader

Phrases like “you know what I mean” and “You might think” pull us into the conversation. The collective “We” shows a whole community facing the storm together.

Direct address: when a poem speaks straight to “you”, the listener or reader.
A paradox to finish

The poem ends on “a huge nothing that we fear.” Air is “nothing”, yet it is huge and terrifying. The enemy cannot be seen, fought or prepared for, which is why the confidence of line 1 collapses.

Paradox: a statement that seems to contradict itself but contains a truth.

In 2022 (conflict) it called Storm “arguably a little more challenging in terms of focus and comparison”. If you choose Storm, make the link to the question obvious: nature's power against human power, or conflict with nature.

The story in short

A boy takes a boat that isn't his and rows out on a lake at night. A huge black mountain peak seems to rise up and chase him, so he rows back, scared. For days afterwards his mind is haunted.

The message

Nature is far more powerful than a proud human. It can teach us through fear as well as through beauty, and that lesson stays in the mind for a long time.

Why it was written

Wordsworth was a Romantic poet who believed nature shaped who he became. He said he grew up “Foster'd alike by beauty and by fear”. Britannica

The title

A “prelude” is an introduction. Wordsworth meant this long poem to introduce an even bigger one, The Recluse, which he never finished. 1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupAn adult poet remembers one evening from his boyhood in the Lake District. Everything is in the past tense and told in his own voice, “I”.
  • Lines 1–7One summer evening he finds a little boat tied to a tree. He unties it and rows out without asking. He knows it is wrong: it is “an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure”.
  • Lines 8–16The oars leave circles of moonlight on the water. Feeling “Proud of his skill”, he fixes his eyes on the top of a rocky ridge and rows in a straight line towards it. Above it there is only sky and stars.
  • Lines 17–20He feels magical and confident. The boat is “an elfin pinnace” (a small fairy boat) gliding “like a swan”.
  • Lines 21–29Suddenly, from behind the ridge, “a huge peak, black and huge” appears. As he rows on, it seems to grow, to rise up like a living creature, and to stride after him.
  • Lines 29–34Terrified, he turns round “With trembling oars”, creeps back, ties up the boat and walks home through the fields in a “grave / And serious mood”.
  • Lines 35–44For many days his mind is troubled. He can no longer picture pleasant things like trees, sea and “green fields”. Instead “huge and mighty forms” move through his thoughts by day and trouble his dreams at night.
The turning point (lines 21–24): “When, from behind that craggy steep…” The peak “Upreared its head”, and the proud, happy mood flips into fear. How it ends: Not on the lake, but inside his mind. The mountain is gone, yet “huge and mighty forms” are still “a trouble to my dreams”. The tone: Starts calm, magical and proud. Becomes tense and frightening. Ends serious and unsettled, as the adult looks back on how the experience changed him.

2The poem, with what it means

The poem
What it means, in plain English
One summer evening (led by her) I found
A little boat tied to a willow tree
Within a rocky cove, its usual home.
Straight I unloosed her chain, and stepping in
Pushed from the shore. It was an act of stealth
And troubled pleasure, nor without the voice
Of mountain-echoes did my boat move on;
“her” in line 1 is Nature: the lines just before this extract talk about “Nature” and “her aim”. So Nature leads him to the boat. He takes it without asking (“stealth” = sneaking). He enjoys it, but feels guilty: “troubled pleasure”. Even the mountains seem to echo back at him.
Leaving behind her still, on either side,
Small circles glittering idly in the moon,
Until they melted all into one track
Of sparkling light. But now, like one who rows,
Proud of his skill, to reach a chosen point
With an unswerving line, I fixed my view
Upon the summit of a craggy ridge,
The horizon’s utmost boundary; far above
Was nothing but the stars and the grey sky.
Here “her” is the boat. The oars leave ripples that shine in the moonlight. He is proud of his rowing and fixes his eyes on the top of a ridge, which seems to be the edge of the world (“utmost boundary”). He thinks he is in control.
She was an elfin pinnace; lustily
I dipped my oars into the silent lake,
And, as I rose upon the stroke, my boat
Went heaving through the water like a swan;
A pinnace is a small boat; elfin means fairy-like. “lustily” = with lots of energy. The boat glides gracefully, like a swan. This is the high point of his confidence.
When, from behind that craggy steep till then
The horizon’s bound, a huge peak, black and huge,
As if with voluntary power instinct,
Upreared its head. I struck and struck again,
And growing still in stature the grim shape
Towered up between me and the stars, and still,
For so it seemed, with purpose of its own
And measured motion like a living thing,
Strode after me. With trembling oars I turned,
As he rows away from the shore, a bigger mountain behind the ridge comes into view. To him it looks alive: “voluntary power instinct” = filled with its own will and power. The harder he rows (“struck” the water), the taller it seems to grow, blocking out the stars. It seems to stride after him like a giant. “For so it seemed” admits it was his imagination.
And through the silent water stole my way
Back to the covert of the willow tree;
There in her mooring-place I left my bark, –
And through the meadows homeward went, in grave
And serious mood; but after I had seen
He creeps back (“stole my way”: a pun, since he stole the boat too), returns the boat (“bark” = boat) to its hiding place (“covert”) and walks home, shaken and serious.
That spectacle, for many days, my brain
Worked with a dim and undetermined sense
Of unknown modes of being; o’er my thoughts
There hung a darkness, call it solitude
Or blank desertion. No familiar shapes
Remained, no pleasant images of trees,
Of sea or sky, no colours of green fields;
But huge and mighty forms, that do not live
Like living men, moved slowly through the mind
By day, and were a trouble to my dreams.
For days he can't stop thinking about it. He senses “unknown modes of being”: kinds of existence or power he doesn't understand. His mind feels dark, lonely and empty. His happy pictures of nature are replaced by giant, mysterious shapes that haunt him day and night.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: what blank verse is, and what Wordsworth does with it
About the form
  • Blank verse is “unrhymed iambic pentameter”: about ten syllables a line in a da-DUM, da-DUM rhythm, with no rhyme. Britannica; poets.org
  • In skilled hands it can sound close to real talk: Britannica says Shakespeare's blank verse is “approximating the rhythms of speech”. Britannica
  • It is the form of great serious poems, such as Milton's epic Paradise Lost. poets.org; Britannica
  • The Prelude is an autobiographical epic: an epic is usually a “long narrative poem recounting heroic deeds”, but here the hero is the poet himself, and the story is how his mind grew. Britannica epic definition: one source
What this poem does with it
  • First person, past tense: the adult Wordsworth looks back on a childhood memory, so we get the boy's feelings and the man's reflection.
  • No rhyme, no stanzas: one unbroken block of 44 lines. It flows like a remembered story told aloud. our count
  • Enjambment (sentences running over line ends) speeds the panic: “I struck and struck again, / And growing still in stature the grim shape / Towered up…” The mountain seems to grow across the lines. our reading
  • Sudden stops: “Upreared its head.” and “Strode after me.” each finish a long sentence. They sit at the start of a line and stop dead at a full stop (a caesura, a pause inside a line), like sudden movements. our reading
  • Structure: calm → proud → terrified → haunted. The poem doesn't end with the escape; it ends inside his mind, which is where the real effect lies.
A sentence you could use: Wordsworth uses blank verse, the flexible, speech-like form of serious epic poetry, to tell a small childhood memory, which shows how important this moment was in the growth of his mind; the run-on lines make the mountain seem to grow before our eyes.
The mountain comes alive

The peak “Upreared its head” and “Strode after me”, “with purpose of its own”. Nature becomes a giant that seems to punish the boy. But “For so it seemed” reminds us it is his guilty imagination at work.

Personification: giving human actions or feelings to something that isn't human.
Light turns to dark

At first everything shines: “glittering”, “sparkling light”, the moon. After the peak appears: “black”, “grim shape”, “There hung a darkness”. The light and colour drain out of his world, just as his confidence does.

Contrast: putting two opposite things close together so each stands out more.
Repetition that grows

“a huge peak, black and huge”, “I struck and struck again”, “and still”. The repeated words make the mountain seem to keep growing and the boy keep panicking, as if he is stuck in a nightmare.

Repetition: using the same word or phrase more than once for effect.
An ending inside the mind

The last ten lines are not about the lake at all. Pleasant images such as “green fields” are replaced by “huge and mighty forms”. Nature's power doesn't just scare him once; it changes the way he thinks.

Turning point (shift): the moment a poem changes direction or mood (here, lines 21–24).
Examiners warn: “If you want to talk about structure, perhaps consider beginnings and endings of the text rather than trying to talk about particular structural features such enjambment / caesura / rhyme schemes if you have nothing useful to say about them.” (AQA 2023 report). So only mention enjambment if you say what it does.

Northward, incessantly, the flickering gunnery rumbles,
Far off, like a dull rumour of some other war.
   What are we doing here?
The barbed wire shakes in the wind like dying men caught on it. Real fighting is happening somewhere else, far away. The short last line asks what the point of it all is.
The poignant misery of dawn begins to grow ...
We only know war lasts, rain soaks, and clouds sag stormy.
Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army
Attacks once more in ranks on shivering ranks of grey,
   But nothing happens.
Dawn should bring hope. Instead it is an enemy army of grey clouds attacking the grey, shivering soldiers. The list of short clauses (war lasts, rain soaks…) sounds flat and hopeless.
Sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence.
Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow,
With sidelong flowing flakes that flock, pause, and renew,
We watch them wandering up and down the wind's nonchalance,
   But nothing happens.
Bullets fly, but the snowy air is more deadly than they are. The hissing “s” sounds copy the bullets and the wind. “nonchalance” = not caring: the wind doesn't care about them at all.
Pale flakes with fingering stealth come feeling for our faces -
We cringe in holes, back on forgotten dreams, and stare, snow-dazed,
Deep into grassier ditches. So we drowse, sun-dozed,
Littered with blossoms trickling where the blackbird fusses.
   – Is it that we are dying?
The snow creeps up like a thief's fingers. Numb with cold, the men slip into a dream of summer. Drowsiness in the cold is a sign of freezing to death, which is why they ask if they are dying.
Slowly our ghosts drag home: glimpsing the sunk fires, glozed
With crusted dark-red jewels; crickets jingle there;
For hours the innocent mice rejoice: the house is theirs;
Shutters and doors, all closed: on us the doors are closed, -
   We turn back to our dying.
Their minds go home, but only as “ghosts”. The fire is low: its embers glow like jewels but give no warmth. Mice own the house now. The doors are shut on them: there is no place for them at home, so they go back to die.
Since we believe not otherwise can kind fires burn;
Now ever suns smile true on child, or field, or fruit.
For God's invincible spring our love is made afraid;
Therefore, not loath, we lie out here; therefore were born,
   For love of God seems dying.
The hardest stanza. They tell themselves they must stay out here so that the fires at home keep burning and children and fields stay safe. “not loath” = not unwilling. But the last line admits their faith is fading.
Tonight, His frost will fasten on this mud and us,
Shrivelling many hands. puckering foreheads crisp.
The burying-party, picks and shovels in their shaking grasp,
Pause over half-known faces. All their eyes are ice,
   But nothing happens.
A prediction: tonight God's frost will freeze them. Soldiers sent to bury the dead will stop over faces they half-recognise, frozen solid. (The full stop after “hands” is how the anthology prints it; other texts have a comma. Don't quote that line.)

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: half-rhyme, a refrain and a circle
About the form
  • Half-rhyme (also called slant rhyme) is when two words nearly rhyme but not quite: “two words that have only their final consonant sounds” in common. Britannica
  • Pararhyme is Owen's own version: he keeps the consonants the same and changes the vowel, as in “hall” and “Hell”, or “mystery” and “mastery”. Yale Modernism Lab
  • Why poets use it: a full rhyme sounds neat and finished. A half-rhyme sounds slightly wrong, so the ear is left unsatisfied. That suits a poem about unease. our reading
  • Britannica notes Owen's “technical experiments in assonance” (repeated vowel sounds), which were later “particularly influential in the 1930s”. Britannica
  • A refrain is a line repeated at the end of stanzas, like a chorus in a song.
What this poem does with it
  • 40 lines in 8 stanzas of five lines. EBSCO; our count Four long lines are followed by a short fifth line that hangs on its own.
  • The first four lines of each stanza half-rhyme in an ABBA pattern: “knive us / silent / salient / nervous”, “wire / brambles / rumbles / war”. our count
  • The Wilfred Owen Association praises the “daring half-rhymes - ‘knive us/nervous’, ‘nonchalance/happens’” and “the short, simple, hanging line at the end of each verse.” Wilfred Owen Association
  • Refrain: “But nothing happens.” ends stanzas 1, 3, 4 and 8. The other short lines move closer and closer to death: “Is it that we are dying?”, “We turn back to our dying.”, “For love of God seems dying.” our count
  • Circular structure: “When it ends, they are exactly where they were in the first verse.” Wilfred Owen Association The poem goes round in a loop, like the soldiers' endless nights.
  • Why it matters: the rhymes never quite click and the refrain never changes, so the form itself feels stuck and uneasy, just like the men. our reading
A sentence you could use: Owen's pararhymes, like “knive us” and “nervous”, never quite chime, so the reader is left as tense and unsatisfied as the soldiers; and because the poem ends with the same line, “But nothing happens.”, its circular structure traps them in endless, pointless waiting.
Nature as the enemy

The wind has knives (“winds that knive us”), dawn is a “melancholy army” and the frost “will fasten” on them. Owen gives the weather the actions of soldiers, so the real battle is against nature.

Personification: giving human actions or feelings to something that isn't human.
Hissing sounds

“Sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence.” The repeated “s” sounds copy the hiss of bullets and wind. AQA's 2025 mark scheme suggests writing about “effects of sibilance”.

Sibilance: repeating “s” and “sh” sounds close together.
“We”, not “I”

Owen speaks for all the men (“we keep awake”, “our ghosts”), in the present tense. Their suffering is shared, and we live through the night with them.

Collective voice: a speaker who talks for a whole group, using “we” and “our”.
Warm home, frozen trench

The dream of “crusted dark-red jewels” and “innocent mice” is warm and gentle, set against the ice and mud. The contrast makes the trench feel even crueller.

Juxtaposition: putting two very different things side by side to show the difference.

Who Owen was

  • Born in 1893 in Oswestry, Shropshire. He worked for a year as a vicar's assistant, then taught English in Bordeaux, France. Britannica; University of Oxford
  • He loved the Romantic poet Keats: his early poems were “consciously modeled” on Keats's work. Britannica; Poetry Archive nature

  • Only four of his poems were published while he was alive. Most of his fame came after his death. Wilfred Owen Association; Poetry Archive

The winter behind the poem

  • The poem is “based on Owen's experiences in the winter of 1917”, giving “a worm's-eye view of the front line”. Wilfred Owen Association conflict · difficult experiences
  • In January 1917 his battalion arrived near Beaumont Hamel on the Somme. The winter of 1916–17 was extremely cold. Wilfred Owen Association nature one source
  • With no dugouts, the men had to “lie out in the snow in shell holes and in a bitterly piercing wind”. More were sent to hospital with frostbite than with wounds. Wilfred Owen Association nature · effects of conflict one source
  • Owen wrote that “one of my party actually froze to death” and “we were marooned on a frozen desert”. Owen's own words, quoted by the Wilfred Owen Association nature · conflict
  • Another letter: “My feet ached until they could ache no more, and so they temporarily died.” London Review of Books (review of Owen's letters) effects of conflict one source

Shell shock, Sassoon and “the pity of War”

  • In spring 1917 he was blown into the air by a shell and sent home with shell shock. At Craiglockhart hospital, Edinburgh, in summer 1917, he met the poet Siegfried Sassoon. Poetry Archive; University of Oxford; Britannica effects of conflict April or May: sources differ
  • Sassoon encouraged him “to bring his war experiences into his poetry”. Wilfred Owen Association
  • In his Preface (1918) for a book of poems he planned, Owen wrote: “My subject is War, and the pity of War. The Poetry is in the pity.” Wilfred Owen Association; Durham University (manuscript) conflict · effects of conflict
  • The printed Preface (1920) also says “This book is not about heroes” and “the true Poets must be truthful.” Exposure tells the truth about suffering, not glory. Owen, Poems (1920), Project Gutenberg patriotism and duty link to Exposure: our reading
  • The poet W. B. Yeats disliked Owen's work: “Passive suffering is not a theme for poetry”. Exposure is entirely about passive suffering. Wilfred Owen Association power · powerlessness one source

How the poem was made

  • Sources give different dates. The Owen Association says he began it at Abbeville in early 1917; the Poetry Archive says “written in February 1918”; Merryn Williams says “The final version of this poem belongs to September 1918”. Wilfred Owen Association; Poetry Archive sources conflict
  • The safe version: the poem comes from the winter of 1917, and Owen reworked it until September 1918, weeks before he was killed. Wilfred Owen Association
  • “Our brains ache” echoes “My heart aches”, the opening of Keats's “Ode to a Nightingale”. Owen felt his generation faced “horrors which the Romantics had not dreamed of”. Wilfred Owen Association nature one source

Putting it into an answer (AO3): Owen was an officer who lay out in the snow on the Somme in January 1917, when one of his own men froze to death. He wrote that “The Poetry is in the pity”, and Exposure shows that pity: instead of heroic battles, it shows ordinary soldiers powerless against the cold, dying while “nothing happens”.

7In the exam

Compare how poets present ideas about conflict in ‘Exposure’ and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’. (AQA, June 2025)
A strong opening (thesis): Both Owen and Armitage show that conflict destroys soldiers' minds as well as their bodies, but Owen's men suffer from endless waiting, while Armitage's soldier suffers from what he has done.
A model comparison paragraph:
Both poets present conflict as something soldiers cannot escape. Owen's men are worn down by waiting: the refrain “But nothing happens.” returns four times, so the reader shares their numb, endless watch. Their enemy is the weather, and the poem ends with a burial party over “half-known faces” whose “eyes are ice”. Armitage's soldier, by contrast, does act: “all three of us open fire.” Yet his conflict never ends either, because the dead looter is “here in my head when I close my eyes”. Owen shows suffering caused by doing nothing; Armitage shows guilt caused by doing something. Both suggest that ordinary soldiers are powerless victims of conflicts far larger than themselves.

  • Short quotations from both poems, with methods named (refrain) and explained

The story in short

A British soldier tells how he and two others shot a looter who may not have been armed. Back home, he can't stop seeing the dead man.

The message

War doesn't end when the fighting stops. A few seconds of violence can haunt a soldier for the rest of his life.

Why it was written

Armitage wrote it for a TV film about ex-soldiers with PTSD. He turned a real soldier's account into a poem. simonarmitage.com; IDFA

The title

“Remains” has two meanings. Human remains are a dead body: the looter's body, “carted off in the back of a lorry.” And something that remains is something that stays. The body is taken away, but the memory and the guilt remain with the soldier. our reading (our reading)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupA soldier talks to us as if we were chatting. He jumps straight into the story with “On another occasion”, as if this was just one of many incidents. The whole poem is told in the present tense, so it feels like it's happening now.
  • Lines 1–4His patrol is sent to deal with men robbing a bank. One of them runs off up the road. He might have had a gun, or might not: “probably armed, possibly not.”
  • Lines 5–10The speaker and two other soldiers all fire at once. He says he sees every bullet go through the man, so clearly that he can see daylight through the wounds.
  • Lines 11–16The man has been hit about a dozen times and lies dying in agony. Another soldier casually “tosses his guts back into his body”. The body is thrown into a lorry and driven away.
  • Lines 17–19“End of story, except not really.” A bloodstain stays on the street, and he walks over it on patrol “week after week.”
  • Lines 20–24He goes home on leave, but the memory follows him. Whenever he blinks, sleeps or dreams, the shooting replays. Alcohol and drugs don't get rid of it.
  • Lines 25–30The dead man now lives inside his head, like an enemy soldier hiding behind the lines. He isn't buried far away in the desert. He is “here and now”, and the soldier feels the man's life is in his hands.
The turning point (line 17): “End of story, except not really.” The soldier tries to close the story with an everyday phrase, then admits it isn't over. The poem moves from the street in Iraq to the inside of his head. How it ends: On guilt that won't go away. The last line, “his bloody life in my bloody hands.”, puts the man's death firmly in the speaker's own hands. The tone: It starts casual and matter-of-fact, almost boastful. It ends haunted, angry and full of guilt. The calm voice cracks as the poem goes on.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: a dramatic monologue that sounds like real speech
About the form
  • A dramatic monologue is “a poem written in the form of a speech of an individual character”. Britannica
  • It packs a person's story into one vivid scene and shows us what is going on in their mind. Britannica
  • Often the most interesting thing is what the speaker gives away about himself without meaning to. Britannica says the subject “is usually far less interesting than what is inadvertently revealed about the speaker himself.” Britannica
  • Poets use it to let us hear a voice directly, with no narrator in between. So we judge the speaker for ourselves. our reading
What this poem does with it
  • The speaker is a soldier, talking straight to us. WJEC calls it “a dramatic monologue from the point of view of a soldier on active duty.” WJEC one source
  • It sounds like real speech: slang such as “legs it”, “my mates” and “letting fly”. That fits its origin: the poems in The Not Dead came from real soldiers’ own accounts (their “testimonies”). simonarmitage.com; WJEC
  • What he gives away: he starts off casual and sure of himself, but by the end he is falling apart. The voice shows the damage without him ever saying “I feel guilty”. our reading
  • Shape: seven 4-line stanzas, then a 2-line ending (30 lines). WJEC suggests the shape may suggest “the continuous suffering of the soldier.” our count; WJEC
  • No regular rhyme or rhythm, which WJEC says stresses “the seriousness of the situation”. Sentences run on over stanza breaks (“I swear” / “I see”; “But I blink” / “and he bursts”), like memories he can't hold back. WJEC; our reading
  • The short last stanza breaks the pattern of fours. The poem stops rather than resolves, because his story has no ending. our reading
A sentence you could use: Armitage writes Remains as a dramatic monologue in a soldier's own slang, so the casual voice of the opening (“legs it”) slowly gives way to guilt; the speaker reveals his trauma without ever naming it, and the poem's unresolved two-line ending mirrors a memory that never ends.
A casual voice for a horrific event

Slang like “legs it” and “sort of inside out” makes the killing sound ordinary. The gap between the casual words and the horror shows how soldiers cope by switching off, until the feelings break through.

Colloquial language: everyday, informal speech, the way people actually talk.
From “we” to “I”

The first half spreads the blame: “all three of us open fire.” The second half is all “I”, “my head”, “my bloody hands.” At home there's no one to share the blame with. WJEC describes a move from “collective responsibility” to “individual feeling of guilt”.

Pronouns: words like I, we, he that stand for people. A shift in pronouns can show a shift in feeling.
Repetition that acts like a flashback

“probably armed, possibly not.” comes back in line 22, and “Sleep, and” / “Dream, and” start lines 22 and 23. The poem keeps circling back, just as his memory does.

Repetition: using the same words more than once, to stress an idea or show it won't go away.
War imagery inside his head

The memory is “dug in behind enemy lines”, military language. The enemy is no longer out on the street; it's inside his mind. The fighting has followed him home.

Metaphor: describing one thing as if it were another.

The story in short

In 1854, about six hundred British cavalrymen are ordered to charge down a valley at Russian guns. The order is a mistake, but they obey. Many die; the poet tells us to honour them.

The message

The soldiers' courage and obedience deserve to be remembered for ever, even though their leaders got it badly wrong.

Why it was written

Tennyson was Poet Laureate. Britannica says the title is given “for poetic excellence”, and laureates from Tennyson on wrote “poems for royal and national occasions”. He wrote this poem “only a few minutes after reading an account in The Times”. Britannica; Poetry Archive

The title

A charge is a fast attack on horseback. The Light Brigade was a unit of light cavalry (soldiers on horseback): Britannica lists lancers, hussars and light dragoons. In the poem they fight with “sabres” (curved swords). The title names a real event, the charge at Balaklava on 25 October 1854, and names the soldiers as a group, not as individuals. (National Army Museum; Britannica)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupA narrator who was not there tells the story of the charge in the third person (“they”). The six numbered stanzas follow the charge from start to finish, then turn to the reader.
  • Lines 1–8 (stanza 1)The horsemen ride on and on: “Half a league onward”. An officer shouts the order to charge the guns. They ride “Into the valley of Death”.
  • Lines 9–17 (stanza 2)Was any soldier frightened? No, even though they knew “Some one had blunder’d”: someone had made a terrible mistake. A soldier's job is to obey, not to ask why, and to “do and die”.
  • Lines 18–26 (stanza 3)Cannons fire at them from the right, the left and the front. They still ride bravely on, into the “jaws of Death”, the “mouth of Hell”.
  • Lines 27–38 (stanza 4)The longest stanza: the fighting. They reach the guns, slash at the gunners with their swords and break through the Russian line. Then they ride back, but “Not the six hundred”: many are missing.
  • Lines 39–49 (stanza 5)Riding back, they are fired on from all sides again, now with “Cannon behind them”. Horses and men fall. Only “All that was left of them” return.
  • Lines 50–55 (stanza 6)The poet steps out of the story and speaks to us. Their glory will never fade. He orders the reader to “Honour the Light Brigade”.
The turning point (line 37): “Then they rode back, but not / Not the six hundred.” The repeated “not” is the first time the famous number shrinks. From here, the poem counts the cost. How it ends: On a command to the reader: “Honour the Light Brigade, / Noble six hundred!” The poem ends as a public memorial, not on the dead. The tone: Fast, loud and admiring, like a song of praise. But there is one quiet note of blame: “Some one had blunder’d”.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: a story in six numbered stanzas, in a galloping rhythm
About the form
  • A narrative poem tells a story. Here the story is told in the third person (“they”, “the six hundred”), by someone who was not there. AQA's mark schemes contrast this with the first person (“I”) in Remains. AQA mark schemes 2017 and 2024
  • A dactyl is a beat of one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed ones: DUM-da-da. Britannica calls dactylic rhythms “fairly rare in English verse”. Britannica
  • Tennyson himself said: “my poem is dactylic, and founded on the phrase, ‘Some one had blundered.’” Tennyson's note, University of Toronto (RPO)
  • Poets often drop the last unstressed syllable at the end of a dactylic line. Britannica one source
What this poem does with it
  • Hear it: say “Half a league, half a league” aloud, leaning on the first word of each half: HALF a league, HALF a league = DUM-da-da, DUM-da-da. Two strong beats a line, like a horse at full gallop. our scansion
  • Numbered stanzas of uneven length: 8, 9, 9, 12, 11 and 6 lines. The longest (stanza 4) is the fighting; the shortest (stanza 6) is the call to honour them. our count
  • Refrains that change: “Rode the six hundred” (stanzas 1–3) becomes “Not the six hundred” (stanza 4), then “Left of six hundred” (stanza 5), then “Noble six hundred!” The structure itself counts the dead.
  • Mirroring: stanza 5 repeats stanza 3, but “Cannon in front of them” becomes “Cannon behind them”, and “Into the jaws of Death” becomes “Came thro’ the jaws of Death”. Going in and coming out are both a trap.
  • Rhyme on one sound: “blunder’d”, “thunder’d”, “wonder’d”, “sunder’d” all echo “hundred”. The mistake, the noise and the dead are tied together by sound. our reading
A sentence you could use: Tennyson tells the story in the third person, so the soldiers are never individuals, only “the six hundred”; and the changing refrain (“Rode”, then “Not”, then “Left of six hundred”) turns the poem's structure into a count of the dead.
Refrain

“Rode the six hundred” returns again and again, but changes as men die. The repetition drives the poem forward; the changes show the cost.

Refrain: a line or phrase repeated through a poem, often at the end of each stanza.
Personified Death

“the valley of Death” and “the jaws of Death” make death a monster with a mouth. The soldiers ride into it knowingly, which makes them seem braver.

Personification: describing a thing or idea as if it were a person or creature.
Repetition and the trap

“Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them,” hammers the same word at the start of each line. You feel the guns firing from every side.

Anaphora: repeating the same word or words at the start of lines.
Commands to the reader

The last stanza switches to imperatives: “Honour the charge they made!” The poem stops telling a story and starts telling us what to feel.

Imperative: a verb that gives an order, like “Honour” or “Look”.
Examiners warn: “a mere observation of the techniques or even a vague comment about ‘flow’ and ‘pace’ will not achieve above Level 2 AO2 if it is not tied to meaning” (AQA 2022 report). So don't just say that the rhythm sounds like galloping: say what that does to the meaning (the men are carried forward, unable to stop or question).

Then the hare breaks the spell and the charge starts again. How it ends: not with a victory or a death, but with the soldier still charging, driven only by “His terror’s touchy dynamite.” We never find out what happens to him. The tone: urgent, confused and frightened. There is no glory and no cheering. The only patriotic feeling in the poem is a tear that turns to sweat.

The form: a tightly ordered poem about chaos
About the form
  • A stanza is a group of lines, like a paragraph in a poem. A rhyming couplet is two lines in a row that rhyme.
  • Third person means the poem is told by a narrator about “he”, not by the man himself (“I”). It lets us watch him from outside.
  • A strict, regular shape can hold in feelings that might get out of control, so the form itself can show a struggle for control. our reading

  • Four stanzas of six lines (24 lines). WJEC says this suggests “regularity and the routine nature of his job.” our count; WJEC
  • Every stanza ends in a rhyming couplet: “Mass … grass”, “feet … heat”, “must … dust”, “where … care”. Each stanza clicks shut, like a neat, finished print. our count
  • WJEC: “The strict, controlled structure contrasts the chaos, confusion and lack of structure in war.” Just as he puts his films in “ordered rows”, the poem puts horror into neat stanzas. WJEC
  • Third person: WJEC says it reflects “the detachment a war photographer must feel when doing the job.” We watch him the way he watches the people he photographs. WJEC
  • Small cracks: some rhymes are only half-rhymes (“then” / “again”, “six” / “prick”), and sentences run on across line ends. The control is not quite perfect, like his hands that “seem to” tremble. our reading
  • Each stanza is a stage: the darkroom, home vs. war, the photo developing, the newspaper and the plane. WJEC: “Each stanza focuses on a different aspect of the war photographer's job.” WJEC

The story in short

A mother talks to her son. She remembers pinning a poppy on him before he left home. Then she follows a dove up the hill to the war memorial and listens for his voice.

The message

War hurts the people left at home, not just the soldiers. A mother's love and fear can be as painful as any wound, even if she has to hide it.

Why it was written

Weir wrote it in 2009, when Carol Ann Duffy asked poets for new war poems about Afghanistan and Iraq. Weir, a textile designer, told the story from a mother's side. Pearson Edexcel mark scheme; Oak National Academy; Wilfred Owen Association

The title

A poppy is the red flower people wear to remember those killed in war. The title is plural: the poem opens with poppies “on individual war graves”, so this is one family's story among many. Oak National Academy suggests the title could point to “remembrance, the many grieving mothers of war or the outward display of deeper grief.” one source (Royal British Legion; Oak National Academy)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupA mother speaks to her son as “you”. He never answers. She mixes three times together: the present, the morning he left, and his childhood. So the poem jumps about, the way memory does.
  • Lines 1–6It is “Three days before Armistice Sunday”, when the dead of all wars are remembered. Before her son leaves, she pins a paper poppy on the lapel of his blazer.
  • Lines 7–17She fusses over him: she uses Sellotape to pick cat hairs off his clothes and smooths his collar. She wants to rub noses like they did when he was little, and to stroke his hair, but she holds back. She can't find the words.
  • Lines 18–22She bravely walks him to the front door. Outside, the world is full of excitement, like a treasure chest. In a split second he is gone, “intoxicated” (thrilled, almost drunk) with it.
  • Lines 23–29She goes into his empty bedroom and lets a song bird out of its cage. Later a dove leads her out past the churchyard walls. She has rushed out without a coat, scarf or gloves, and her stomach is in knots.
  • Lines 30–35At the top of the hill she traces the names carved on the war memorial and leans on it. The dove flies off into the sky. She listens, hoping to hear his childhood voice on the wind.
The turning point (lines 21–23): “A split second / and you were away”. Up to here she is with her son. After “After you’d gone” she is alone, and the poem moves from the house out to the war memorial. How it ends: On the mother listening for a voice she cannot hear. We never learn whether the son has died. The poem leaves him somewhere between a boy leaving home and a name on a memorial. The tone: Tender, quiet and sad. AQA calls it “reflective”. Under the calm there is fear and grief that she works hard to hide.

Lines 1–6Three days before Armistice Sunday … spasms of paper red

She wraps Sellotape round her hand to pick up cat hairs from his clothes. “bandaged” makes an everyday job sound like treating a wound. She smooths his collar and forces her face to stay hard (“steeled”) so she won't cry.
Lines 11–16play at being Eskimos … gelled blackthorns of your hair
She longs to rub noses with him, a game from his childhood. She also wants to run her fingers through his hair. She resists both. His spiky gelled hair is like “blackthorns”, a prickly hedge: he is grown up now, and harder to touch.
Lines 16–18All my words flattened, rolled, turned into felt, … slowly melting
Her words fail her. Felt is a cloth made by pressing and rolling wool, so her words are squashed into something soft and silent. The sentence runs over the stanza break, as if her feelings spill out.
Lines 18–22I was brave … the world overflowing like a treasure chest
Her bravery is not fighting but letting him go. She opens the door on an exciting world full of treasure for a young man. Then he is gone in “A split second”, “intoxicated” (giddy with excitement).
Lines 23–26released a song bird from its cage. … this is where it has led me,
In his bedroom she frees a bird. It could stand for letting her son go, or for his spirit leaving. A “single dove”, a symbol of peace, leads her outside. “this is where it has led me” jumps into the present: she is speaking from the hill now.
Lines 27–29making tucks, darts, pleats … without a winter coat
Her nervous stomach is described in sewing words: tucks, darts and pleats are folds stitched into cloth. She has gone out unprotected, with no “reinforcements” (another army word) of scarf or gloves.
Lines 30–32On reaching the top of the hill … leaned against it like a wishbone.
She traces the carved names on the war memorial with her finger. She leans on it like a wishbone, the forked chicken bone people snap to make a wish. It suggests she is fragile, and still wishing he is safe.
Lines 33–35an ornamental stitch … your playground voice catching on the wind.
The dove is a tiny stitch against the sky. She listens for her son's voice, but only as a child in a playground. The poem ends with her waiting, with no answer.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: a mother's dramatic monologue in free verse
About the form
  • A dramatic monologue is “a poem written in the form of a speech of an individual character”. Britannica
  • We hear only one voice, so we see only what the speaker feels and remembers. There is no narrator to explain things.
  • Free verse is “poetry organized to the cadences of speech and image patterns rather than according to a regular metrical scheme”. In other words: no regular rhyme or beat, more like natural speech. Britannica
  • Pearson Edexcel (a different exam board, not AQA, which also sets this poem) calls it “a mother’s dramatic monologue directly addressed to her son”, “written in four stanzas of unequal length and is in free verse”. Pearson Edexcel mark scheme, Nov 2020
What this poem does with it
  • Talking to “you”: the whole poem is spoken to her son, but he never replies. His silence makes him feel absent, maybe lost for good. our reading
  • Shape: 6, 11, 12 and 6 lines. Oak National Academy says the “Two sestets either side of two longer stanzas may suggest the mother trying hard to contain her emotions”. A sestet is a six-line stanza. our count; Oak one source
  • Time jumps about: Pearson Edexcel’s mark scheme (not AQA) notes “Three different times”, “the present, the specific moment and the past”, and calls the poem “non-chronological”. The present (“this is where it has led me”) and his childhood (“when / you were little”) break into the story of his leaving. Pearson Edexcel one source
  • Breaks and run-ons: full stops land in the middle of lines (“war graves. Before you left,”), like a voice catching. The sentence “turned into felt,” runs on into the next stanza: “slowly melting.” Her feelings spill over the edges. Pearson Edexcel; our reading
  • Free verse fits a private, wandering voice. There is no drumbeat of marching, unlike older war poems such as The Charge of the Light Brigade. our reading
A sentence you could use: Weir writes Poppies as a dramatic monologue to a son who never answers, and its free verse moves between past and present like memory, so the reader feels the mother's grief through what she cannot say.
Textile imagery

Weir fills the poem with sewing words: “bias binding”, “felt”, “tucks, darts, pleats”, “an ornamental stitch”. They show a mother who makes and mends, using what she knows to cope. But cloth cannot protect a soldier. Weir trained in textile design, and Examiners for Pearson Edexcel (a different exam board, not AQA) praised students who made this link.

Semantic field: a group of words from the same topic (here, sewing) spread through a text.
War words in a home

Everyday things are described in military terms: a poppy “disrupting a blockade”, Sellotape “bandaged”, “reinforcements of scarf, gloves”. War has crept into the family home before the son has even gone. AQA's 2017 mark scheme points to Weir's “metaphor to present effects of war”.

Juxtaposition: placing two very different things side by side so we notice the contrast.
Birds as symbols

She frees a “song bird” in his room, then follows a “single dove”, the bird of peace. At the end the dove “pulled freely against the sky”. The birds may stand for the son flying the nest, his spirit, or the peace she longs for. The meaning is left open.

Symbol: an object that stands for a bigger idea, like a dove for peace.
Love she can't show

She keeps stopping herself: “I resisted the impulse”; she “steeled the softening” of her face. The poem is full of touches that never happen. Her bravery is holding back, not fighting. AQA’s Nov 2020 mark scheme points to “the reflective quality of ‘Poppies’”.

Restraint: holding back feelings; in poetry, saying less so the reader feels more.

Memories of home, and the language you grew up with, are part of who you are. War, borders and governments cannot take them away, even when they take away the place itself.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: free verse in three stanzas that all end in “sunlight”
About the form
  • Free verse is poetry “organized to the cadences of speech”, not to “a regular metrical scheme”. In plain English: no fixed rhythm and no rhyme pattern. Britannica
  • Because it follows the rhythms of speech, free verse can sound like a real person talking or thinking. Here it lets the speaker's memories and fears come out in her own voice. our reading
  • Enjambment is “the continuation of the sense of a phrase” beyond the end of a line. Britannica
What this poem does with it
  • Shape: 25 long lines in three stanzas of 8, 8 and 9 lines. No rhyme scheme and no regular rhythm. our count
  • The sunlight ending: every stanza ends on the word “sunlight” (lines 8, 16 and 25). Whatever threat each stanza brings, war, tyrants, a banned language, accusing voices, the memory comes back at the end. our reading
  • From past to present: stanza 1 looks back (“I left it as a child”); by stanza 3 it is all present tense (“I have no passport”). The memory is not finished: it is still happening to her. our reading
  • The last stanza breaks up: short, sharp sentences pile up: “They accuse me of absence, they circle me.” The full stops and repeated “They” make the threat close in, and the enjambment on “the city / of walls” makes the walls arrive with a jolt.
A sentence you could use: Rumens writes in free verse, like a real voice remembering, but ends each of the three stanzas on “sunlight”; however much conflict each stanza brings, the bright memory always returns, which shows that her identity cannot be taken from her.
Light and sunlight as a motif

Light runs through the poem: “sunlight-clear”, “bright”, “glow even clearer”, “shining eyes”, and “sunlight” at the end of every stanza. Light stands for her happy, hopeful memory of home. Against it are darker words: war, tyrants, tanks, walls, dark, death.

Motif: an image or idea that keeps coming back in a text.
The city as a person

In the last stanza the city “lies down in front of me”, and she combs “its hair”. Then “My city hides behind me.” The place has become someone she loves and protects. AQA's mark scheme names the “use of places as representation of people”, giving Tissue and The Emigrée as examples.

Personification: describing a thing as if it were a person.
Conflict creeping into the memory

War words appear inside lovely images: time “rolls its tanks”, “the frontiers rise”, the language is “banned by the state”. Conflict is never shown directly, but it is always pressing in on her memories.

Semantic field: a group of words linked by the same topic (here, war and state control).
An unnamed “I” and an unnamed “They”

We never learn her name, her country or who “They” are. That makes her story fit many people who have been forced from home. The repeated “They accuse me” sounds like a crowd ganging up on one person.

Persona: a voice or character a poet speaks through, who is not the poet.

  • Shape: seven stanzas of exactly six lines (42 lines). The neat, regular shape could suggest the order and discipline expected of the pilot, while the sentences pour freely through it. our count; our reading
  • Only three sentences. WJEC notes there are “only three sentences in the poem”, each marking a key moment. Sentence 1 (lines 1–30) is the flight and his memories, all in one breath, like a mind drifting. Sentence 2 (lines 31–40) is his return and rejection. Sentence 3 (lines 41–42) is his question. WJEC; our count
  • Shifting voice: a narrator reports what “she thought”; the daughter breaks in with “yes, grandfather’s boat”; from line 32 she speaks herself (“my mother”); line 41 goes back to “she said”. The pilot never speaks. His thoughts are only guessed: “he must have”. our reading
  • Enjambment acts out the meaning: “a one-way / journey” breaks off like the journey itself, and “we too learned / to be silent” lets the silence spill over the line. our reading
A sentence you could use: Garland tells Kamikaze second-hand, through a daughter's memories, so the pilot never speaks for himself; the form itself silences him, just as his family does after he returns.
Beautiful nature against a deadly mission

The sea is full of colour, movement and life: “green-blue translucent sea”, fish “flashing silver”. Nature reminds him that life is worth living. AQA's 2019 mark scheme names the “description of the natural world in ‘Kamikaze’”.

Sibilance: repeating “s” and “sh” sounds, here giving a soft, shimmering feel like the sea.
A story told second-hand

The story is reported (“she thought, / recounting it later to her children”) and the pilot's thoughts are guessed (“he must have”). We never hear him. This shows how far he has been shut out, and how the story is passed down through three generations.

Narrative perspective: the point of view a story is told from, such as first person (“I”) or third person (“he”).
Rich lists, then bare silence

Stanza 5 overflows with a list of the catch: mackerel, crabs, prawns, whitebait, tuna. After line 31 the language becomes plain and simple. The richness of life before his return is set against the emptiness after it.

Listing: a run of items one after another, often to show abundance or build up a picture.
An ironic last line

He survives, yet the family behaves as if he is dead. The poem ends on “the better way to die”: either way, he dies. AQA's 2022 mark scheme names “the internal conflict of beliefs and attitudes towards conflict” in Kamikaze.

Irony: when the result is the opposite of what someone meant or expected.

A likely question: Compare how poets present the ways people are affected by conflict in ‘Kamikaze’ and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’. (AQA's June 2024 wording)

A model comparison paragraph:
Both poets show conflict damaging a family far from the fighting, and both show it through silence. Garland's daughter remembers that her mother “never spoke again / in his presence”, and that the children “too learned / to be silent”. The enjambment carries the silence over the line break, as if it spreads through the whole family. Weir's mother is silenced too: “All my words / flattened, rolled, turned into felt”, a sewing metaphor that makes her words soft and useless. However, the causes are opposite. MODEL ANSWER · Q26 · WRITTEN TO AQA'S TOP LEVEL (26–30 MARKS)

Ozymandias and My Last Duchess

“Compare how poets present ideas about power in ‘Ozymandias’ and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’.” [30 marks]
AQA, June 2018, Question 26
This answer was written to match AQA's Level 6 descriptors. It has not been marked by AQA, and no one can guarantee a mark, but every part is designed to hit a top-level requirement. About 700 words: a realistic length for 45 minutes.

Where the 30 marks come from

AO1 · Ideas and argument
12

A clear argument that answers the question, compares the two poems, and uses well-chosen short quotations.

AO2 · How the poets write
12

Close analysis of words, structure and form, and the effect each choice creates.

AO3 · Context
6

The ideas and times behind the poems, linked to the question, not a history lesson.

Step 1: the 5-minute plan

  • Pick the partner: the question is about power, so choose My Last Duchess: another arrogant ruler. AQA's specimen mark scheme suggests this pairing.
  • Big idea (thesis): both rulers think their power will last and give themselves away through their own words. Shelley's tyrant has already been destroyed by time; Browning's is still alive and dangerous.
  • Three comparison points: 1. the rulers' pride and words; 2. power as giving orders (“command”); 3. art and who controls it. Then form, context and a judgement.

Step 2: the answer, with what earns the marks

Introduction Both Shelley and Browning present power as something arrogant men abuse and wrongly believe will last forever, and both poets let the powerful condemn themselves through their own words. However, while Shelley shows a tyrant whose power has already been destroyed by time, Browning shows a tyrant whose power is still alive, which makes My Last Duchess the more disturbing warning.
AO1
  • Answers the question in the first sentence, using its key word, “power”.
  • Sets up a similarity and a difference, so the whole essay is a comparison.
  • Ends with a judgement (“more disturbing”) to prove later.
Mark scheme, Level 6: “It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task”. Examiners (2020): students who began with “a thesis statement … tended to be more successful with AO1.”
Paragraph 1 · pride in their own words Ozymandias's pride is clearest in the words carved on his statue: “king of kings”. The title places him above every other ruler, and the imperative “Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!” commands rival kings to feel hopeless before his greatness. Yet Shelley follows it at once with the blunt, short sentence “Nothing beside remains.” The boast is cut dead, and the command turns against him: rulers should despair, but only because their works will crumble too. Browning's Duke shows the same pride in status when he complains that his wife ranked “My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name” with anybody else's gift. Calling his family name a “gift” reveals that he sees his status as a favour he hands down to others, and the long hyphenated phrase makes his ancestry sound heavy and self-important.
AO1AO2
  • Short, precise quotations, built into sentences.
  • Zooms in on single words (“despair”, “gift”) and explains what they suggest.
  • Uses terms (imperative) only where they help, then explains the effect.
  • Moves between the poems in the same paragraph.
Level 6: “Judicious use of precise references” and “a fine-grained and insightful analysis of methods”. Examiners (2023): “A well-selected word or phrase … is often much more effective than a long direct quotation.”
Paragraph 2 · power means giving orders For both rulers, power means giving orders, and both poets use the same word to show it. Shelley's sculptor captured the king's “sneer of cold command”: the harsh, repeated “c” sounds make his rule sound cruel and unfeeling. Browning's Duke uses the word himself: “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.” The semicolon creates a sharp pause, and the plain words hide a murder inside a single calm sentence. This is more chilling than Shelley's sneer, because the Duke never admits what he did; his polite understatement shows how easily absolute power ends a life.
AO1AO2
  • An original link: both poems use the word “command”. Spotting connections like this makes the comparison exploratory.
  • Analyses sound (“c” sounds) and punctuation (semicolon), each tied to meaning.
  • Evaluates which is more chilling, and says why.
Level 6: “Critical, exploratory comparison” and “Exploration of effects of writer's methods”. Examiners (2022): naming techniques “will not achieve above Level 2 AO2 if it is not tied to meaning.”
Paragraph 3 · art and who controls it Both poems present a work of art that outlives the person it shows, but they disagree about who controls it. In Ozymandias, the artist has the final word: “The hand that mock’d them” suggests the sculptor both copied the king's passions and made fun of them, so art judges the tyrant. In My Last Duchess, the tyrant owns the art. The Duke keeps his wife's portrait behind “The curtain I have drawn for you”, so even after her death he decides who may look at her. He ends by pointing out another possession: “Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse,”. The image of a god “taming” a creature mirrors how he tamed his wife.
AO1AO2
  • A less obvious idea (art and power) shows independent thinking.
  • Explains a word with two meanings (“mock’d”): the kind of detail top answers notice.
  • Uses a symbol (the curtain, Neptune), which the 2023 examiners praised.
Examiners (2023): “highly-effective discussion of the impact of ‘the curtain’, the image of the statue of Neptune”.
Paragraph 4 · form and structure The poets' choices of voice support these ideas. Shelley never lets Ozymandias speak directly: the poem is a story within a story, beginning “I met a traveller from an antique land”, so the king reaches us second-hand, pushed far into the past, and the poem ends not on him but on “The lone and level sands”. Time and nature have the last word. Browning does the opposite. As a dramatic monologue, the poem gives the Duke every word, and his silent listener never replies. The Duke controls the conversation just as he controlled his wife, and the tight rhyming couplets reflect that control, even while his words reveal more than he intends.
AO2
  • Structure through openings and endings, as the examiners recommend.
  • Form (dramatic monologue) is linked to the idea of control, not just named.
  • No rhyme-scheme spotting and no “sonnets are love poems”.
Examiners (2023): helpful structure points are “openings/endings”. They praised “Browning's use of dramatic monologue to depict megalomania” and using perspective “to indicate the passage of time”.
Paragraph 5 · context linked to ideas The poets' attitudes to power reflect their own beliefs. Shelley was a radical who, in Britannica's words, “rejected the monarchy, religion, war, and commerce”, and he published Ozymandias in 1818, when the editors of the newspaper that printed it had been jailed for attacking the Prince Regent. His poem reassures readers that no ruler lasts forever. Browning, a Victorian poet famous for the dramatic monologue, takes us inside the mind of a tyrant instead. By letting the Duke speak, he exposes how power used purely for personal pride destroys the people under it, which is a warning about the responsibility that comes with power.
AO3
  • Context explains why the poets think as they do, and links straight to the question.
  • No history lesson: one or two facts, then back to the idea.
  • Ends on responsibility and abuse of power, which the examiners rewarded.
Level 6: “specific, detailed links between context/text/task”. Examiners (2023): answers on “the responsibilities / abuse of power, fared much better” than those that stayed in the story.
Conclusion · the judgement Both poets present power as arrogant, controlling and ultimately self-defeating, because both rulers condemn themselves with their own words. But Shelley offers hope: time has already turned Ozymandias's empire into empty sand. Browning offers none. The Duke is still in power, already arranging his next marriage, so the danger has not ended. That is why My Last Duchess is the more frightening presentation of power: Shelley's tyrant is history, but Browning's could strike again.
AO1
  • Returns to the thesis from the introduction.
  • Makes a clear evaluative judgement and gives a reason for it.
  • Short: the conclusion pulls ideas together and adds no new quotations.
AQA's example answer commentary: “By the end, this comparison becomes evaluative as there is a judgement made as to which poem demonstrates the effects of power more strongly.”

  • ✔ Both poems in almost every paragraph, so it is a comparison throughout.
  • ✔ Short quotations, with individual words explained.
  • ✔ Every technique linked to its effect and to the question about power.
  • ✔ Structure through openings, endings and voice, not rhyme schemes.
  • ✔ Context used to explain ideas, briefly, never as a history lesson.

Remains and War Photographer

“Compare how poets present the ways people are affected by difficult experiences in ‘Remains’ and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’.” [30 marks]
AQA, November 2020 (the June 2020 paper), Question 26
This answer was written to match AQA's Level 6 descriptors. It has not been marked by AQA, and no one can guarantee a mark, but every part is designed to hit a top-level requirement. Remains is printed on the exam paper; War Photographer is written from memory, so its quotations are kept short. About 700 words: a realistic length for 45 minutes.

Step 1: the 5-minute plan

  • Pick the partner: the question is about how people are affected, so choose War Photographer: another man who brings a violent death home with him. AQA's mark scheme for this question names it, and the examiners said it “was used by many students”.
  • Big idea (thesis): both poems show a difficult experience that does not stay in the past, and a man who has no power to escape it. Armitage's soldier is haunted by a death he caused; Duffy's photographer by deaths he could only record, and by a public that does not care.
  • Three comparison points: 1. the violence itself; 2. how the experience comes back later; 3. who else is affected (or not). Then voice and endings, context, and a judgement.

Step 2: the answer, with what earns the marks

Introduction Both Armitage and Duffy present difficult experiences as wounds that do not stay in the past: each man carries a violent death home with him, and neither has the power to escape it. However, Armitage's soldier is haunted by guilt for a death he caused, while Duffy's photographer is haunted by deaths he could only record, and by a public that does not care. Remains shows the deeper personal damage, but War Photographer spreads the blame wider, to us.
AO1
  • Answers the question in the first sentence, using its key words: “difficult experiences” and how people are affected.
  • Sets up a similarity and a difference, so the whole essay is a comparison.
  • Ends with a judgement to prove later.

Paragraph 1 · the violence itself Both poets confront the reader with brutal images of violence. Armitage's speaker says “I see every round as it rips through his life”: the harsh alliteration of “round” and “rips” sounds like tearing, and “his life”, not “his body”, shows that a whole person is destroyed. The vague phrase “sort of inside out” suggests he has no proper words for what he saw. Duffy's violence is quieter but just as disturbing. The photographer remembers “the cries / of this man's wife” and “how the blood stained into foreign dust.” “Foreign” shows the distance between where the suffering happens and the safe England where it becomes pictures.
AO1AO2
  • Short, precise quotations, built into sentences.
  • Zooms in on single words (“life”, “foreign”) and explains what they suggest.
  • Names a technique (alliteration) only to explain its effect.
  • Moves between the poems in the same paragraph.
Mark scheme (Nov 2020), AO2: “use of brutal, vivid imagery such as ‘War Photographer’ or ‘Exposure’”. AO3: “any treatment of the effects of experiencing violence”.
Paragraph 2 · the experience comes back In both poems the experience returns long after it is over. Armitage's blunt line “End of story, except not really.” is the turning point: the soldier tries to close the story, then admits he cannot. Back home, the looter “bursts again through the doors of the bank”, and the verb “bursts” makes the memory as sudden and violent as the event. The military metaphor “dug in behind enemy lines” suggests the dead man is now the enemy, occupying the soldier's mind like a trench. Duffy shows the same delayed effect through the photographer's body: his hands “did not tremble then / though seem to now.” “Then” and “now” show the shock arriving only once he is safe. As the photograph develops, “a half-formed ghost” appears, so the image rising on the paper mirrors the memory rising in his mind.

  • Spots the turning point in Remains and explains it, not just quotes it.
  • Explains a metaphor (“dug in”) and the contrast of two small words (“then”, “now”): fine-grained analysis.
  • Finds a shared idea (delayed trauma) shown through different methods.
Mark scheme (Nov 2020), AO1: “any valid comparison of the effects of the first-hand experience of difficult experiences on the individual, such as ‘Bayonet Charge’ or ‘Exposure’ or ‘War Photographer’”. Level 6: “a fine-grained and insightful analysis”.
Paragraph 3 · who else is affected The poets differ in who shares the burden. Armitage's soldier is completely alone: “the drink and the drugs won’t flush him out”, and “flush” suggests he is trying, and failing, to wash the memory away. Duffy goes further and shows the people at home who are hardly affected at all. From “A hundred agonies in black-and-white” the editor will choose only “five or six”, and the reader's “eyeballs prick / with tears between the bath and pre-lunch beers.” Their sympathy is squeezed between two comforts. So both men are powerless in different ways: the soldier cannot control his own mind, and the photographer cannot make anyone care.
AO1AO2
  • Widens the question to people not directly involved: a less obvious, more exploratory idea.
  • Uses the idea of powerlessness, which the Nov 2020 examiners singled out in the best answers.
  • Every quotation is tied to the question, not left to speak for itself.
Examiners (Nov 2020): “some of the best responses took a wider approach to the task focus and wrote very effectively about how two poets explore ideas about the lack of individual power as their interpretation of ‘difficult experiences’.”
Paragraph 4 · voice and endings The poets' choices of voice match these experiences. Armitage writes in the first person, in a soldier's everyday speech (“legs it”, “my mates”), so we hear the trauma from the inside. Duffy uses the third person, keeping the same distance from the photographer that his job forces him to keep from his subjects. Her regular stanzas echo his “spools of suffering set out in ordered rows”: an attempt to put chaos in order. The endings show the difference. Remains closes on a short two-line final stanza, “his bloody life in my bloody hands.” “Bloody” is both literal and a swear word, and the guilt has no end. War Photographer ends instead on the public: “they do not care.”
AO2
  • Person (first vs third) is linked to closeness and distance, not just named.

  • Explains a word with two meanings (“bloody”), and no rhyme-scheme spotting.

Paragraph 5 · context linked to ideas The poets' purposes explain these differences. Armitage wrote Remains for The Not Dead, poems based on the testimonies of real ex-soldiers, men whom his website describes as having “seldom been heard before”. That is why the poem sounds like a spoken confession. Duffy was a friend of war photographers and was interested in people who must record horror without being able to help. Her poem is therefore about more than one man: it criticises a comfortable society that looks at suffering in a Sunday magazine and then forgets it.
AO3
  • Context explains why the poets write as they do, and links straight back to the question.
  • No history lesson: one fact per poet, and no extra detail about the real soldier.
  • Moves from the individual to society, a wider idea.

Conclusion · the judgement Both poets show that difficult experiences change people permanently and leave them powerless. Armitage's soldier suffers the deeper wound: his guilt is inside him, and the poem ends with no way out. But Duffy's poem is the more accusing. It suggests that the real difficult experience is not only what the photographer saw, but that nobody else wants to see it, and that makes the reader part of the problem.

MODEL ANSWER · Q26 · WRITTEN TO AQA'S TOP LEVEL (26–30 MARKS)

Exposure and Storm on the Island

“Compare how poets present ideas about conflict in ‘Exposure’ and in one other poem from ‘Power and conflict’.” [30 marks]
AQA, June 2025, Question 26
This answer was written to match AQA's Level 6 descriptors. It has not been marked by AQA, and no one can guarantee a mark, but every part is designed to hit a top-level requirement. About 700 words: a realistic length for 45 minutes. It shows how to use your nature poem for a conflict question.

Where the 30 marks come from

AO1 · Ideas and argument
12

A clear argument that answers the question, compares the two poems, and uses well-chosen short quotations.

AO2 · How the poets write
12

Close analysis of words, structure and form, and the effect each choice creates.

AO3 · Context
6

The ideas and times behind the poems, linked to the question, not a history lesson.

Step 1: the 5-minute plan

  • Pick the partner: the question is about conflict, and in Exposure the soldiers' worst enemy is the weather. So choose Storm on the Island: a community under attack from a storm. The June 2025 mark scheme suggests exactly this: “possible consideration of conflict with nature/the elements such as ‘Storm on the Island’”.
  • Be careful: in 2022 the examiners said Storm on the Island was “arguably a little more challenging in terms of focus and comparison” for a conflict question. So say the word “conflict” in every paragraph and keep showing that the storm is described as a war.
  • Big idea (thesis): both poets present nature as an enemy that attacks people who can only sit and wait. Heaney's islanders are left alive but facing “nothing”; Owen's soldiers are killed by it while “nothing happens”.
  • Three comparison points: 1. nature as a weapon; 2. powerless waiting and the word “nothing”; 3. comfort that turns hostile. Then openings and endings, context and a judgement.

Step 2: the answer, with what earns the marks

Introduction Both Owen and Heaney present conflict as a war between people and nature, in which the weather is the real enemy and humans can do nothing but wait. However, Heaney's islanders are ready for the attack and nobody dies, while Owen's soldiers are already fighting one war and are slowly killed by a second. This makes Exposure the darker presentation of conflict, because nature finishes what the war started.
AO1
  • Answers the question in the first sentence, using its key word, “conflict”.
  • Explains straight away why a storm poem fits a conflict question: it is a war with nature.
  • Sets up a similarity, a difference and a judgement (“darker”) to prove later.
Mark scheme, Level 6 (2025): “It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task”. Examiners (2020): students who began with “a thesis statement … tended to be more successful with AO1.”
Paragraph 1 · nature as a weapon Both poets turn the weather into a weapon. Owen opens with “the merciless iced east winds that knive us”: “merciless” gives the wind a cruel human will, and the verb “knive” turns it into a soldier stabbing the men. He then says the bullets are “Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow”, so the natural enemy is more dangerous than the human one. Heaney uses military words for a storm: the wind “strafes invisibly”, “Space is a salvo,” and “We are bombarded by the empty air.” These are words for attacks by aircraft and guns, so an ordinary storm becomes an air raid. The difference is that Heaney's war is a metaphor, while Owen's men face a real war and nature joins in against them.
AO1AO2
  • Short, precise quotations from both poems, built into sentences.
  • Zooms in on single words (“merciless”, “knive”, “strafes”) and explains what they suggest.
  • Groups Heaney's war words together, then explains their effect instead of just naming a “semantic field”.
Mark scheme (2025): “relevant treatment of the use of natural imagery to present ideas about conflict, such as ‘Storm on the Island’, or ‘Bayonet Charge’”. Level 6: “Judicious use of precise references to support interpretation(s)”.
Paragraph 2 · powerless waiting and “nothing” In both poems, the people in the conflict cannot fight back. Owen's refrain “But nothing happens.” is repeated at the end of four stanzas, so the poem itself seems stuck, like the soldiers waiting for an attack that never comes. The blunt question “What are we doing here?” shows they no longer understand why they are fighting. Heaney's islanders are just as passive: “We just sit tight while wind dives”. Strikingly, both poets use the word “nothing”. Heaney ends with “Strange, it is a huge nothing that we fear.” The enemy is invisible air, and the oxymoron “huge nothing” makes it impossible to fight. For Owen, “nothing” is worse: nothing happens, yet men are dying anyway.
AO1AO2
  • An original link: both poems use the word “nothing”. Spotting connections like this makes the comparison exploratory.
  • Explains repetition (the refrain) by its effect: the poem feels stuck, like the men.
  • Uses one term (oxymoron) only because it helps explain the idea.
Mark scheme (2025): “use and effect of particular features to create meanings, such as effects of sibilance, or repetition, or imagery” and “ideas about power/powerlessness”. Level 6: “Critical, exploratory comparison”.
Paragraph 3 · comfort that turns hostile Both poets show that, in conflict, even comforting things turn against people. Heaney admits “You might think that the sea is company,” but it “spits like a tame cat / Turned savage.” The simile makes something cosy and domestic attack, so nothing on the island is safe. Owen's soldiers dream of home: “Slowly our ghosts drag home”, but they find “on us the doors are closed”. Calling themselves “ghosts” suggests they already feel dead, and the closed doors show they are shut out from the life they are fighting for. Owen then takes the conflict deeper, into their faith: “For love of God seems dying.” Heaney's conflict stays outside the house; Owen's reaches the soldiers' minds and beliefs.
AO1AO2
  • A less obvious idea (comfort turning hostile) shows independent thinking.
  • Explains why the simile and the word “ghosts” matter, not just that they are there.
  • Shows conflict can be internal as well as physical.
Level 6 (2025): “a fine-grained and insightful analysis of methods” and “Exploration of effects of writer’s methods to create meanings”.
Paragraph 4 · openings and endings The poems' openings and endings show how the conflicts end. Heaney begins confidently: “We are prepared:”. The colon introduces a list of defences, and the chatty “you know what I mean” sounds calm. Yet the poem ends on the word “fear”: his confidence has collapsed. Owen starts in pain, “Our brains ache”, and ends with a burial party finding frozen men: “All their eyes are ice,”. It then closes on the refrain once more. Heaney's islanders are frightened but alive; Owen's ending treats death as if it were nothing at all, which is his bitterest point.
AO2
  • Structure through openings and endings, as the examiners recommend.
  • Tracks a change in tone (confident to fearful) and links it to the idea of conflict.
  • No rhyme-scheme spotting and no list of caesura and enjambment.
Examiners (2023): helpful structure points are “openings/endings, plot development, changes of pace / scene”. Examiners (2022): naming techniques “will not achieve above Level 2 AO2 if it is not tied to meaning.”
Paragraph 5 · context linked to ideas These differences come from the poets' experiences. Owen wrote from the front line: at the end of January 1917 his platoon spent two days and nights without shelter in a snowstorm, and the Wilfred Owen Association says that in Exposure “passive suffering is what it is all about.” That is why his soldiers never fight: Owen shows ordinary men left powerless in a war decided by others, and he was himself killed a week before it ended. Heaney's war is imagined. A 1976 review of his first collection noticed that he often describes nature through weapons, comparing bullfrogs to “mud grenades”. In Storm on the Island this habit shows how small any community is in conflict with the elements.
AO3
  • Context explains why the poets present conflict as they do, and links straight to the question.
  • No history lesson: one or two facts for each poet, then back to the idea.
  • Ends on powerlessness, an idea the 2025 mark scheme lists for AO3.
Mark scheme (2025): “ideas about conflict with the natural world” and “possible exploration of ideas about the ways individuals are affected by wider conflict”. Examiners (2022): paragraphs about “Owen’s war experiences” were “not useful if they meant the student lost sight of the poem.”
Conclusion · the judgement Both poets present conflict as a struggle with nature in which humans are powerless against an enemy they cannot fight. But Heaney's conflict ends in fear, not death: his community is prepared, and nobody dies. Owen's ends with frozen bodies and the flat words “nothing happens”. That is why Exposure is the more disturbing presentation of conflict: for Heaney nature is a frightening enemy, but for Owen it is one more enemy for men who were already losing a war.
AO1
  • Returns to the thesis from the introduction.
  • Makes a clear evaluative judgement and gives a reason for it.
  • Short: the conclusion pulls ideas together and adds no new evidence.
AQA's example answer commentary: “By the end, this comparison becomes evaluative as there is a judgement made as to which poem demonstrates the effects of power more strongly.” The same move works for conflict.

Checklist: why this reaches the top level

  • ✔ One big idea (a thesis) stated at the start and proved all the way through.
  • ✔ A nature poem made to fit a conflict question: every paragraph says how nature is presented as an enemy.
  • ✔ Both poems in every paragraph, so it is a comparison throughout.
  • ✔ Short quotations, with individual words explained.
  • ✔ Every technique linked to its effect and to the question about conflict.
  • ✔ Structure through openings, endings and repetition, not rhyme schemes.
  • ✔ Context used to explain ideas, briefly, never as a history lesson.
  • ✔ A clear judgement at the end.
Remember: Storm on the Island is not a war poem, so it only works for a conflict question if you keep showing that Heaney describes the storm as a war. If the question is about the effects of war on soldiers, a war poem such as Remains or Bayonet Charge may be a safer partner.

★AQA's own example answer

AQA has published one example answer to a Power and Conflict question, with an examiner's comments. It answers the specimen question on Ozymandias, paired with My Last Duchess.

How it opens: “Both Ozymandias and My Last Duchess show the effects of power and how it corrupts. Shelley describes Ozymandias’ ‘sneer of cold command’ as if to suggest that he is a cruel and heartless leader, only concerned with his own power and the immortality it will bring.”

The examiner's summary: “A thoughtful comparison which becomes exploratory as it develops. The student holds both poems together which enables a considered comparison to be made as to the extent to which both poems deal with ideas about power. By the end, this comparison becomes evaluative as there is a judgement made as to which poem demonstrates the effects of power more strongly.”

What to copy: answer the question for both poems in your first sentence; keep both poems in play; end with a judgement about which poem shows the idea more strongly, and why.

Source: AQA, “GCSE English Literature Assessment” guide (AQA-8702-EX-COMMENTARY). AQA calls it an “Exemplar response” and gives no mark.
