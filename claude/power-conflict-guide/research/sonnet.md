## 1. What a sonnet is

| Fact | Evidence (verbatim) | Label |
|---|---|---|
| 14 lines | BRIT-S: "fixed verse form of Italian origin consisting of 14 lines that are typically five-foot iambics rhyming according to a prescribed scheme". POETS: "Traditionally, the sonnet is a fourteen-line poem written in iambic pentameter". PA: "A sonnet, in English poetry, is a poem of fourteen lines, usually in iambic pentameter, that has one of two regular rhyme schemes" | SAFE |
| Usually iambic pentameter (five-foot iambics: ten syllables, da-DUM x5) | Same three quotes as above. The "ten syllables" gloss comes from EBSCO-OZ: "iambic pentameter (that is, ten syllables per line of coupled unstressed then stressed sounds)" | SAFE |
| Name means "little sound/song" | POETS: "The name is taken from the Italian sonetto, which means 'a little sound or song.'" | USE WITH CARE (single source) |
| Origin: Italy, 13th century (Sicily, Giacomo da Lentini) | BRIT-S: "The form seems to have originated in the 13th century among the Sicilian school of court poets". EBSCO-S: "Likely invented by thirteenth-century Italian poet Giacomo da Lentini" | SAFE (both sources hedge, with "seems" and "Likely") |
| Petrarch perfected it in the 14th century | BRIT-S: "it reached its highest expression in the 14th century in the poems of Petrarch". EBSCO-S: "Originating in Italy around 1235, the form was perfected by Francesco Petrarca (1304–74), known simply as Petrarch" | SAFE |
| Came to England in the 16th century with Wyatt and Surrey | BRIT-S: "The sonnet was introduced to England, along with other Italian verse forms, by Sir Thomas Wyatt and Henry Howard, earl of Surrey, in the 16th century." | Traditional subject: love | BRIT-S: "The typical Elizabethan use of the sonnet was in a sequence of love poems in the manner of Petrarch". EBSCO-S: "the woeful lover bewailing the indifference of his beloved, the trueness of his love for her, and the anguish her coldness causes him" | SAFE |
| Not only love. | The volta (turn) is a shift in thought or argument | BRIT-V: "the turn in thought in a sonnet that is often indicated by such initial words as But, Yet, or And yet." POETS: "This turn marks a shift in the direction of the foregoing argument or narrative." PA: "there is a volta, from the Italian for 'turn' - this is a shift in the poem's tone, subject or logic" | SAFE |

## 2. The two main types

### Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet
| Fact | Evidence (verbatim) | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Octave (8 lines) + sestet (6 lines) | POETS: "divided into two stanzas, the octave (the first eight lines) followed by the answering sestet (the final six lines)." EBSCO-S: "The Petrarchan, or Italian, sonnet consists of an octave (eight lines), rhyming abbaabba, and a sestet (six lines)" | SAFE |
| Octave rhymes **ABBAABBA** | BRIT-S: "The octave is rhymed abbaabba." PA: "a group of eight lines, rhymed abbaabba". POETS: "abba, abba, cdecde, or cdcdcd" | SAFE |
| Sestet rhyme **varies** (e.g. CDECDE, CDCDCD) | BRIT-S: "The rhyme scheme of the sestet varies; it may be cdecde, cdccdc, or cdedce." PA: "The distribution of these rhymes can vary, including cdcede, cdecde, cdedce, or even cdcdcd." | SAFE. Tell the student there is no single fixed sestet pattern. |
| Octave sets up a problem, sestet answers it | BRIT-S: "The first eight lines, the octave, state a problem, ask a question, or express an emotional tension. The last six lines, the sestet, resolve the problem". EBSCO-S: "The volta, or turn, is the shift between the octave, which sets up a dilemma, and the sestet, which offers a solution." | SAFE |
| Volta at line 9 (between lines 8 and 9) | POETS: "a turn, or volta, occurs between the eighth and ninth lines." BRIT-V: "The volta occurs between the octet and sestet in a Petrarchan sonnet". PA: "Often, at the point where the eight-line section, known as the octave, turns into the six-line section, or sestet, there is a volta" | SAFE |

### Shakespearean (English) sonnet
| Fact | Evidence (verbatim) | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Three quatrains + couplet, **ABAB CDCD EFEF GG** | BRIT-S: "The rhyme scheme of the English sonnet is abab cdcd efef gg." POETS: "three quatrains and a couplet follow this rhyme scheme: abab, cdcd, efef, gg." PA: "three quatrains, followed by a couplet, rhymed abab cdcd efef gg" | SAFE |
| Shakespeare used the form but did not invent it (Surrey set the rhyme scheme) | PA: "this is the form Shakespeare used for his sonnets, although he did not invent it." EBSCO-S: "It was Surrey who established the rhyme scheme that Shakespeare and others would later use" | SAFE |
| The quatrains build an argument, and the couplet concludes, sums up or twists it | PA: "the three quatrains tend to make an argument in three stages, which the couplet will sum up or comment on." POETS: the couplet is "usually arriving in the form of a conclusion, amplification, or even refutation of the previous three stanzas." | SAFE |
| Turn often at the couplet (lines 12/13), sometimes at 8/9 | BRIT-V: "sometimes between the 8th and 9th or between the 12th and 13th lines of a Shakespearean sonnet" | USE WITH CARE (single source; POETS and PA support the couplet's key role but do not give line numbers) |

## 3. Why poets use the form

- **Argument with a turn.** The form is built for setting up an idea and then shifting or answering it: "state a problem ... resolve the problem" (BRIT-S), "a shift in the direction of the foregoing argument or narrative" (POETS), "make an argument in three stages" (PA). **SAFE**
- **Tight control / compression.** GALE-OZ, on Ozymandias: "Shelley succeeded in containing his expression within the confines of the sonnet". The idea of the sonnet as a strict, controlled form is my own wording, based on the fixed-length and fixed-rhyme definitions above. **USE WITH CARE** (a single quote; the rest is inference).
- **Joining a tradition (and breaking it on purpose).** PA: "calling a poem a sonnet is not necessarily to define it strictly, but to say that it stands in relation to the long tradition of sonnets." PA also notes "years of experimentation that have loosened this definition." **USE WITH CARE** (single source)
- **Poets did bend the rules.** POETS: Milton "took liberties with the turn, allowing the octave to run into the sestet as needed." **USE WITH CARE** (single source). It is relevant to Ozymandias, whose sentence runs on across lines 8 and 9.
- **Love, but not only love.** See section 1. Love is the traditional subject (SAFE), but by the 1600s sonnets also dealt with politics (EBSCO-S, single source).

## 4. Ozymandias as a sonnet

The poem text (ANTH, lines 705–718) has 14 lines in one single block, with no stanza break.

### Rhyme scheme: ABABACDCEDEFEF (SAFE)
My check against ANTH: land **A** / stone **B** / sand **A** / frown **B** / command **A** / read **C** / things **D** / fed **C** / appear **E** / kings **D** / despair **E** / decay **F** / bare **E** / away **F** = **ABABACDCEDEFEF**.
- RPO: rhyme scheme "ababacdcedefef".
- GALE-OZ: "The rhyme scheme is ABABACDCEDEFEF."
- **Conflict:** EBSCO-OZ gives "abab, acdc, ece, fef". Its lines 9–11 should be EDE ("kings" rhymes with "things", line 7, not with "read"/"fed"). EBSCO-OZ has a slip here, so use the RPO/Gale version.
- Some rhymes are not perfect ("stone/frown", "appear/despair"; this is my own observation). UDEL: "Some of the rhymes are near rhymes or off rhymes (that is, they're not exact)." **USE WITH CARE**

### Neither standard type: an irregular, interlocking scheme (SAFE)
- GALE-OZ: "Rather than adhere to the English or Petrarchan rhyme schemes, Shelley does something different in 'Ozymandias.'"
- EBSCO-OZ: Shelley "chooses to forgo the conventional scheme and employs a more eccentric abab, acdc, ece, fef pattern that creates the immediate effect of a woven tapestry of sound and rhythm that helps to underscore the poem's essential irony."
- GALE-OZ on why: "the rhyme scheme actually evolves from start to finish. There is not a rhyme scheme separating an octave and a sestet; there is not a change at the end to finish with a neat couplet. The rhyme scheme of 'Ozymandias' gradually changes over time, just as the subject matter (Ozymandias's statue) does. The last two lines have little in common with the first two, just as the rubble of Ozymandias's statue has little in common with the original structure." (SINGLE SOURCE for this interpretation, but it comes from a published reference work. USE WITH CARE as an interpretation.)
- **It has no final rhyming couplet.** Lines 13–14 rhyme E/F (bare/away), from my check against ANTH, and GALE-OZ agrees ("not a change at the end to finish with a neat couplet"). **SAFE**
- "Interlocking" is my own word, not quoted. It describes how each new rhyme is threaded through the old ones (A runs to line 5; D spans lines 7–10; E spans lines 9–13). No allowed source uses the words "hybrid" or "interlocking". Those labels appear only on non-allowed sites, so do not cite them.

### Petrarchan in shape? Octave and sestet (USE WITH CARE)
- EBSCO-OZ: "The poem follows the traditional structure of the fourteen-line Italian sonnet, featuring an opening octave, or set of eight lines, that presents a conflict or dilemma, followed by a sestet, or set of six lines, that offers some resolution or commentary upon the proposition introduced in the octave."
- UDEL (weak): "While it's not exactly the rhyme scheme of a Petrarchan sonnet, it's closer to it than it is to a Shakespearean sonnet and uses the 8/6 line structure." Also: "Look at how the first 8 lines are stacked on top of the last 6, like a statue on a pedestal."
- **Conflict:** GALE-OZ says the *rhyme* does not separate an octave from a sestet ("There is not a rhyme scheme separating an octave and a sestet"). The two claims fit together. The *sense* divides 8/6, but the *rhymes* do not: the D rhyme ("things", l.7) returns in the sestet ("kings", l.10). That last point is my own check.

### Volta: is there one, and where? (USE WITH CARE / partial GAP)
- No allowed source I opened uses the word "volta" or "turn" for Ozymandias specifically. That is a **GAP**.
- The nearest support: EBSCO-OZ's octave "presents a conflict or dilemma" and is followed by a sestet that "offers some resolution or commentary". This implies a turn between lines 8 and 9.
- What happens at line 9 (my own reading of ANTH, safe to state as observation): line 9, "And on the pedestal these words appear:", moves from describing the broken statue (lines 2–8) to the words on the pedestal (lines 10–11). After that, "Nothing beside remains" (line 12) moves to the empty desert. Some students call this a second turn. That is only an interpretation; no allowed source says it.
- The sentence runs on across the line 8/9 break (line 8 ends with ";" and line 9 begins "And"). So the turn is softened, not a hard break. This is my observation. Compare POETS on Milton "allowing the octave to run into the sestet".
- **AQA does expect volta talk** (AQA-SMS: "use / effect of volta"). So the student can safely say "the volta / shift comes at line 9, 'And on the pedestal'" as his own analysis. He should not attribute it to a critic.

### Metre: iambic pentameter (SAFE)
- GALE-OZ: "the poem is fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, which are very traditional elements."
- EBSCO-OZ: "The poem is conventionally written in iambic pentameter (that is, ten syllables per line of coupled unstressed then stressed sounds)."
- Wikipedia's infobox says "Loose iambic pentameter" but cites no source, so it is not used. There is a GAP on a reliable source discussing irregular stresses (e.g. a strong first syllable on "Half sunk", "Tell that", "Look on", "Nothing").

### Narrative frame (for context; already covered elsewhere)
- RPO opening: "I met a traveller from an antique land, / Who said". The poem passes through several voices: speaker → traveller → inscription. See strand1_power.md and context_shelley.md.

## 5. AQA's own words (reuse from exam_materials.md, already verified in verify1_power.md)
- AQA-SMS (AO2): "Use of sonnet to explore ideas, possibly dealing with structural elements, such as use / effect of volta, rhyming couplet, line lengthening to stress particular ideas"
- AQA-SMS (AO3): "Any valid points about the use of sonnet / lyric poetry to explore universal ideas" and "Any valid points about literary / generic conventions"
- AQA-ER23 (warning): "What was less successful was where students attempted to say anything meaningful about Shelley's use of sonnet form, leading to comments such as 'he uses a sonnet because sonnets are love poems which shows Ozymandias loves himself'. This isn't particularly helpful as a line of argument as there isn't really anywhere to go with this idea once it has been stated."
- AQA-ER23 (what worked): "consideration of the use of perspective to indicate the passage of time and thereby drawing attention to the 'antique'-ness of his tyrannical rule and all power eventually turning to dust, was much more effective"
- Note: the mark scheme mentions a "rhyming couplet", but Ozymandias **has no final rhyming couplet**. The mark scheme lists general sonnet features a student *might* discuss. A strong answer can say the expected couplet is missing, so the poem refuses a neat ending (GALE-OZ: "not a change at the end to finish with a neat couplet").

### Safe exam sentences (student's own analysis, based on the above)
- "Shelley uses a sonnet, a strict 14-line form in iambic pentameter, but breaks its rules: the rhyme scheme (ABABACDCEDEFEF) fits neither the Petrarchan nor the Shakespearean pattern, and there is no closing couplet. Like the statue, the form is half-ruined."
- "The shift at line 9 ('And on the pedestal') moves from the broken face to the boastful words, and 'Nothing beside remains' turns again to the empty desert, so the structure itself undercuts the king's power."
- Avoid: "sonnets are love poems so Ozymandias loves himself" (AQA-ER23).

2. **GAP:** no allowed source uses "hybrid" or "interlocking" for Ozymandias. Those labels appear only on non-allowed sites. Allowed sources say "eccentric" (EBSCO-OZ) and "does something different" (GALE-OZ).
3. **CONFLICT:** EBSCO-OZ's rhyme scheme ("abab, acdc, ece, fef") disagrees with RPO, Gale and my own check (ABABACDCEDEFEF). Use ABABACDCEDEFEF.
