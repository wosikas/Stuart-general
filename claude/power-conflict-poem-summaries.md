# Power and Conflict: The 15 Poems for Q26

Oct 6, 2026 · @Stuart

## How to use this guide

In Q26 one poem is printed on the exam paper. You compare it with **one other poem you know from memory**. So you need a quick grasp of all 15 poems, and deep knowledge of a few partner poems.

**Start with three anchor poems:** Ozymandias (power), Remains (war) and Storm on the Island (nature; and your partner when Ozymandias or Remains is the printed poem). Together they give you a sensible partner for any of the 15. Learn their quotations first.

Each poem section has the same parts:

1. **What happens:** the poem in plain English.
2. **Big ideas (AO1):** the themes a question could ask about.
3. **Key quotations:** short quotations to learn, each with what it shows.
4. **Methods (AO2):** language, structure and form, and why they matter.
5. **Context (AO3):** facts about the poet and the time, each from a named source.
6. **Best partners:** which poems compare well, by theme.
7. **Exam history:** whether the poem has been printed on a past paper.

**How to read the labels:** words in quotation marks are copied exactly from the poem or a named source. Advice (such as which partner to choose) is our suggestion, not AQA's. AQA rewards any well-supported reading, so the readings here are strong options, not the only right answers.

**The three things Q26 marks (the "assessment objectives"):**

- **AO1 (12 marks):** your ideas and argument, backed up with references to both poems.
- **AO2 (12 marks):** how the poets write: their choice of words, images, structure and form, and the effect.
- **AO3 (6 marks):** context: the ideas, times and situations behind the poems.

**Key terms used in this guide:**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Alliteration | Words close together starting with the same sound ("sneer of cold command") |
| Sibilance | Repeated "s" sounds, often hissing or soft |
| Metaphor | Describing one thing as if it were another ("Space is a salvo") |
| Simile | A comparison using "like" or "as" ("spits like a tame cat") |
| Personification | Giving something non-human human actions (the mountain "Strode after me") |
| Oxymoron / paradox | Two opposite ideas put together ("marriage hearse"; "a huge nothing") |
| Imperative | A command ("Honour the charge they made!") |
| Enjambment | A sentence running on over a line break |
| Caesura | A pause or full stop in the middle of a line |
| Refrain | A line repeated through a poem ("But nothing happens.") |
| Sonnet | A 14-line poem, usually with a set rhyme pattern |
| Blank verse | Unrhymed lines of about ten syllables with a regular beat |
| Dramatic monologue | A poem spoken by one character who is not the poet, often to a silent listener |
| Romantic poets | A group of poets around 1800, including Wordsworth and Shelley. Britannica says Wordsworth and Coleridge's Lyrical Ballads (1798) "helped launch the English Romantic movement" |

**Check the question first.** If the question is about **power**, Ozymandias is usually the best partner. If it is about **conflict or its effects**, use Remains. If it is about **nature**, use Storm on the Island. If Ozymandias itself is printed, use Storm on the Island. The examiners say: pick the poem that fits the question "rather than simply choosing one you know well".

There have been 9 real Power and Conflict exam questions, plus AQA's specimen (sample) question. Almost every one asks about **power** or **conflict, war and its effects**. The frame never changes: *Compare how poets present \[idea\] in \[printed poem\] and in one other poem from 'Power and conflict'.* It is worth 30 marks: 12 for AO1, 12 for AO2 and 6 for AO3.

There were no exams in summer 2020 or summer 2021. In Nov 2021 and June 2022, Power and Conflict was on a separate 50-minute poetry paper worth 30 marks.

**Never printed yet:** Extract from The Prelude, The Charge of the Light Brigade, Storm on the Island, Poppies, Tissue, The Emigrée and Checking Out Me History. Any of these could be printed, so know a partner for each (see the table above).

**Two rules from the mark scheme to remember:**

- You must write about **two** poems. An answer on the printed poem alone can be "capped" at the top of Level 2 (10 marks).
- AQA's suggested points are not the only right answers. Every mark scheme says: "Examiners are encouraged to reward any valid interpretations."

**What the top level (Level 6, 26–30 marks) looks like, in AQA's words (June 2025):** "a critical, exploratory, well-structured argument. It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task supported by a range of judicious references to the text. There will be a fine-grained and insightful analysis of methods supported by judicious use of subject terminology."

In plain English: build one clear argument that answers the question, back it with well-chosen short quotations, and explain *how* the poets' choices of words and structure create meaning.

Sources: AQA question papers and mark schemes, specimen to June 2025 (links in Sources).

## The 15 poems

The poems are grouped by theme. The three **anchor poems** are marked ★ and have 8 quotations each; learn those first. All quotations follow the wording in AQA's own anthology. Exam papers sometimes print small spelling differences (for example "shattered" for "shatter'd" in 2018), so if the printed poem differs, quote the paper in front of you.

### War and its aftermath

#### ★ Remains — Simon Armitage

**What happens:** A British soldier tells how he and two others shot a looter running from a bank, who was "probably armed, possibly not." The body is carted off and he tries to call it "End of story". But back home on leave he keeps seeing the dead man in flashbacks and dreams, and drink and drugs cannot get rid of him.

**Big ideas (AO1):**

- Guilt that never goes away: one split-second decision stays with him for life.
- Doubt: the poem never settles whether the killing was justified.
- The mental damage of war lasts long after the soldier comes home.
- He moves from shared blame ("all three of us") to carrying it alone ("my bloody hands").

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "On another occasion" | The casual opening suggests killing is routine, one "occasion" among many. The poem starts mid-story, like a soldier chatting. |
| "probably armed, possibly not." | The doubt at the heart of the poem. It is repeated later, in his dreams, so the question keeps going round his head. |
| "myself and somebody else and somebody else" | Repeating "somebody else" spreads the blame. The other soldiers are never named. |
| "I see every round as it rips through his life" | Violent alliteration ("round… rips"). "I see" makes the killing feel like a replay in his mind. |
| "tosses his guts back into his body" | The casual verb "tosses" shows how soldiers become hardened to death. |
| "End of story, except not really." | The turning point. The slang tries to close the story, but "except not really" shows it cannot end. The poem then moves home. |
| "dug in behind enemy lines" | Military metaphor: the memory is like an enemy soldier in a trench inside his mind. The war has followed him home. |
| "his bloody life in my bloody hands." | The last line. "bloody" is literal blood and a swear word. "my… hands" shows he now takes the guilt as his own. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Voice:** first person, in casual soldier's slang ("legs it", "my mates"). It sounds like a real soldier talking to us.
- **Structure:** seven four-line stanzas and a final two-line stanza. The first half is the shooting; from "End of story" it moves home, so the second half shows the effects lasting longer than the event.
- **Repetition:** "probably armed, possibly not" and "I see… I see" return like flashbacks, so the form itself acts out trauma.
- **Enjambment across stanzas** ("But I blink // and he bursts again") suggests memories he cannot hold back.
- **Ending:** the short final stanza leaves the guilt unresolved.

**Context (AO3):**

- The poem is from *The Not Dead*. Armitage's own website says these poems "were originally aired on a Channel 4 documentary film of the same name, shown in the summer of 2007" and "focus on the testimonies of veterans". [Source](https://www.simonarmitage.com/works/the-not-dead/)
- Use with care (one source only): the exam board WJEC says it is based on the experiences of a soldier in Iraq in 2003, who later suffered from PTSD. [Source](https://resource.download.wjec.co.uk/vtc/2024-25/edu/edu24-25_2g-14/pdf/remains-by-simon-armitage.pdf)
- Keep context to ideas. AQA's 2022 report warned that "extraneous information about Guardsman Tromans" (the soldier) does not help if you lose sight of the poem.

**Best partners:** War Photographer (men haunted by what they saw), Bayonet Charge (terror during, against guilt after), Kamikaze, Exposure, Poppies.

**Exam history:** printed in Nov 2020 (the June 2020 paper), on "the ways people are affected by difficult experiences".

#### The Charge of the Light Brigade — Alfred Tennyson

**What happens:** In the Crimean War, about 600 British cavalrymen are ordered to charge down a valley at the Russian guns. The order is a mistake ("Some one had blunder'd"), but they obey without question. Many are killed, and only some ride back. The poem ends by telling us to honour them.

**Big ideas (AO1):** duty and obedience over questioning; glory and courage; a quiet criticism of the commanders; loss on a huge scale.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward," | Repetition and a galloping rhythm, like horses' hooves pushing forward. |
| "Into the valley of Death / Rode the six hundred." | A refrain. Death is personified; the men ride knowingly towards it. |
| "Some one had blunder'd:" | The only open criticism. The vague "Some one" avoids naming who was to blame. |
| "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die:" | Repeated "Theirs": a soldier's job is to obey, not to think. It can be read as praise or as quiet protest. |
| "Into the jaws of Death, / Into the mouth of Hell" | Metaphor: the battlefield is a monster swallowing the men. |
| "Honour the charge they made!" | Imperative and exclamation: the poem ends as a public command to remember them. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Third-person narrative:** the men are seen from outside as one heroic group, "the six hundred", not as individuals.
- **Rhythm:** short lines with a falling beat that copies the gallop of the charge.
- **The changing refrain tracks the losses:** "Rode the six hundred" becomes "Not the six hundred", then "Left of six hundred".
- **Mirroring:** "Cannon to right of them… left of them… in front of them" later becomes "Cannon behind them", so they are surrounded going in and coming out.

**Context (AO3):**

- The charge took place at Balaklava on 25 October 1854. The National Army Museum says it symbolises "both the bravery of the British soldier and the incompetence of the Army commanders", and that "Airey's orders were misunderstood". [Source](https://collection.nam.ac.uk/detail.php?acc=1962-11-4-3)
- Casualties: the National Army Museum gives "Around 260 men of the Light Brigade's 673 were killed or wounded". Other sources give different figures, so avoid exact numbers. [Source](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/battle-balaklava)
- Tennyson was Poet Laureate from 1850 and wrote the poem "only a few minutes after reading an account in The Times" of the charge (Poetry Archive). [Source](https://poetryarchive.org/poet/alfred-tennyson/)

**Best partners:** Remains or Bayonet Charge (obeying orders: glory against fear and guilt), Exposure, Kamikaze.

**Exam history:** never printed yet.

#### Bayonet Charge — Ted Hughes

**What happens:** A soldier suddenly finds himself running across a field under fire towards a hedge. Halfway, he almost stops, wondering why he is doing this. A wounded hare in the furrows shows the horror, and he charges on with only terror left. The poem never names the war, but it is usually read as the First World War.

**Big ideas (AO1):** patriotic ideals collapse in real combat; one man as a tiny part of a huge war machine; nature is harmed by war; fear takes over from thought.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "Suddenly he awoke and was running – raw" | Starts in the middle of the action. "awoke" suggests waking up to reality; "raw" means inexperienced and exposed. |
| "Bullets smacking the belly out of the air" | Violent verb and personification: even the air is wounded. |
| "The patriotic tear that had brimmed in his eye" | Patriotism is in the past ("had brimmed"). It turns into "Sweating like molten iron": ideals become pain. |
| "In what cold clockwork of the stars and the nations" | Metaphor: war is an uncaring machine and he is a cog in it. |
| "Threw up a yellow hare that rolled like a flame" | The one bright, natural image is destroyed, showing war's cruelty to nature. |
| "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera" | Grand ideals end in a dismissive "etcetera", then are "Dropped like luxuries". |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Third person ("he"):** an unnamed soldier, so he stands for every soldier. (One AQA mark scheme wrongly calls it first person; the poem uses "he".)
- **Three stanzas, three stages:** action, then time freezing as he questions ("his foot hung like / Statuary in mid-stride"), then action again.
- **Dashes, enjambment and uneven lines:** they copy breathless, stumbling running and panic.
- **Ending:** "His terror's touchy dynamite" leaves no glory, only fear about to explode.

**Context (AO3):** Hughes was Poet Laureate from 1984 to 1998 (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ted-Hughes) AQA's 2022 report praised links between the "patriotic tear" and the rejection of "King, honour…", and warned that paragraphs about "Hughes' father as a soldier" do not help unless linked to the poem.

**Best partners:** Remains (fear during against guilt after), Exposure, The Charge of the Light Brigade (honour celebrated against honour dropped), Kamikaze.

**Exam history:** printed in June 2017 ("the effects of war") and June 2022 ("ideas about conflict").

#### War Photographer — Carol Ann Duffy

**What happens:** A war photographer is back in England, alone in his darkroom, developing his films. As a picture of a dying man appears, he remembers the man's wife crying. His editor will use only "five or six" photos in a Sunday paper, readers will feel sad briefly, and from the aeroplane he stares "impassively" at where he earns his living, while the readers back home "do not care".

**Big ideas (AO1):** carrying other people's suffering; a public that soon forgets; safe England against war zones; the duty to record rather than help.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "spools of suffering set out in ordered rows." | Sibilance and metaphor: the films hold "suffering". "ordered rows" can suggest war graves. |
| "a priest preparing to intone a Mass." | Religious simile: his work is solemn, like a funeral for the dead. |
| "Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh. All flesh is grass." | A list of real war zones, each cut off by a full stop like a roll-call. All life is short. |
| "which did not tremble then / though seem to now." | Delayed trauma: steady at the scene, shaking at home. |
| "A hundred agonies in black-and-white" | Each photo is an "agony", yet the editor picks only "five or six". |
| "The reader's eyeballs prick / with tears between the bath and pre-lunch beers." | Readers' sympathy is squeezed between comforts: satire of people who do not care. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Third person:** the narrator keeps a distance from the photographer, as he keeps a distance from his subjects.
- **Regular form:** four six-line stanzas, each ending in a rhyming couplet. The ordered form contrasts with the chaos of war.
- **Contrast of places:** "Rural England" against "fields which don't explode beneath the feet / of running children".
- **Developing a photo as structure:** "a half-formed ghost" appears on the paper as the memory returns.
- **Ending:** "they do not care" ends on cold indifference.

**Context (AO3):**

- Duffy was the first woman Poet Laureate, from 2009 to 2019 (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Carol-Ann-Duffy)
- The poem was first published in *Standing Female Nude* in 1985 (Scottish Poetry Library). [Source](https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/war-photographer/)
- Use with care (one source only): WJEC says Duffy was inspired by her friendships with the war photographers Don McCullin and Philip Jones Griffiths. [Source](https://resource.download.wjec.co.uk/vtc/2024-25/edu/edu24-25_2g-11/pdf/war-photographer-carol-ann-duffy.pdf)

**Best partners:** Remains (the strongest pair), Poppies, Kamikaze, The Charge of the Light Brigade (a poem that tells readers to remember, against readers who forget).

**Exam history:** printed in June 2019, on "the ways that people are affected by war".

### Power and pride

#### ★ Ozymandias — Percy Bysshe Shelley

**What happens:** The speaker meets a traveller who describes a huge, broken statue of an ancient king in the desert: two legs standing, and a cracked face with a cruel sneer. On its base the king boasts that other rulers should "despair" at his works. But nothing is left around it except empty sand.

**Big ideas (AO1):**

- Human power does not last: the "king of kings" is now a ruin.
- Time and nature are stronger than any ruler.
- Pride: the tyrant's own boast becomes the joke against him.
- Art outlasts power: the sculptor's work, and the poem, survive while the empire has gone.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "I met a traveller from an antique land" | The story reaches us second-hand, so the king is far away in time. "antique" stresses how ancient and forgotten he is. |
| "Two vast and trunkless legs of stone" | "vast" shows his ambition; "trunkless" shows the body has gone. Power is reduced to fragments. |
| "Half sunk, a shatter'd visage lies" | Verbs of collapse: his face, the symbol of who he was, is sinking into the sand. |
| "sneer of cold command" | Hard "c" sounds capture his cruelty and contempt for his people. |
| "The hand that mock'd them and the heart that fed" | The sculptor's hand "mock'd" (copied and ridiculed) the king. Art judges the tyrant. |
| "'My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:" | An arrogant title, setting himself above all rulers. Ironic, because we can see the ruin. |
| "Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!'" | Imperative and exclamation. He meant them to despair at his greatness; now it reads as a warning that their works will fall too. |
| "Nothing beside remains." / "The lone and level sands stretch far away." | The short, blunt sentence cuts off the boast. The final line's long vowels show endless, empty time and space. Nature has the last word. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Sonnet:** 14 lines. Its rhyme pattern fits neither of the usual sonnet patterns, which can mirror the broken statue. Do not say "sonnets are love poems": the 2023 examiners' report says this argument goes nowhere.
- **Layered voices:** the speaker, then the traveller, then the king's words on the pedestal. Each layer pushes the king further into the past. The 2023 report praised using perspective "to indicate the passage of time".
- **Irony and structure:** the boast is followed at once by "Nothing beside remains.", so the poem itself proves the boast false.
- **Ending on sand:** AQA's 2018 mark scheme calls the sand a "metaphor for the passing of time and impermanence of human constructions".

**Context (AO3):**

- Published in *The Examiner* on 11 January 1818 (Britannica). Britannica calls it "a political statement by one of English literature's most radical voices". [Source](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ozymandias-poem)
- Shelley wrote it in friendly competition with Horace Smith, whose sonnet with the same title appeared in *The Examiner* on 1 February 1818 (Britannica; Representative Poetry Online, University of Toronto). [Source](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/ozymandias)
- "Ozymandias" is the Greek name for the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II (Representative Poetry Online). [Source](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/ozymandias)
- Shelley was a radical Romantic who rejected "the monarchy, religion, war, and commerce" (Britannica). Oxford expelled him in 1811 over a pamphlet called *The Necessity of Atheism*. [Source](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Percy-Bysshe-Shelley)

**Best partners:** Storm on the Island (nature beats human power), My Last Duchess (another arrogant ruler), London, Tissue, Checking Out Me History.

**Exam history:** printed on the specimen paper and in June 2018, both on "ideas about power".

#### London — William Blake

**What happens:** The speaker walks through London and sees misery on every face. He hears suffering everywhere: crying men and babies, child chimney sweeps, dying soldiers, and at night a young prostitute whose curse spreads disease even into marriage. He blames those in power, and people's own "mind-forged" acceptance.

**Big ideas (AO1):** abuse of power by the Church, the monarchy and those who own things; suffering that reaches everyone, even babies; people trapped by their own minds; the city as a picture of a corrupt society.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "I wander through each chartered street," | "chartered" means owned and controlled. Even the streets and the "chartered Thames" belong to the powerful. |
| "Marks of weakness, marks of woe." | Repetition and alliteration. "mark" means to notice; "marks" are signs left behind, like scars. Suffering is written on every face. |
| "The mind-forged manacles I hear:" | Metaphor: chains made by the mind. Oppression is mental as well as physical. |
| "Every black'ning church appalls," | The soot-stained church is also morally stained for ignoring child chimney sweeps. |
| "Runs in blood down palace walls." | The soldier's dying sigh becomes blood on the palace: the monarchy is to blame for his death. |
| "And blights with plagues the marriage hearse." | Oxymoron: "marriage" (new life) joined to "hearse" (death). Corruption reaches every part of life. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Form:** four four-line stanzas with regular alternate rhyme. The simple, song-like form makes the horrors more shocking.
- **Repetition of "every":** suffering is everywhere and universal.
- **From seeing to hearing:** "mark in every face" becomes "I hear", so the cries of the city get louder.
- **Ending:** the final image of the "new-born" and the "marriage hearse" shows corruption infecting the next generation.

**Context (AO3):**

- Published in *Songs of Experience* in 1794 (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/topic/London-poem-by-Blake)
- Blake was born and died in London and worked as "an English engraver, artist, poet, and visionary" (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Blake)
- AQA's 2021 report praised answers "exploring ideas about power and abuse of power by church and state", and different readings of "mind-forged manacles".

**Best partners:** Ozymandias ("an excellent choice", 2021 report), My Last Duchess, Checking Out Me History.

**Exam history:** printed in Nov 2021, on "ideas about power and control".

#### My Last Duchess — Robert Browning

**What happens:** The Duke of Ferrara shows a visitor a portrait of his dead wife. He complains that she smiled at everyone and valued small gifts as much as his ancient family name. He hints that he "gave commands" and her smiles "stopped", meaning he had her killed. The visitor is arranging the Duke's next marriage.

**Big ideas (AO1):** power and control over others; pride in status; women treated as possessions; jealousy and violence hidden under polite speech.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "That's my last Duchess painted on the wall," | "my" shows ownership; "last" suggests one in a series. Cold from the first line. |
| "The curtain I have drawn for you, but I)" | Only he may open the curtain, so he controls who sees her even after death. |
| "she liked whate'er / She looked on, and her looks went everywhere." | He resents her friendly, free nature. |
| "My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name" | His pride in his family status. |
| "This grew; I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together." | A chilling hint of murder, told in a few blunt words. |
| "Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse," | The god taming a creature mirrors the Duke taming women. He moves straight on to another possession. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Dramatic monologue:** one speaker, not the poet. The Duke reveals his cruelty without meaning to; we judge him by his own words.
- **Silent listener:** the visitor never speaks. The Duke controls the conversation as he controlled his wife.
- **Rhyming couplets in one long stanza:** 56 lines. The strict rhyme mirrors his control, while enjambment makes it sound like casual speech.
- **Dashes and hesitations:** "A heart – how shall I say? – too soon made glad," show his jealousy breaking through.

**Context (AO3):**

- Published in 1842 in *Dramatic Lyrics*. Britannica: "It becomes apparent at last that he himself brought about her death." [Source](https://www.britannica.com/topic/My-Last-Duchess)
- Browning was a "Major English poet of the Victorian age, noted for his mastery of dramatic monologue" (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Robert-Browning)
- **Common mistake:** the 2023 examiners' report says some students thought the Duke "is grieving the loss of his first wife, which means they missed the point of the poem entirely."

**Best partners:** Ozymandias (two arrogant rulers), London, Checking Out Me History.

**Exam history:** printed in June 2023, on "the effects of power".

#### Tissue — Imtiaz Dharker

**What happens:** The speaker thinks about thin paper that light shines through: pages in old books where family births and deaths are written, maps, receipts. Buildings, borders and money seem solid but could "fall away" like paper. The speaker imagines an architect building instead with "living tissue", a structure "never meant to last", which turns "into your skin".

**Big ideas (AO1):** human-made power, borders and buildings are fragile; paper and skin record what matters most (family, birth, death); light and openness are better than solid walls; human life is precious.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "Paper that lets the light / shine through" | Opening image: something fragile that lets light (hope) through. |
| "this / is what could alter things." | Fragile things have the power to change the world. |
| "If buildings were paper" | Imagines that solid buildings, and the power they stand for, could "fall away on a sigh". |
| "The sun shines through / their borderlines" | Borders on maps look flimsy when light shines through them. |
| "through the shapes that pride can make" | Buildings and monuments are signs of human pride, which light can break through. |
| "turned into your skin." | The last line stands alone: paper becomes skin, linking it to human life. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Extended metaphor:** paper and tissue stand for human life and the things humans build. AQA's 2020 mark scheme names the "use of metaphor" in Tissue.
- **Form:** nine four-line stanzas and a final one-line stanza, with no regular rhyme. The single last line makes the link to "skin" stand out.
- **Contrast:** solid "brick / or block" and "monoliths" against fragile, "luminous" paper.
- **A hopeful ending:** the poem wishes for structures "never meant to last", made of "living tissue". It accepts that nothing human is permanent.

**Context (AO3):**

- From *The Terrorist at My Table* (Bloodaxe, 2006). Bloodaxe says Dharker "grew up a 'Muslim Calvinist' in a Lahori household in Glasgow, was adopted by India and married into Wales." [Source](https://www.bloodaxebooks.com/ecs/category/imtiaz-dharker)
- The poem mentions "the back of the Koran, where a hand / has written in the names and histories" of a family.

**Best partners:** Ozymandias (fragile human power; the specimen mark scheme lists Tissue under power), The Emigrée (places and identity), Poppies.

**Exam history:** never printed yet.

#### Checking Out Me History — John Agard

**What happens:** The speaker complains that his British-style schooling taught him about 1066, Lord Nelson, Florence Nightingale and nursery-rhyme characters, but never about Black and Caribbean heroes. In between, he celebrates people he was never taught about: Toussaint L'Ouverture, Nanny de Maroon and Mary Seacole. He ends by finding out his own history and "carving out" his own identity.

**Big ideas (AO1):** the power of whoever controls history and education; identity built by reclaiming your own culture; resistance against imposed power; anger turning into pride.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "Dem tell me" | A repeated refrain. "Dem" are faceless authorities; the repetition shows frustration. |
| "Bandage up me eye with me own history" | Metaphor: education "bandaged" his eyes and blinded him. |
| "Blind me to me own identity" | Losing your history means losing your identity. |
| "Toussaint de beacon / of de Haitian Revolution" | Metaphor: a guiding light of freedom, unlike the dull British list. |
| "a healing star / among the wounded" | Mary Seacole as light and hope, against the British-taught "Florence Nightingale and she lamp". |
| "I carving out me identity" | The last line. "carving" is active and permanent: he now takes control. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Caribbean dialect and spelling** ("Dem", "me", "dat"): writing in his own voice rejects the version of English and history forced on him.
- **No punctuation:** this rejects the rules of the imposed system and gives the feel of a spoken performance.
- **Two kinds of section:** the "Dem tell me" lines alternate with set-apart, indented sections celebrating his heroes in images of light and fire ("beacon", "healing star", "fire-woman").
- **Irony:** listing "1066" next to "Dick Whittington and he cat", and Florence Nightingale next to "ole King Cole", treats official history like a nursery rhyme. AQA's 2020 mark scheme names "the use of irony in 'Checking Out Me History'".
- **From passive to active:** it begins "Dem tell me" and ends "I carving out".

**Context (AO3):**

- Agard "was born in Guyana and came to Britain in 1977" (Bloodaxe). [Source](https://www.bloodaxebooks.com/ecs/category/john-agard)
- Guyana was a British colony and became independent on 26 May 1966 (Britannica), so Agard grew up and went to school under British rule. [Source](https://www.britannica.com/place/Guyana/Independence)
- Mary Seacole "was refused a place as an army nurse so funded her own trip to the Crimea" (National Army Museum). [Source](https://www.nam.ac.uk/whats-on/mary-seacole-and-crimean-war)

**Best partners:** London or My Last Duchess (power used to control), The Emigrée (identity), Kamikaze. In 2021 the examiners said it "worked very well" with London.

**Exam history:** never printed yet.

### Nature

#### ★ Storm on the Island — Seamus Heaney

**What happens:** An islander speaks for his community ("we"). He calmly explains that their houses are built low and strong, and that the bare island has no crops or trees to lose. Then a storm arrives: the sea spits at the windows, the wind attacks, and the people can only "sit tight". He ends by saying it is strange that what they fear is "a huge nothing": empty air.

**Big ideas (AO1):**

- Nature's power is greater than human preparation: confidence at the start becomes fear at the end.
- Isolation: no trees, no shelter, no company. The community faces the threat alone.
- Conflict: the storm is described like a military attack.
- Fear of something you cannot see or fight.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "We are prepared:" | A confident, short opening statement, as if ready for an attack. |
| "we build our houses squat," | "squat" (low and solid) shows practical human defence against nature. |
| "This wizened earth" | Personification: the land is old and shrivelled, with nothing to lose. |
| "Can raise a tragic chorus in a gale" | Metaphor from theatre: trees would make the storm sound like a lament, but there are no trees. |
| "spits like a tame cat / Turned savage." | Simile: something familiar becomes violent. The line break mirrors the sudden turn. |
| "strafes invisibly." | Military verb (to strafe is to attack with gunfire from low-flying aircraft): the wind is an unseen enemy. |
| "Space is a salvo," | Metaphor (a salvo is a burst of gunfire) with sibilance: emptiness itself becomes the weapon. |
| "it is a huge nothing that we fear." | Paradox ending: the enemy is invisible. The poem ends on fear, not confidence. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Form:** one 19-line stanza in blank verse (unrhymed lines of about ten syllables). The steady lines sound controlled and "prepared"; breaks like "Blast: you know what I mean" disrupt them as the storm builds.
- **Conversational voice:** "as you see", "You might think", "you know what I mean". He speaks to a listener, which makes the danger feel everyday and lived-with.
- **Language of war:** "Exploding", "strafes", "salvo", "bombarded". AQA's 2021 and 2023 mark schemes both point to the "imagery of war in 'Storm on the Island'".
- **The turn:** "But no:" marks the shift from confidence to the sea's betrayal.
- **Ending:** "Strange, it is a huge nothing that we fear." closes on a paradox.

**Context (AO3):**

- Heaney "was born on 13 April, 1939 in rural County Derry, in Northern Ireland" (the Heaney estate). [Source](https://www.seamusheaney.com/life-and-legacy)
- Use with care: the poem is usually placed in his first collection, *Death of a Naturalist* (1966). The estate confirms that 1966 is when that collection came out, but no reliable page lists this poem in it. [Source](https://www.seamusheaney.com/death-of-a-naturalist)
- Some readers see the war language as hinting at conflict in Northern Ireland. If you use that idea, say "some readers suggest": the book came out in 1966, and Britannica dates the Troubles from "about 1968". [Source](https://www.britannica.com/event/The-Troubles-Northern-Ireland-history)
- Do not use the claim that "Storm" is a pun on Stormont (Northern Ireland's parliament). No reliable source supports it.

**Best partners:** Ozymandias (nature beats human power), Extract from The Prelude (nature frightens a confident human), Exposure (weather as the enemy; "nothing happens" against "a huge nothing"), Remains.

**Exam history:** never printed yet. The 2021 report says some students "struggled" pairing it with London, so use it for questions about nature, fear or conflict, or about human power against nature (as with Ozymandias), rather than questions about political power and control.

#### Extract from The Prelude — William Wordsworth

**What happens:** One summer evening, the young Wordsworth takes a boat that isn't his and rows out on a lake, proud and excited. As he rows, a huge black mountain peak appears from behind a ridge and seems to rise up and follow him. Frightened, he rows back and returns the boat. For days afterwards his mind is troubled by "huge and mighty forms".

**Big ideas (AO1):** nature's power humbles human pride; guilt (he takes the boat without asking); memory and growing up; fear that changes how he sees the world.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "(led by her)" | "her" is Nature, guiding him from the start. |
| "an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure" | Excitement mixed with guilt from the beginning. |
| "She was an elfin pinnace;" | Metaphor: the boat seems magical, showing his childish pride. |
| "a huge peak, black and huge," | Repetition and dark colour: the sudden size and threat of the mountain. |
| "Upreared its head." / "Strode after me." | Personification: the mountain behaves like a giant hunting him. |
| "huge and mighty forms, that do not live / Like living men," | The lasting effect: his peaceful thoughts are replaced by fear and awe. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **First person, looking back:** an adult remembers a childhood event, in blank verse (unrhymed lines of ten syllables). It reads like natural, thoughtful speech.
- **Shift in tone:** calm and proud ("like a swan", "glittering") turns to fear ("With trembling oars") and then troubled reflection.
- **Enjambment:** "I struck and struck again, / And growing still in stature" carries his panic and the peak's growth across the lines.
- **Ending:** it closes on his troubled mind, "a trouble to my dreams", not on the event. The real effect is internal.

**Context (AO3):**

- *The Prelude* is "an autobiographical epic poem in blank verse", "published posthumously in 1850" (Britannica). Its subject is "the narrator's development as a poet". [Source](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Prelude)
- Wordsworth went to school at Hawkshead, "in the heart of the Lake District". *Lyrical Ballads* (1798), written with Coleridge, "helped launch the English Romantic movement" (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Wordsworth)
- AQA's 2023 examiners' report praised essays that understood "the Romantic movement".

**Best partners:** Storm on the Island (nature against human confidence), Ozymandias (human pride against something greater), Kamikaze (nature changes a person's mind), Remains (a memory that troubles the mind).

**Exam history:** never printed yet.

#### Exposure — Wilfred Owen

**What happens:** Soldiers lie out at night in freezing trenches on the Western Front, awake and waiting for an attack that never comes: "But nothing happens." The snow and wind are deadlier than the bullets. They half-dream of home but feel shut out from it. The last stanza predicts that tonight the frost will kill some of them, and a burial party will find their frozen faces.

**Big ideas (AO1):** nature (the weather) is the real enemy; waiting and pointlessness; the suffering of ordinary soldiers; home and faith out of reach.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "Our brains ache, in the merciless iced east winds that knive us" | Personification: the wind attacks like a knife. Sibilance creates the hiss of the wind. |
| "But nothing happens." | The repeated short last line: boredom, waiting and pointlessness. |
| "What are we doing here?" | A rhetorical question: doubt about the purpose of the war. |
| "Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army" | Metaphor: even the dawn is an enemy army. |
| "Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow," | The weather is deadlier than the "flights of bullets". |
| "All their eyes are ice," | Metaphor in the final stanza: the dead are frozen. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Form:** eight five-line stanzas, each ending with a short half-line. Half-rhymes that never quite match ("knive us / nervous") make it feel unsettled.
- **Circular structure:** it ends as it began, with "But nothing happens." Nothing has changed except more men have died.
- **Collective voice:** "we" and "our" speak for all the soldiers; the present tense makes us share the waiting.
- **Sound:** sibilance in "Sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence" copies the hiss of bullets and wind. Ellipses ("…") suggest exhaustion and time dragging.
- **Dream of home:** warm images ("crusted dark-red jewels") end with "on us the doors are closed": home has shut them out.

**Context (AO3):**

- The Wilfred Owen Association says it is "based on Owen's experiences in the winter of 1917", and that "The final version of this poem belongs to September 1918, a few weeks before Owen was killed". [Source](https://www.wilfredowen.org.uk/poetry/exposure)
- Owen was killed on 4 November 1918, "a week before the end of the First World War" (Poetry Archive). [Source](https://poetryarchive.org/poet/wilfred-owen/)

**Best partners:** Storm on the Island (weather as the enemy), Remains and Bayonet Charge (soldiers' suffering), The Charge of the Light Brigade (duty, questioned or celebrated).

**Exam history:** printed in June 2025, on "ideas about conflict".

### Identity, home and loss

#### Poppies — Jane Weir

**What happens:** A mother remembers the morning her son left home for the army. She pins a poppy on his blazer, smooths his collar and holds back her tears. After he has gone, she goes into his empty bedroom, then follows a dove to the war memorial at the top of the hill, where she traces the names and listens for his voice. The poem never says he has died, but the setting strongly suggests loss.

**Big ideas (AO1):** a parent's grief and fear when a child goes to war; the home front side of conflict (the families left at home); memory of childhood; public remembrance against private love.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "spasms of paper red" | The poppy's petals are like a body in pain: remembrance hints at injury. |
| "Sellotape bandaged around my hand" | A medical word in a home setting: war's language invades family life. |
| "steeled the softening / of my face" | Hard against soft: she forces herself to hide her feelings. |
| "All my words / flattened, rolled, turned into felt," | Textile metaphor: her words fail and go soft and silent. |
| "released a song bird from its cage." | Symbol of letting her son go, or of his freedom or soul. It can be read more than one way. |
| "leaned against it like a wishbone." | Simile: fragile and about to snap; a wish for his safety that may not come true. |
| "your playground voice catching on the wind." | The ending: she can only hear the child he once was. No closure. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **Dramatic monologue:** the mother speaks directly to "you", her son, who never answers. His silence adds to the sense of loss.
- **Free verse in four stanzas of unequal length:** no fixed pattern, like grief that cannot be contained.
- **Sewing words:** "bias binding", "felt", "tucks, darts, pleats", "ornamental stitch". She processes war through the language of home.
- **Time jumps:** the present, the morning he left, and his childhood are mixed, as memory loops in grief.

**Context (AO3):**

- In July 2009 the Poet Laureate Carol Ann Duffy commissioned war poems for the Guardian, at a time when "the conflict in Afghanistan" was "escalating" (Wilfred Owen Association). [Source](http://www.wilfredowen.org.uk/news/article/exit-wounds)
- Use with care (one source only): Pearson's 2020 mark scheme says Poppies is from that collection, *Exit Wounds*. [Source](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/GCSE/English%20Literature/2015/Exam-materials/1ET0_02_msc_20210211.pdf)
- Weir has worked in "textile design, fashion and costume", which helps explain the sewing imagery (Writing East Midlands). [Source](https://writingeastmidlands.co.uk/writers-directory/jane-weir/)

**Best partners:** Remains (war's effects long after), Kamikaze (a family's view of a soldier), War Photographer, The Charge of the Light Brigade (public glory against private grief).

**Exam history:** never printed yet. The 2021 report says some students "struggled" using it with London.

#### The Emigrée — Carol Rumens

**What happens:** The speaker left her home country as a child and cannot go back. She hears bad news about it, and it may now be at war, ruled by tyrants, with her language banned, but her childhood memory stays bright and full of sunlight. In the last stanza her city "comes to" her, and she protects it while people in her new city accuse and threaten her.

**Big ideas (AO1):** memory is stronger than bad news; language and identity; exile and not belonging; conflict and tyranny in the background.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "There once was a country…" | A fairy-tale opening: the homeland is like a story, possibly lost. |
| "my memory of it is sunlight-clear" | Light imagery: her memory is vivid and perfect. |
| "the bright, filled paperweight." | Metaphor: a sealed, perfect snapshot, beautiful but frozen. |
| "It may be at war, it may be sick with tyrants," | Repetition of "may": she admits things may have gone wrong but will not let it change her memory. |
| "I am branded by an impression of sunlight." | "branded" means burned in: the memory is a permanent part of her identity. |
| "as time rolls its tanks" | Metaphor: time becomes a military invasion; conflict pushes into her memories. |
| "They accuse me of being dark in their free city." | Irony in "free": in her new home she is an outsider, not free. |
| "my shadow falls as evidence of sunlight." | The last line: even her shadow proves the light of her memory. A defiant ending. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **First person:** a personal voice makes exile feel real. The country is never named, so it could be anywhere.
- **Three stanzas, each ending with "sunlight":** the memory keeps returning, whatever happens.
- **Light against dark:** sunlight images against war, tanks and "the city / of walls".
- **Shift in the last stanza:** repeated "They" ("They accuse me… they circle me") builds menace in the present.

**Context (AO3):**

- AQA's anthology credits the poem as being from *Thinking of Skins*. Rumens was born in Forest Hill, South London, and "has a particular interest in the literary cultures of Central and Eastern Europe" (Poetry Archive). [Source](https://poetryarchive.org/poet/carol-rumens/)

**Best partners:** Checking Out Me History (identity), Tissue (places and borders), Remains (a memory that will not leave, but dark not sunlit), Kamikaze.

**Exam history:** never printed yet.

#### Kamikaze — Beatrice Garland

**What happens:** A woman tells her children about her father, a Japanese kamikaze (suicide) pilot in the Second World War. Halfway to his target he looks down at the fishing boats and the sea, remembers his childhood, and turns back. When he returns, his wife never speaks to him again, the neighbours treat him "as though he no longer existed", and finally even his children learn to act as if he had never come back.

**Big ideas (AO1):** duty and honour against the choice to live; nature and memory change a mind; conflict affects families across generations; rejection as a kind of death.

**Key quotations:**

| Quotation | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| "a shaven head / full of powerful incantations" | He has been filled with the ideas of duty, like a ritual. |
| "a one-way / journey into history" | The line break acts out the one-way journey; suicide is dressed up as glory. |
| "little fishing boats / strung out like bunting" | Simile: a happy, homely image of life pulls him back. |
| "the dark shoals of fishes / flashing silver" | Sibilance: nature is beautiful and alive, unlike his mission. |
| "my mother never spoke again / in his presence" | The switch to "my" makes it personal: the family's punishment is silence. |
| "they treated him / as though he no longer existed," | Rejection is a living death. |
| "which had been the better way to die." | The bitter last line: choosing life led to a different kind of death. |

**Methods (AO2):**

- **A story told second-hand:** the daughter tells it to her own children, mostly in the third person, then switches to "my mother" and "we children". The pilot never speaks for himself.
- **Regular form:** seven six-line stanzas, but no set rhyme or rhythm.
- **Very few sentences:** only three. The long first sentence flows like his flight and memories; the short final one lands hard.
- **Contrast:** rich, colourful nature imagery in the middle, then plain, bare language for his rejection.

**Context (AO3):**

- Kamikaze pilots were Japanese pilots "who in World War II made deliberate suicidal crashes into enemy targets, usually ships". The word means "divine wind" (Britannica). [Source](https://www.britannica.com/topic/kamikaze)
- AQA's anthology credits the poem as being from *The Invention of Fireworks*.

**Best partners:** Remains (living with a choice made in conflict), Poppies (a family member's view), The Charge of the Light Brigade or Bayonet Charge (duty questioned), Storm on the Island (the power of nature).

**Exam history:** printed in June 2024, on "the ways people are affected by conflict".

## What examiners say

The examiners' message is consistent: **answer the question's idea, choose your second poem carefully, and link every method to meaning.** Four reports are public: Nov 2020, Nov 2021, June 2022 and June 2023. Everything in quotation marks below is copied from them.

**Do this**

1. **Start with a short answer to the question.** "Students who began their answer with a thesis statement that they could develop / explain / illustrate during their response, and perhaps return to at the end, tended to be more successful with AO1." (Nov 2020)
2. **Pick the partner poem that fits the question, not just the one you know.** "Think about which one would be best rather than simply choosing one you know well" (June 2022). A weak second poem was "the problem in this exam" in Nov 2021.
3. **Simple structure is fine.** "You can get just as many marks by offering an introduction that shows your understanding of the connection between the named poem and the one you have chosen to use, then dealing with each poem separately, then perhaps drawing the two poems together at the end of your response." (June 2023)
4. **Short quotations beat long ones.** "A well-selected word or phrase from the text is often much more effective than a long direct quotation." (June 2023)
5. **For structure, talk about openings and endings.** "perhaps consider beginnings and endings of the text rather than trying to talk about particular structural features such enjambment / caesura / rhyme schemes if you have nothing useful to say about them." (June 2023)
6. **Context = the ideas in the question.** "The focus for AO3 will be in the question – just talk about that. Know the what, the how and the why." (June 2023)
7. **Think wider than the story.** In 2020 "some of the best responses took a wider approach to the task focus". In 2023 the report praised students who used the poems "as starting-points for discussion of wider ideas".

**Avoid this**

- **Writing mostly about the printed poem.** In Nov 2020, some students "only responded to the named poem".
- **Naming techniques without meaning.** "a mere observation of the techniques or even a vague comment about 'flow' and 'pace' will not achieve above Level 2 AO2 if it is not tied to meaning." (June 2022)
- **Rhyme-scheme spotting.** "the observation of a rhyme scheme \[ABAB\] is best avoided." (June 2022)
- **Bolted-on history.** "paragraphs about, for example, Owen's war experiences, Hughes' father as a soldier or extraneous information about Guardsman Tromans were not useful if they meant the student lost sight of the poem." (June 2022)
- **Formula paragraphs.** These "tended to become self-contained 'mini-essays'" and "encourage bolted-on context". (Nov 2020)
- **Empty form comments.** Writing that Shelley "uses a sonnet because sonnets are love poems which shows Ozymandias loves himself" leads nowhere. (June 2023)
- **Misreading My Last Duchess.** Some thought the Duke "is grieving the loss of his first wife, which means they missed the point of the poem entirely." (June 2023)

**Reassurance from the examiners**

- "Don't worry if you can't remember exact quotations. It's fine to pinpoint parts of a poem and it's also understandable if you slightly misquote because the examiner will get the idea." (June 2022)
- "You don't need to label parts of speech in order to do well. If you want to talk about the effect of a particular word choice, just use the word 'word'!" (June 2023)

**Partner poems the examiners praised:** for London (2021): Ozymandias ("an excellent choice"), Checking Out Me History and My Last Duchess. For Bayonet Charge (2022): Remains, Exposure, The Charge of the Light Brigade and Kamikaze "all of which worked well". For My Last Duchess (2023): Ozymandias and London were most popular; Checking Out Me History, Kamikaze, The Prelude and Storm on the Island were "really interesting" choices. In 2021, some students "struggled" with Storm on the Island and Poppies as partners for London.

The June 2017, 2018, 2019, 2024 and 2025 reports are not on AQA's public website.

## Student answers and examiner commentary

We found only **one** public AQA example Q26 answer with examiner comments. AQA may share real marked scripts with teachers through its secure site, but we could not check this.

The example is in AQA's guide [GCSE English Literature Assessment](https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/resources/english/AQA-8702-EX-COMMENTARY.PDF). It answers the specimen question: "Compare the ways poets present ideas about power in Ozymandias and in one other poem from 'Power and conflict'." It pairs Ozymandias with My Last Duchess. AQA calls it an "Exemplar response". It does not say a real student wrote it, and it gives no mark.

**How it opens:** "Both Ozymandias and My Last Duchess show the effects of power and how it corrupts. Shelley describes Ozymandias' 'sneer of cold command' as if to suggest that he is a cruel and heartless leader, only concerned with his own power and the immortality it will bring."

Notice the pattern: the first sentence answers the question for **both** poems at once. Then a short quotation is used to make a point about the ruler's character.

**The examiner's margin notes, in order:**

1. "AO1: clear comparative approach, understanding of ideas."
2. "AO2: understanding of effects of form on meaning with relevant use of terminology."
3. "AO1/AO3: direct references linked to interpretation, understanding of contextual ideas."
4. "AO2: reference to language choice/imagery, relevant use of terminology."
5. "AO1: developed comparison."
6. "AO1/AO2/AO3: evaluative comparison of ideas linked to context with reference to form."

**The examiner's summary:** "A thoughtful comparison which becomes exploratory as it develops. The student holds both poems together which enables a considered comparison to be made as to the extent to which both poems deal with ideas about power. By the end, this comparison becomes evaluative as there is a judgement made as to which poem demonstrates the effects of power more strongly."

**What to copy from it:**

- Answer the question in your first sentence, for both poems.
- Keep both poems in play, rather than writing two separate essays.
- End with a judgement, for example which poem shows the idea more strongly, and why.

The words "thoughtful" and "exploratory" are the Level 5 and Level 6 words in the mark scheme. So it looks like an answer moving from Level 5 into Level 6, but that is our reading: AQA gives no mark.
