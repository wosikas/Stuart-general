## 3. The story behind the poem (Armitage's own words, OAK-V video transcript)

All USE WITH CARE as single source, but these are the poet's own words on video, hosted by a government-funded body: strong.

| # | Quote | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | "I only got about six doors down the road to a rented house about quarter of a mile from here, actually. And my mum came along to help me measure up for carpets and curtains, and she'd brought with her a tape measure" | The real event; leaving home was a small physical distance but a big step. |
| 3.2 | "It's a rite of passage poem. It's a poem about leaving home. It's a poem about growing up." | **growing up** (matches AQA's June 2017 focus) |
| 3.3 | "It was a highly ritualised day full of significance and meaning, on reflection." / rites of passage "often happen in more metaphorical ways." / "There was still a certain amount of uncertainty around it." | memory; ordinary moment = big meaning |
| 3.4 | "The best line in 'Mother, any distance,' if I'm allowed to choose a best line in my own poem, are those two one-word sentences next to each other, 'Anchor. Kite.'" and "I like the economy of those two words on their own." | AO2: Anchor. Kite. |
| 3.5 | "Is my mother the anchor? And if she is, is that a good thing? Does it mean that I'm safe and secure through being tied to her? Or is it something that's stopping me move on? Am I the kite? If so, is that a good thing 'cause I'm flying in the air? Or am I still tied to her, and is she still controlling where I can go and what I can do?" | Ambiguity: support vs constriction (matches MS17 AO3 "freedom, or support, or constriction") |
| 3.6 | "There's quite a lot of space imagery, I think, in 'Mother, any distance,' and I think that's kind of generational." / "the space walking in that poem was a kind of dream of what I might become, which is, you know, part of what the poem is about" | "space-walk" |
| 3.7 | On poems that state feelings too openly: "They're too obvious, and they're too sentimental." (OAK-T2 key point: "Armitage says personal writing must convey a specific emotion.") | Why he shows feeling through objects (tape) not statements: our link |
| 3.8 | "I'm not sure that I would write 'Mother, any distance' now. I think when I wrote that poem, I was still thinking about it from being nearer to it as something that happened." / "it's been on the GCSE exam syllabus for so long that I've become kind of numb to it" | Written close to the event, by a young man |

Note: OAK-T2's key point writes "Kite. Anchor" (reversed). The poem (ANTH line 8) has "Anchor. Kite." and Armitage in the transcript says "'Anchor. Kite.'" Use the poem's order.

## 5. AQA material (from lr/exam_materials_lr.md)
- June 2017 Q25 printed the poem: "Compare how poets present growing up in ‘Mother, any distance’ and in one other poem from ‘Love and relationships’."
- MS17 AO2: "the use of the tape measure to present connection / distance, or use of the home as metaphor for security"; AO3: "Ideas about parenting and how it is presented in ‘Mother, Any Distance’, e.g. ideas about freedom, or support, or constriction"; suggested partners Walking Away, Follower, Before You Were Mine, Eden Rock.
- MS-N20/MS23 AO2: "imagery of flight/freedom in ‘Mother, Any Distance’"; "parental view in ‘Walking Away’ compared with view of child in ‘Mother, Any Distance’".
- ER-N21 (romantic question): "Mother any distance and Walking Away were not really appropriate choices and comparison was difficult to address." ER-J22: Follower, Mother, any distance and Eden Rock "did not usually work as well" for the power of love.
- ER23: "‘Follower’ and ‘Mother, Any Distance’ were by far the most popular choices of second poem".

## 6. PMT leads checked
- PMT: Book of Matches (1993), poems "around the length of a sonnet", match idea: consistent with FAB-BLURB/Oak.
- PMT: "asking his mother to help her measure" (sic) and "the second stanza... pressure to explore independence": fine as reading.
- PMT: tape = umbilical cord: a reading (Oak lists "umbilical cord" as a lesson keyword, so a common reading); label our reading.
- PMT: "We are left on the word ‘fly’ which suggests a positive ending": overstates; the line is "to fall or fly", deliberately open. Armitage's own anchor/kite questions support ambiguity.
- PMT: calling her "mother" = formality/distance: a possible reading only.
- Revision sites' "Book of Matches (2001)": reprint date, not first publication.
