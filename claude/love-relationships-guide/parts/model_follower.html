MODEL ANSWER · Q25 · WRITTEN TO AQA'S TOP LEVEL (26–30 MARKS)

Follower and Mother, any distance

“Compare how poets present feelings about growing up in ‘Follower’ and in one other poem from ‘Love and relationships’.” [30 marks]
AQA, June 2025, Question 25
This answer was written to match AQA's Level 6 descriptors. It has not been marked by AQA, and no one can guarantee a mark, but every part is designed to hit a top-level requirement. About 700 words: a realistic length for 45 minutes.

Where the 30 marks come from

Step 1: the 5-minute plan

  • Pick the partner: the question is about feelings about growing up, so choose Mother, any distance: a son leaving home while his mother holds on. The June 2025 mark scheme names this pairing: “valid comparison of feelings about growing up, such as ‘Mother, Any Distance’”.
  • Big idea (thesis): both poems show a child who depends on a parent, then moves away. Armitage captures the moment of leaving, torn between excitement and fear. Heaney looks back as an adult, and his admiration has turned into guilt and responsibility.
  • Comparison points: 1. the child who needs a parent; 2. the bond stretched by growing up (shadow, tape measure); 3. the endings: “fall or fly” against a father who “will not go away”. Then context and a judgement.

Step 2: the answer, with what earns the marks

Introduction Both Heaney and Armitage present growing up as a change in the bond between a child and a parent: a child who once depended on a parent moves away. However, Armitage captures the moment of leaving home itself, torn between excitement and fear, while Heaney looks back as an adult and finds that his admiration has turned into guilt and responsibility. Growing up may free Armitage's speaker, but Heaney's can never escape his father, which makes Follower the more troubled poem.
AO1
  • Answers the question in the first sentence, using its key words, “growing up”.
  • Names feelings (excitement, fear, guilt), because the question asks about feelings.
  • Ends with a judgement (“more troubled”) to prove later.
Mark scheme (2025), Level 6: “It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task supported by a range of judicious references to the text.” Examiners (2020): students who began with “a thesis statement … tended to be more successful with AO1.”
Paragraph 1 · admiring a parent In Follower, the young child's feeling is pure admiration. Heaney describes his father: “His shoulders globed like a full sail strung”: the simile makes him huge and powerful, like a ship in full wind. The two-word sentence “An expert.” sounds like a child's proud verdict. Yet growing up is only a wish: “I wanted to grow up and plough,”. The past tense of “wanted” hints that the wish never came true. Instead, “All I ever did was follow”, and the boy walks “In his broad shadow round the farm.” The “shadow” suggests protection, but also that the small boy is overshadowed by his father.
AO1AO2
  • Short, precise quotations, built into sentences.
  • Zooms in on single words (“wanted”, “shadow”) and explains what they suggest.
  • Gives two meanings for one image (protection and being overshadowed).
Mark scheme (2025): “valid comparison of admiration for an adult family figure”. Level 6: “a fine-grained and insightful analysis of methods”. Examiners (2023): “A well-selected word or phrase from the text is often much more effective than a long direct quotation.”
Paragraph 2 · the bond is stretched Armitage's speaker also needs a parent at first. Moving into his first home, he admits the job “requires a second pair of hands.” The rule-like wording hides real feeling: he cannot do this alone. The joking exaggeration “the prairies of the floors.” shows how vast his new independence feels. The tape measure becomes the poem's central image: the mother stays at the “zero-end”, the starting point, while the son, “me with the spool of tape”, controls how far the line runs. As he climbs the stairs, the tape is “unreeling” the “years between us.” Distance in metres becomes distance in time. Like Heaney's shadow, the tape both connects and separates parent and child.
AO1AO2
  • Explains an extended image (the tape measure) and what it means, not just its name.
  • Links back to the first poem (shadow and tape), so the comparison runs through the essay.
  • Tracks feelings: need, nerves, distance.
Mark scheme (2025): “use of memory/incidents as symbolic of ideas, such as ‘Before You Were Mine’, or ‘Mother, Any Distance’”. Mark scheme (2017): “the use of the tape measure to present connection / distance”.
Paragraph 3 · Armitage's ending: fear and hope The two one-word sentences “Anchor. Kite.” hold the poem's mixed feelings. An anchor gives safety but also holds a ship still; a kite flies free but is still tied to the ground. Tension builds as he climbs “to breaking point, where something” “has to give;”: the short line itself seems to give way. Yet the mother's “fingertips still pinch” “the last one-hundredth of an inch”: the tiny measurement shows how tenderly she holds on, and how little is left. The poem ends “to fall or fly.” The alliteration balances the two outcomes equally. Growing up is a risk, and Armitage refuses to promise success.
AO1AO2
  • Explores two readings of one image (anchor as safety or as a weight).
  • Structure through the ending, tied to meaning, as the examiners recommend.
  • Comments on a line length only because it creates an effect.
Mark scheme (2025): “any valid treatment of the ways growing up changes feelings and attitudes, such as ‘Mother, Any Distance’”. Examiners (2023): helpful structure points are “openings/endings”.
Paragraph 4 · Heaney's ending: the roles reverse Heaney's ending reverses his opening. The adult judges himself guiltily: “I was a nuisance, tripping, falling,”. Then “But today” turns the poem: “It is my father who keeps stumbling”. The verb “stumbling” echoes “I stumbled in his hob-nailed wake,”, so the roles have swapped exactly: the son leads, and the old father follows. The final words, “Behind me, and will not go away.”, are ambiguous. They could show irritation with an ageing father, or that his father's memory will always stay with him. Unlike Armitage's open sky, Heaney ends trapped: growing up has not freed him, but made him responsible for the man he once followed.
AO1AO2
  • Spots an echo (“stumbled”/“stumbling”) that proves the role reversal.
  • Offers more than one interpretation of the last line, tentatively (“could”).
  • No rhyme-scheme spotting, even though Follower rhymes abab.
Mark scheme (2023): the “more ambiguous tone of ‘Follower’ or ‘Mother, Any Distance’”. Mark scheme (2025): “any valid ideas about responsibility towards others.” Examiners (2022, writing about the Power and conflict question): “the observation of a rhyme scheme [ABAB] is best avoided.”
Paragraph 5 · context linked to ideas These feelings reflect the poets' lives. Heaney grew up on a farm in Northern Ireland but left for school and university, a move he described as one from “the earth of farm labour to the heaven of education”. The boy who wanted to plough became a poet instead, which may explain his guilt. Published in 1966, the poem also records what Britannica calls “a passing way of life”: one local history talk records that by 1958 Northern Ireland had “more tractors than working horses”, so the father's skill was vanishing too. Armitage, by contrast, called his poem “a rite of passage poem. It's a poem about leaving home.” His poem looks forward to a step every young adult takes; Heaney's looks back.
AO3
  • Context explains why the feelings differ (leaving the farm; leaving home), and links to growing up.
  • No history lesson: a few facts, each tied straight back to the poem.
  • Heaney's words come from Britannica one source; the tractor figure from a talk reported by Bangor Historical Society one source; Armitage's words from an Oak National Academy video one source (his own words).
Mark scheme (2025): “ideas about identity and how this changes with growing up” and “ideas about changing attitudes towards parents over time”. Level 6: “specific, detailed links between context/text/task”.
Conclusion · the judgement Both poets present growing up as a loosening of the tie between parent and child. But Armitage presents the moment of leaving with nervous hope: the son is still held, and his future is open. Heaney presents the cost of having grown up: admiration has turned into guilt and responsibility, and the father who once carried him now follows him. That is why Follower is the more complex presentation of growing up: Armitage's speaker may fall or fly, but Heaney's can never leave his father behind.
AO1
  • Returns to the thesis from the introduction.
  • Makes a clear evaluative judgement and gives a reason for it.
  • Short: the conclusion pulls ideas together and adds no new quotations.
Mark scheme (2025), Level 6: “a critical, exploratory, well-structured argument”.

Checklist: why this reaches the top level

  • ✔ One big idea (a thesis) stated at the start and proved all the way through.
  • ✔ Feelings named throughout (admiration, nerves, hope, guilt), because the question asks about feelings.
  • ✔ Short quotations, with individual words explained.
  • ✔ Every technique linked to its effect and to growing up.
  • ✔ Structure through openings and endings, not rhyme schemes.
  • ✔ Context used to explain ideas, briefly, never as a history lesson.
  • ✔ A clear judgement at the end.
