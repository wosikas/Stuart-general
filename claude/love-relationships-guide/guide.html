AWhat the question asks you to do

Read this first. It explains the question in plain English.

On the exam paper, one of the 15 poems is printed in full. The question asks you to compare how poets present an idea (a theme) in that poem and in one other poem that you choose from the 15 Love and Relationships poems. The second poem is not printed, so you write about it from memory.

The question always looks like this:

Compare how poets present [a theme, for example “romantic love”] in [the printed poem] and in one other poem from ‘Love and relationships’. [30 marks]

It is Question 25 on Paper 2. (In Nov 2021 and June 2022, during Covid, it was Question 1 on a separate poetry paper.)

You must write about two poems

If you only write about the printed poem, AQA's mark scheme says your answer can be “capped” at the top of Level 2. That means a maximum of 10 out of 30, however good it is. So always choose a second poem and write about both.

Time and marks

Section B is worth 30 of the 96 marks on Paper 2. AQA doesn't set a time for each section; about 45 minutes is a sensible guide (5 minutes planning, 40 minutes writing). timing is advice, not an AQA rule

Which poems could come up?

All 15 are listed on the exam paper, and any of them can be the printed poem. AQA's specification says students “should study all 15 poems in their chosen cluster and be prepared to write about any of them in the examination.” The paper also lists the other clusters (Power and conflict, and Worlds and lives): you only answer the question on the cluster your school studied.

BWhere the 30 marks come from

AQA's mark scheme splits the marks into three “assessment objectives” (AOs).
AO1 · Ideas and argument
12

A clear argument that answers the question, compares the two poems, and uses well-chosen references.

AO2 · How the poets write
12

Analysis of the poets' words, structure and form, and the effect of their choices.

AO3 · Context
6

The ideas, times and situations behind the poems, linked to the question.

What the top level (26–30 marks) looks like, in AQA's words

“a critical, exploratory, well-structured argument. It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task supported by a range of judicious references to the text. There will be a fine-grained and insightful analysis of methods supported by judicious use of subject terminology. Convincing exploration of themes/ideas/perspectives/contextual factors/interpretations.” AQA mark scheme, June 2025

In plain English: have one big idea that answers the question and keep proving it; choose short, precise quotations; zoom in on individual words and explain their effect; use technical terms only where they help; and link context to the poems' ideas.

Good news: AQA says examiners should “reward any valid interpretations”. The readings in this guide are strong options, not the only right answers.

How to get the AO2 marks (language, structure, form)

  • Explain effects, don't just name techniques. AQA's 2022 report warns that comments on how a poem is built can become “rather descriptive and offering only limited potential to move up the mark scheme because they interrupt a student’s focus on examining words, ideas and meanings.”
  • Zoom in on single words. “You don't need to label parts of speech in order to do well. If you want to talk about the effect of a particular word choice, just use the word ‘word’!” (2023 report)
  • For structure, use openings, endings and turning points. AQA's 2023 report says helpful structure points are “openings/endings, plot development, changes of pace / scene”.
  • Avoid rhyme-scheme spotting. In 2022, comments on Sonnet 29's “ABBA rhyme scheme” were singled out as limiting.
  • A realistic target: 3–4 short quotations analysed closely from each poem, plus one or two structure or form points linked to meaning. our advice

EWhat examiners say

Copied word for word from AQA's examiners' reports (Nov 2020, Nov 2021, June 2022, June 2023).

Do this

  • Start with a thesis. “Students who began their answer with a thesis statement that they could develop / explain / illustrate during their response, and perhaps return to at the end, tended to be more successful with AO1.” (2020)
  • Simple structure is fine. “Those who demonstrate the connection between the poems at the start, then discuss each within the context of this, and draw them together at the end, are often more successful.” (2023)
  • Make the question's idea the centre. AO3 “is much more appropriately addressed by focusing on ideas such as the power of love to make someone obsessive, very unhappy, grief-stricken, unbalanced, etc than writing about, for example, Browning’s father’s attitude, the sonnet written in Portuguese or Browning’s illness.” (2022)
  • Plan around the question. “your plan could list an idea about that for each paragraph so you have a starting point for each paragraph when you begin to write.” (2022)
  • Use your own understanding. “at 16 years old, many students understood the power of love to affect your whole being – it’s a universal thing and so much more interesting than historical facts about the poets.” (2022)

Avoid this

  • A partner from the wrong kind. For a romantic question, “Mother any distance and Walking Away were not really appropriate choices” (2021).
  • Not knowing your second poem. “a proportion of students did not know another poem very well, so detail, and sometimes understanding, were limited.” (2021)
  • Misreading Before You Were Mine. Some students “think that the speaker’s mother has died, which is a shame as it means they can miss the point of the poem.” (2023)
  • Missing the AO3 chance in The Farmer's Bride. “the opportunity to address gender issues as a way to access AO3 was sadly often missed.” (2021)
Reassurance: “Don't worry if you can't remember exact quotations. It's fine to pinpoint parts of a poem and it's also understandable if you slightly misquote because the examiner will get the idea.” (AQA examiners' report, 2022)
POEM 1 OF 15  ·  THEME GROUP: ROMANTIC LOVE

Love’s Philosophy

Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792–1822) · published December 1819
01
Themes (solid = linked by AQA · dashed = our reading)
Romantic love · AQA mark schemes 2019, Nov 2021, 2022, 2024Desire · our readingLonging · our readingNature · our reading (AQA 2022 mark scheme names its “imagery”)Rejection and heartbreak · our reading
Type of poem
Lyric: two 8-line stanzas, rhyme ABABCDCD
In the exam?
Not yet the printed poem; AQA has suggested it as a second poem four times (2019, Nov 2021, 2022, 2024)
Best partner
Porphyria’s Lover (your anchor); Sonnet 29 also works well

The story in short

A man tries to talk someone he loves into kissing him. He points out that everything in nature joins with something else, so why not the two of them?

The message

Love feels like a law of nature, so being kept apart seems unnatural. But the poem ends with an unanswered question: we never find out if she agrees.

Why it was written

Shelley was a Romantic poet, writing in Italy in 1819. The Romantics prized feeling and nature. Britannica

The title

“Philosophy” comes from the Greek for “love of wisdom”. Shelley's title promises a serious, logical argument about love. In fact it is a clever chat-up line dressed up as philosophy, so the title is a little playful. our reading (Britannica; Universidad Complutense Madrid)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupA speaker (an “I”) talks to someone he calls “thou”. We never hear her answer. He builds an argument, like a lawyer, using examples from nature.
  • Lines 1–4Fountains flow into rivers, rivers flow into the ocean, and the winds blend together. Everything in nature joins with something else.
  • Lines 5–7He turns this into a rule: “Nothing in the world is single;”. He claims a “law divine” (a law made by God) makes all things mix together.
  • Line 8So he asks: “Why not I with thine?” If everything joins up, why can't he be with her?
  • Lines 9–12Stanza two gets more physical. Mountains “kiss” the sky and waves “clasp” (hug) each other. A flower that rejected its partner would never be forgiven.
  • Lines 13–14Sunlight hugs the earth and moonlight kisses the sea. Nature is full of kisses, day and night.
  • Lines 15–16His final question: what is all this kissing worth if she won't kiss him?
The turning point: The turn in each stanza: the speaker builds up evidence from nature, then suddenly switches to himself. In stanza one this happens in the last line (line 8); in stanza two, in the last two lines (lines 15–16). How it ends: On a question with no answer. We never hear her reply, so his argument is left hanging. Has it worked? The tone: Playful, flirty and persuasive at first, then more urgent and a little desperate by the end.

2The poem, with what it means

Highlighted words are the ones worth quoting in the exam.
The poem
What it means, in plain English
The fountains mingle with the river
And the rivers with the Ocean,
The winds of Heaven mix for ever
With a sweet emotion;
Water and wind keep joining together. “mingle” = mix. Even the wind has “emotion”: nature is given human feelings (personification).
Nothing in the world is single;
All things by a law divine
In one another's being mingle —
Why not I with thine?
His “rule”: nothing is alone (“single” also hints at not being married). A “law divine” (from God) joins everything. Then the question: why not us?
See the mountains kiss high Heaven,
And the waves clasp one another;
No sister-flower would be forgiven
If it disdain'd its brother:
Now the images are physical: kissing and hugging (“clasp”). “disdain'd” = looked down on, rejected. A flower that rejected its partner would be blamed, and so, he hints, would she.
And the sunlight clasps the earth,
And the moonbeams kiss the sea —
What are all these kisses worth,
If thou kiss not me?
Day (sun) and night (moon): love is everywhere, all the time. Then the blunt final question: all these kisses are worthless to him without hers. “thou” = you.

Learn these word for word. Short quotations score better than long ones.
LINE 1
“The fountains mingle with the river”
Means: water in nature flows together. Why it matters: the first example of things joining; it sets up his whole argument.
LINE 5
“Nothing in the world is single;”
Means: nothing is alone. Technique: a short statement, like a rule; “single” also hints at being unmarried.
LINE 6

Means: God's law makes everything join. Why it matters: he makes his own desire sound holy and unarguable.
LINE 8
“Why not I with thine?”
Means: why can't we be together? Technique: rhetorical question; the first time he mentions himself.
LINE 9

Means: even mountains “kiss” the sky. Technique: personification; the command “See” makes her look.
LINES 11–12

Means: a flower that rejected its partner would be blamed. Why it matters: a hidden warning: saying no would be “unnatural”.
LINES 15–16
“What are all these kisses worth, / If thou kiss not me?”
Means: nature's kisses mean nothing unless she kisses him. Technique: final rhetorical question; short words, no answer.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: a love lyric built like an argument
About the form
  • A lyric is a short poem that expresses one speaker's feelings, often like a song. When the poem first appeared, the editor Leigh Hunt called it a “delightful little lyric” and a “song”. The Indicator, 1819
  • A rhetorical question is a question asked to make a point, not to get information. Persuasive speeches use them to push the listener to agree.
  • One handwritten copy is headed “An Anacreontic”: a poem in the style of Anacreon, an ancient Greek poet whose poems praise “love, wine, and revelry”. Hutchinson's Oxford edition; Britannica heading: one source
What this poem does with it
  • Two stanzas of 8 lines, each rhyming ABABCDCD. our count from the anthology; Universidad Complutense Madrid
  • Each stanza works the same way: evidence from nature first, then a closing question about himself (line 8; lines 15–16). It is like a debate: examples, then the conclusion. our reading
  • The rhymes pair up, “single” with “mingle”, just as he wants the two of them to pair up. Everything in the poem has a partner, except him. our reading
  • Stanza two is more urgent: the verbs move from mixing (“mingle”, “mix”) to touching (“kiss”, “clasp”).
  • The ending is open: the poem stops on a question, so the woman's answer is never heard. The neat form contrasts with the unresolved situation.
A sentence you could use: Shelley shapes the poem like a logical argument, with evidence from nature piled up before each closing question, yet the final question is never answered, so his tidy “philosophy” cannot actually make her love him.
Nature given human feelings

Winds have a “sweet emotion”, mountains “kiss”, waves “clasp”. Making nature romantic makes his own wish look natural too.

Personification: giving human actions or feelings to something that isn't human.
Questions that push

Both stanzas end with a question: “Why not I with thine?” and “If thou kiss not me?” They put pressure on her to answer “yes”.

Rhetorical question: a question asked to make a point or persuade, not to get information.
Building a list

He piles up example after example, linked by “And”: “And the waves”, “And the sunlight”, “And the moonbeams”. The pile of evidence makes his argument feel overwhelming.

Anaphora: repeating the same word or phrase at the start of several lines.
Words of touch

“mingle”, “mix”, “kiss” and “clasp” run through the poem. They show his desire is physical, not just a feeling.

Semantic field: a group of words linked by the same topic.
Examiners warn: in 2022, comments on Sonnet 29’s rhyme scheme were “sometimes compared to the ABAB of Love’s Philosophy and its structure”, and AQA's report said such comments risk “becoming rather descriptive”. Don't just name the rhyme scheme: say what it shows about love.

Who Shelley was

  • A second-generation Romantic poet, with Keats and Byron. Britannica calls him the “most politically radical” of the Romantics. Britannica; Cambridge History of English Poetry
  • Oxford expelled him in 1811 over a pamphlet called The Necessity of Atheism. So it is striking that his love poem calls on a “law divine” and “Heaven”: he borrows God's authority to win an argument. Britannica; University College Oxford romantic love · desire the link is our reading
  • He broke the rules in love too: at 19 he eloped with 16-year-old Harriet Westbrook, then in 1814 eloped with Mary Godwin, whom he married in 1816. Britannica; poets.org romantic love · desire
  • His early poem Queen Mab (1813) attacked several institutions, including marriage. Britannica romantic love one source

How the poem came about

  • First printed on 22 December 1819 in The Indicator, a weekly paper run by his friend Leigh Hunt. Shelley's name wasn't given: Hunt introduced it as a lyric “by a friend”. The Indicator (1819 original); Hutchinson's Oxford edition name missing: as printed in 1819
  • Hunt praised it as “elemental, Platonical; a meeting of divineness with humanity”. In other words, a poem where nature, the holy and human love meet. The Indicator (1819 original) romantic love · nature one source
  • That autumn Shelley was living in Florence, Italy. In Florence he met an English traveller, Sophia Stacey, and later copied this poem by hand into a pocket book he gave her. No source says he wrote it for her. Britannica; Maidstone Museum; Eton College catalogue desire Stacey: one source each
  • After Shelley died, his wife Mary reprinted it in Posthumous Poems (1824). Hutchinson's Oxford edition; Universidad Complutense Madrid
  • Watch out: there are different versions. Some books print “What is all this sweet work worth”. The AQA anthology has “What are all these kisses worth,”. Always quote the anthology. Hutchinson's Oxford edition; The Indicator; AQA anthology

Love, nature and the Romantics

  • Romanticism prized “a deepened appreciation of the beauties of nature” and put emotion above reason. Here nature becomes the evidence for love. Britannica nature · romantic love one source
  • In 1818 Shelley translated Plato's Symposium (an ancient Greek discussion about love) and wrote his own essay “On Love”. Love was something he thought about seriously, as philosophy. Britannica romantic love one source
  • Note the irony: the poem argues like a philosopher, using reason, but its real aim is a kiss. The Romantics' trust in feeling wins over the logic. our reading desire our reading
  • AQA's 2022 report says AO3 works best through ideas such as “the power of love to make someone obsessive, very unhappy”, rather than poets' life stories. AQA 2022 examiners' report powerful feelings
Putting it into an answer (AO3): Shelley, a Romantic who valued feeling and nature above reason, uses nature as “evidence” for love. Yet, as someone expelled from Oxford over atheism, he still calls on a “law divine”, borrowing God's authority to make desire sound unarguable. Link this to the power of love to make someone argue, plead and feel rejected.

7In the exam

A likely question: Compare how poets present powerful feelings about love in ‘Love’s Philosophy’ and in one other poem from ‘Love and relationships’. (AQA's 2024 focus: “powerful feelings about love”)
A strong opening (thesis): Both Shelley and Browning show a man's powerful desire for a silent woman, but Shelley's speaker can only plead with questions, while Browning's turns desire into deadly possession.
A model comparison paragraph:
Both poets present desire as a feeling so powerful that the man believes it is justified by something greater than himself. Shelley's speaker claims “All things by a law divine” must join, so refusing him would be unnatural. Yet his desire stays a request: the rhetorical question “Why not I with thine?” leaves the woman free to say no, and the poem ends unanswered. Browning's speaker also believes he is right, but he acts on his desire. The repetition in “she was mine, mine, fair,” shows possessive obsession, and he strangles her to keep the moment. His closing line, “And yet God has not said a word!”, echoes Shelley's appeal to God, but chillingly. Together the poems suggest that powerful desire can make a lover twist even divine law to suit himself.
Why it scores:
  • Opens with a clear comparative point that answers the question about powerful feelings
  • Short quotations from both poems, each one explained, with methods named (rhetorical question, repetition)
  • Compares all the way through (“Both”, “also”, “echoes”) and ends with an overall idea

The story in short

A woman thinks about the man she loves while he is away. Her thoughts grow round him like wild vines round a tree, until she can hardly see him. She orders him to come back and shake them off.

The message

Love is so powerful it can take over your whole mind. But thinking about someone is no substitute for being with them: the real person is better than any daydream.

Why it was written

Barrett Browning wrote the poem in secret while Robert Browning was courting her (1845–46). It is one of 44 love sonnets published in 1850 as Sonnets from the Portuguese. Britannica; poets.org; Victorian Web

The title

“Sonnet 29” means it is the 29th poem in a sequence of 44 love sonnets. In the original book it has only a number (XXIX); the anthology adds the first words, “I think of thee!”, as a subtitle. anthology point: our reading Watch how the last line answers the title: “I do not think of thee”. The poem travels from thinking about him to being with him. Beware: the sonnets were written in English, not Portuguese. The title Sonnets from the Portuguese was “a ruse to disguise the sonnets' personal nature”; it played on Robert’s pet name for her, “the Portuguese”. (Britannica; Project Gutenberg text)

1What happens, step by step

The poem
What it means, in plain English
I think of thee! – my thoughts do twine and bud
About thee, as wild vines, about a tree,
Put out broad leaves, and soon there’s nought to see
Except the straggling green which hides the wood.
The opening exclamation shows how excited she is. Her thoughts are like wild vines: they “twine” (wind round) and “bud” (grow) round him, the tree. Soon there is nothing to see but the messy (“straggling”) leaves, which hide the “wood” (the tree trunk). Her thoughts are growing so fast that they hide the real man. Love is powerful, but it could smother him.
Yet, O my palm-tree, be it understood
I will not have my thoughts instead of thee
Who art dearer, better! Rather, instantly
“Yet” marks the turn. She now calls him “my palm-tree”: a tall, proud, exotic tree, and “my” shows he belongs to her heart. She makes a firm statement: she will not settle for thoughts “instead of” him, because the real man is “dearer, better!”
Renew thy presence; as a strong tree should,
Rustle thy boughs and set thy trunk all bare,
And let these bands of greenery which insphere thee
Drop heavily down, – burst, shattered, everywhere!
A string of orders: come back (“Renew thy presence”), shake your branches (“boughs”), strip your trunk bare. The vines are now “bands”, like ropes, that “insphere” (wrap all round) him. She wants them to fall and break apart. The violent verbs “burst, shattered” show her thoughts being destroyed so the real man can be free.
Because, in this deep joy to see and hear thee
And breathe within thy shadow a new air,
I do not think of thee – I am too near thee.
“Because” gives her reason. When he is with her, she uses her senses (see, hear, breathe), not just her mind. Standing in his “shadow” she breathes “a new air”: love gives her fresh life. The last line answers the first: she no longer needs to think of him, because she is “too near”.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

The form: a Petrarchan sonnet with an early turn
About the form
  • A sonnet is a 14-line poem, usually in iambic pentameter (ten syllables, five beats per line). Britannica; poets.org; Poetry Archive
  • The Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet has an octave (8 lines) rhyming ABBAABBA and a sestet (6 lines). The octave sets up a problem; the sestet answers it. Britannica; Poetry Archive; poets.org
  • The volta (turn) is a shift in thought, “often indicated by such initial words as But, Yet, or And yet”. It usually comes between lines 8 and 9. Britannica; poets.org
  • Traditionally a man wrote the sonnets to an idealised woman: Petrarch wrote “317 sonnets, addressed to his idealized beloved, Laura”. Barrett Browning’s sequence revived the love sonnet in the 19th century. Britannica
What this poem does with it
  • Strict rhyme: the octave rhymes ABBAABBA (bud, tree, see, wood …) and the sestet CDCDCD (bare, thee, everywhere …). It follows the Petrarchan pattern exactly. our count from the anthology
  • An early turn: the volta comes at line 5, with “Yet”, not at line 9. The vines take only four lines to grow too thick; she reacts at once. our reading; many students wrote about “the unusual placing of the volta” (AQA June 2022 examiners' report)
  • Spilling over: from “Rather” in line 7, one long sentence runs across the octave–sestet break to line 11. Her feelings will not stay inside the form’s neat boxes. our reading
  • A woman in charge: here the woman is the lover and speaker, and the man is the beloved (the tree). A student essay on the Victorian Web, quoting a critic, says writing love sonnets “from the female perspective” was seen as “not the sphere of a Victorian woman”. Victorian Web one source; link to this poem is our reading
  • The ending circles back: three of the sestet’s six lines end on “thee” (“insphere thee”, “hear thee”, “near thee”). The poem keeps landing on him, just as her mind does. our reading
  • Keep form linked to meaning: after comments on “the ABBA rhyme scheme”, examiners warn that comments on poetic construction can become “rather descriptive” and interrupt “a student’s focus on examining words, ideas and meanings”. AQA June 2022 examiners' report
A sentence you could use: Barrett Browning uses the traditional love sonnet but turns it early, at line 5 with “Yet”, as if the power of her love is too urgent to wait for line 9; the woman, not the man, is the one who speaks and commands.
The vine and the tree

The whole octave is one picture: her thoughts are “wild vines” and he is the tree. It shows love as alive and growing, but also clinging and smothering. Most students in 2022 wrote about this image.

Extended metaphor: a comparison that is kept going over several lines or a whole poem.
Exclamations

“I think of thee!”, “dearer, better!”, “shattered, everywhere!” The exclamation marks show how excited and overwhelmed she is. AQA’s mark scheme points to the “exclamatory language” of this poem.

Exclamatory language: sentences ending in “!” that show strong feeling.
Giving orders

“Renew thy presence”, “Rustle thy boughs”, “set thy trunk all bare”. A woman commanding a man was unusual for the time. Love gives her power and confidence.

Imperatives: command verbs that tell someone what to do.
Thee, thee, thee

She addresses him directly all the way through. “thee” comes in the first line’s exclamation and is the poem’s very last word; three lines of the sestet end on it. He is at the centre of everything she says, which shows how love fills her whole mind.

Direct address: speaking straight to someone, using “you” (here the old form “thee”).

The story in short

On a stormy night a woman called Porphyria leaves a party to visit her lover in his cottage. She tells him she loves him. He strangles her with her own hair, then sits with her body all night.

The message

Love can turn into a terrifying wish to own someone. The speaker wants to keep one perfect moment for ever, so he destroys the person he loves.

Why it was written

Browning was a Victorian poet famous for the dramatic monologue. This is one of his earliest: first printed in 1836, then paired with another poem under the title “Madhouse Cells”. Britannica; EBSCO; Encyclopedia.com (Gale)

The title

The poem first appeared simply as “Porphyria”. In 1842 it was printed under the heading “Madhouse Cells”, telling readers the speaker was mad. The title “Porphyria’s Lover” was added in 1849. It names him only by his link to her: he exists through owning her. last point: our reading The name comes from the Greek word for purple, a colour linked with royalty, which may hint that she is from a richer, higher-class world. one source Beware: some revision notes say she is named after the disease porphyria. That is wrong: the medical term was not coined until 1889, 53 years after the poem. (EBSCO; Encyclopedia.com (Gale); Victorian Web)

The poem
What it means, in plain English
The rain set early in to-night,
The sullen wind was soon awake,
It tore the elm-tops down for spite,
and did its worst to vex the lake:
I listened with heart fit to break.
A storm. The wind is described like a bad-tempered person: “sullen”, acting “for spite”, trying to “vex” (annoy) the lake. The angry weather mirrors the speaker’s mood (pathetic fallacy). He is sitting alone, heartbroken.
When glided in Porphyria; straight
She shut the cold out and the storm,
And kneeled and made the cheerless grate
Blaze up, and all the cottage warm;
Which done, she rose, and from her form
Porphyria arrives smoothly, almost like a ghost or angel (“glided”). She shuts out the storm and lights the fire. She brings warmth and life; she is the active one.
Withdrew the dripping cloak and shawl,
And laid her soiled gloves by, untied
Her hat and let the damp hair fall,
And, last, she sat down by my side
And called me. When no voice replied,
She takes off her wet things and lets her hair down. She calls to him, and he deliberately says nothing. His sulky silence is a first sign that something is wrong.
She put my arm about her waist,
And made her smooth white shoulder bare,
And all her yellow hair displaced,
And, stooping, made my cheek lie there,
And spread o’er all her yellow hair,
She does everything: puts his arm round her, bares her shoulder, rests his head on it. He is passive. Notice how often he mentions her “yellow hair”: it becomes the murder weapon.
Murmuring how she loved me – she
Too weak, for all her heart’s endeavour,
To set its struggling passion free
From pride, and vainer ties dissever,
And give herself to me for ever.
She says she loves him. But (he says) she is too weak to cut (“dissever”) her ties: her pride and her other connections, perhaps family or social class. She will not give herself to him “for ever”. This is his complaint.
But passion sometimes would prevail,
Nor could tonight’s gay feast restrain
A sudden thought of one so pale
For love of her, and all in vain:
So, she was come through wind and rain.
Tonight her passion won. She left a party (“gay feast” = happy celebration) to see him, pale with hopeless love. She braved the storm for him. Note: he says his love was “all in vain”, yet she came.
Be sure I looked up at her eyes
Happy and proud; at last I knew
Porphyria worshipped me: surprise
Made my heart swell, and still it grew
While I debated what to do.
He looks into her eyes and decides she “worshipped” him: love like a god receives. His pride swells. Then, worryingly, he wonders “what to do” with this moment.
That moment she was mine, mine, fair,
Perfectly pure and good: I found
A thing to do, and all her hair
In one long yellow string I wound
Three times her little throat around,
At this perfect moment she is “mine, mine”. To stop it changing, he winds her hair round her throat three times. Her hair is now just a “string”, and the word “little” makes her sound small and helpless.
And strangled her. No pain felt she;
I am quite sure she felt no pain.
As a shut bud that holds a bee,
I warily oped her lids: again
Laughed the blue eyes without a stain.
Four blunt words: “And strangled her.” He repeats that she felt no pain, as if trying to convince himself. He opens her eyelids carefully (“oped” = opened), like opening a flower bud with a bee inside, and claims her eyes still “Laughed”.
And I untightened next the tress
About her neck; her cheek once more
Blushed bright beneath my burning kiss:
I propped her head up as before,
Only, this time my shoulder bore
He loosens the hair (“tress”) and kisses her, imagining she blushes. Then he swaps places: before, he rested on her shoulder; now her dead head rests on his. He is in control now.
Her head, which droops upon it still:
The smiling rosy little head,
So glad it has its utmost will,
That all it scorned at once is fled,
And I, its love, am gained instead!
He calls her “it”: she is now an object. He claims she is “glad”: she has got her greatest wish, because the things she cared about (pride, other ties) are gone and she has him.
Porphyria’s love: she guessed not how
Her darling one wish would be heard.
And thus we sit together now,
And all night long we have not stirred,
And yet God has not said a word!
He says he granted her “one wish”, as if he were God. Only now does he say “we”: they are together, but only because she is dead. Last line: no punishment has come, and he sounds triumphant, or perhaps uneasy.

The form: a dramatic monologue that hides its own pattern
About the form
  • A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by one character who is not the poet, “in a specific situation at a critical moment.” Baylor University (M. H. Abrams)
  • Why poets use it: to reveal the speaker’s character, often more than the speaker means to. So we read between the lines. Baylor University (M. H. Abrams)
  • “First-person narration provides no sure way of authenticating the speaker’s statements.” In other words, we cannot check whether he is telling the truth. Victorian Web (George P. Landow, Brown University)

The story in short

A farmer tells how he married a very young girl three years ago. She became terrified of him and ran away. The men of the village hunted her down and locked her in. Now she lives in his house but will not let him near her, and he longs for her.

The message

A marriage without love or understanding traps both people. She has no voice and no way out; he has power over her but cannot make her love him, and his desire is building.

Why it was written

Charlotte Mew was an English poet known for dramatic monologues. This poem was first printed in the magazine The Nation in 1912 or 1913 (sources disagree), then gave its name to her first book (1916). EBSCO; Britannica; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)

The title

She is never named. The title calls her only “The Farmer’s Bride”: the apostrophe shows she belongs to him. our reading She is still a bride, not a wife, three years on. One critic says the last stanza implies “they are not sexually intimate”. one source So the marriage has never really begun. our reading (EBSCO (Sandra J. Holstein))

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe whole poem is spoken by the farmer, in his country dialect. He tells the story in the past tense, then switches to the present. His bride never gets to tell her side.
  • Lines 1–3Three summers ago he “chose a maid”, perhaps “Too young”. He was too busy with the harvest to “bide and woo” (wait and court her).
  • Lines 4–9After the wedding she became “afraid / Of love and me and all things human”. Her smile went out like a winter day ending. One autumn night “she runned away.”
  • Lines 10–19Villagers say she is out among the sheep. The men chase her across the fields by lantern-light, “flying like a hare”. They catch her, bring her home and lock her in: “turned the key upon her, fast.”
  • Lines 20–29Now (present tense) she does the housework “like a mouse”. She is happy with birds and animals, as long as men keep away. Her eyes beg “Not near, not near!” He has “hardly heard her speak at all.”
  • Lines 30–33He describes her beauty with nature similes: a young hare, a larch tree, violets. She is sweet “To her wild self. But what to me?”
  • Lines 34–41Winter closes in: brown oaks, a grey sky, frost. Christmas is coming, and he wishes there were “Some other in the house than we!” (a child).
  • Lines 42–46She sleeps alone in the attic. “’Tis but a stair / Betwixt us.” His desire bursts out: “Oh! my God!” He ends repeating “her eyes, her hair, her hair!”
The turning point (line 20): “She does the work about the house”. The poem moves from past to present tense. The chase is over, but the story is not: she is trapped, and he is stuck waiting just below her. How it ends: Nothing is solved. Only a stair separates them, and his broken, exclamatory last lines suggest his desire is close to breaking out. The poem stops on that tension, which is what makes it so uneasy. The tone: Plain and matter-of-fact at first, almost puzzled. It grows lonelier and more frustrated, ending in a rush of desperate longing.

About the form
  • A dramatic monologue is a poem spoken by one character who is not the poet, at a critical moment. The speaker reveals more about himself than he means to. Baylor University (M. H. Abrams)
  • This poem is classed as a dramatic monologue, and Mew was known for them. EBSCO; Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)

A model comparison paragraph:
Both poets show strong feelings becoming a desire to own a woman. Mew’s farmer “chose a maid”, and when she flees he hunts her “flying like a hare” and “turned the key upon her, fast.” The simile turns his bride into prey, and the lock shows his power over her. Browning’s speaker goes further: “That moment she was mine, mine, fair,” where the repeated possessive shows love becoming ownership, just before he strangles her. The story in short

A young Sikh man runs one of his father's shops. Whenever the shop is empty, he locks up and sneaks upstairs to his new wife. Customers complain that it is the worst shop on the road. At midnight the couple sit together in the empty shop and look at the moon.

The message

New love makes everything else, even work and family duty, seem unimportant. The bride is rebellious and mixes two cultures, and her husband loves her for it. Their love is “priceless”.

Why it was written

Nagra writes in “Punglish”, the Punjabi-flavoured English “of his parents' generation”. His parents ran a shop in Sheffield, and he said he wanted to write this poem “as an Indian Music Hall song.” Poetry International; South Asian Britain (University of Bristol / British Library)

The title

Say it aloud: Singh Song sounds like “sing-song”, a light, bouncy tune. That pun is our reading, but AQA's 2018 mark scheme points to “the use of ‘song’ and the exclamation mark, to suggest a positive, happy view of love”. Singh is also the speaker's name: Britannica explains that Sikh men of the Khalsa (the Sikh community) carry Singh (“lion”) as their last name. (AQA mark scheme 2018; Britannica (Khalsa, one source); the pun is our reading)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker is a newly married young Sikh man who runs one of his father's shops in Britain. He tells us about his day, his new bride and the grumpy customers. He speaks in a Punjabi-English dialect, spelt as it sounds (for example “ov” for of, “vee” for we, “di” for the).
  • Lines 1–4He works in his father's shop from nine in the morning to nine at night. His father doesn't want him to take a break, but whenever the shop is empty he locks the door.
  • Lines 5–9This is because his new bride is upstairs. They share chapattis and chutney and make love, which he compares, comically, to rowing through Putney.
  • Lines 10–17When he comes back down with his apron untied, the customers complain: the lemons are limes, the bananas are plantain, the floor is dirty. They chant that it is the worst Indian shop on the whole Indian road.
  • Lines 18–34Upstairs he hears his wife's high heels as she plays on the internet, on a Sikh dating site (the wordplay here is deliberately confusing). Then come three short stanzas that each begin “my bride”: she swears at his mum in Punjabi, makes fun of his dad, has a gun-like stare and a teddy-bear tummy, a red crew cut, a tartan sari and a donkey jacket.
  • Lines 35–42The refrain returns. He comes back from tickling his bride, and the shoppers complain again: out-of-date milk, stale bread, special offers never in stock. Once more: the worst Indian shop.
  • Lines 43–50Late at night, when the customers are asleep and the shopping precinct is quiet and cool, the couple creep downstairs. They sit on his silver stool behind the chocolate bars and look past the half-price signs at the moonlit UK.
  • Lines 51–58Four two-line stanzas of dialogue. Each night she asks what he would charge for the moon; he says it is half the cost of her. She asks how much that comes to; he answers that she is “priceless”.
The turning point (line 43): “Late in di midnight hour”. The noise of the shop and the customers stops. The busy, comic poem slows down into a quiet, romantic scene, and for the first time the bride speaks. How it ends: On a joke about prices that becomes a declaration of love. In a shop where everything has a price, his wife is worth more than anything: “Is priceless baby”. The tone: Comic, cheeky and fast in the shop scenes; tender, quiet and romantic in the moonlit ending.

Key words to quote
What it means, in plain English
Lines 1–4I run just one ov my daddy’s shops … ven nobody in, I do di lock
He runs the shop for his father (“daddy”), twelve hours a day. His father wants no breaks. The word “just” makes the job sound small. But he breaks the rule: when the shop is empty, he locks up.

The reason: his new wife is upstairs. “Share” is repeated: they do everything together. The simile comparing lovemaking to rowing through an English place is silly and funny, and it mixes Indian food with an English setting.
Lines 10–17Hey Singh, ver yoo bin? … in di worst Indian shop … on di whole Indian road
He comes back with his “pinnie” (apron) untied, a hint of what he has been doing. The customers moan in a chant, like the chorus of a song. Their complaints are comic exaggerations (hyperbole).
Lines 18–21high heel tap di ground … her Sikh lover site
He hears his wife's high heels above him while she is online, on a Sikh dating or chat site. The wording is full of deliberate mishearings, so it is hard to pin down exactly what she is doing; she is clearly independent and modern.
Lines 22–34she effing at my mum … tiny eyes ov a gun … di tummy ov a teddy … she wear a Tartan sari
Three stanzas begin “my bride”. She swears (“effing”) at his mum and mocks his dad: not the obedient daughter-in-law his family might expect. She is fierce (“gun”) and cuddly (“teddy”). Her clothes mix cultures: a tartan (Scottish) sari (Indian), a donkey jacket (a British workman's coat) and pumps. He clearly adores all of it.
Lines 35–42Ven I return from di tickle ov my bride … Di milk is out ov date
The refrain comes back with new complaints: old milk, stale bread, offers never in stock. The shop is failing because he cares more about his wife than his work.
Lines 43–50Late in di midnight hour … sit on my silver stool … at di beaches ov di UK in di brightey moon
The mood changes. At midnight the precinct (shopping area) is “concrete-cool” and the stairs are “whispering”. The couple sit together in the empty shop and look out past the half-price signs at the moonlit UK. The ordinary shop becomes a romantic place.
Lines 51–58How much do yoo charge for dat moon baby? … Is half di cost ov yoo baby … Is priceless baby
A little dialogue, repeated “each night”: a loving game. She asks the price of the moon, as if it were stock. He says the moon is worth half of her. When she asks what that comes to, he says she is “priceless”: love can't be bought.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: a comic song in one man's voice
About the form
  • A dramatic monologue is “a poem written in the form of a speech of an individual character”. The form is “chiefly associated with Robert Browning”, who wrote Porphyria's Lover. Britannica
  • A refrain is a “phrase, line, or group of lines repeated at intervals throughout a poem, generally at the end of the stanza.” It works like the chorus of a song. Britannica
  • Nagra said he wanted to write this poem “as an Indian Music Hall song.” (Music hall was a kind of popular comic stage show with songs.) Poetry International one source
  • Dialect: the way a group of people really speak. Nagra plays with the “‘Punglish’ dialect of his parents' generation” and its “convoluted syntax” (unusual word order). Poetry International; Bloodaxe Books; South Asian Britain
What this poem does with it
  • 58 lines in 13 stanzas of different lengths (from eight lines down to two). There is no regular rhyme scheme, though rhymes and half-rhymes pop up like song lyrics (“o’clock” / “lock”, “chutney” / “Putney”). our count
  • Refrains: the shoppers' chant (lines 10–17 and 35–42) and the words “my bride” (lines 22, 27, 30) repeat like a chorus. The poem is built like the song in its title. our reading
  • Dialect spelling: “ov”, “vee”, “di” make us hear the speaker's real voice. He is not mocked: he is the hero of his own love song. AQA's mark schemes name “dialect in ‘Singh Song!’” as a method to discuss.
  • The ending changes shape: after long, noisy stanzas, the last eight lines are four calm two-line stanzas of question and answer, repeated “each night”. Love settles into a gentle routine, like a couple talking in turn. our reading
A sentence you could use: Nagra builds the poem like a song, with the shoppers' complaints repeated as a comic refrain, but the noisy chorus gives way at midnight to quiet two-line stanzas of dialogue, so the form moves from the pressures of the shop to the private, priceless world of the couple.
Dialect and voice

Spellings like “ver yoo bin?” and “my newly bride” let us hear the speaker. Nagra uses the Punjabi-English of his parents' generation. Using this voice in a love poem shows that love poetry can belong to anyone.

Dialect: the words, grammar and pronunciation of a particular group or region.
Refrain and repetition

The customers' chant ends with “in di worst Indian shop” twice, and three stanzas start with “my bride”. The repetition makes the poem sound like a song, and sets the moaning shop against the adored bride.

Refrain: a line or phrase repeated through a poem, like a chorus.
Humour and exaggeration

Lovemaking is “like vee rowing through Putney”; the customers list absurd complaints. The comedy makes the love feel joyful and real, not grand or tragic.

Hyperbole: deliberate exaggeration for effect.
Contrasts that combine

The bride has “tiny eyes ov a gun” but also “di tummy ov a teddy”, and she wears a “Tartan sari”. Opposites sit side by side, just as two cultures sit together in their marriage.

Juxtaposition: placing two different things side by side to make us compare them.

The story in short

After what seems to have been a quarrel, a couple walk silently round a lake in winter. Swans on the lake make them stop. By the end, without a word, they are holding hands again.

The message

Love can heal itself after a quarrel. Sometimes the healing happens through actions and touch, not through talking.

Why it was written

Sheers's collection Skirrid Hill is named after a Welsh hill whose name comes from a word meaning “divorce or separation”. Most of its love poems end in parting; this is one where the couple come back together. Swansea University CREW; Seren

The title

Winter is the cold, dead season: a picture of a relationship at a low point. Swans are famous for staying with one partner: Britannica says plainly, “They mate for life.” So the title puts a bleak time and a symbol of lasting love side by side, and the poem moves from the first to the second. (Britannica (swans); the link to the relationship is our reading)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker (“I”) remembers a walk with a partner (“you”) after two days of rain. We are never told what has gone wrong between them, only that they are “silent and apart”.
  • Lines 1–3The rain has finally stopped. The clouds have “given their all”, and in the break the couple go for a walk.
  • Lines 4–6The ground is soaked and seems to gulp for air at their feet. They walk round the edge of the lake without speaking, keeping their distance.
  • Lines 7–9Swans appear and stop them. The swans tip forwards together, in perfect time, as if heavy weights were rolling down to their heads.
  • Lines 10–12Half of each swan disappears under the dark water, leaving white feathers showing like icebergs. Then they pop back up, like boats righting themselves in a storm.
  • Lines 13–15The partner breaks the silence: “They mate for life”. The swans glide away, white as china. The speaker doesn't answer, and the couple walk on into the afternoon light.
  • Lines 16–20Walking slowly over the stones and sand, the speaker notices that their hands have somehow found each other. They are folded together like a pair of wings settling after flight.
The turning point (lines 13–14): the partner's words “They mate for life” and the speaker's silence. Nobody says sorry, but straight afterwards the couple start to come back together. How it ends: On the joined hands, not on words. The final image is calm and settled: the storm in the relationship has passed, like the rain at the start. 4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: three-line stanzas that settle into a pair
About the form
  • A tercet is “a unit or group of three lines of verse, usually containing rhyme.” Britannica
  • A couplet is “two successive lines of poetry, often rhymed”: a stanza that is “a couple, a pairing”. poets.org
  • Sheers's poems in Skirrid Hill usually have no regular metre or rhyme scheme. Most have at least one full rhyme, “frequently supplemented by internal rhymes, assonance, and half rhymes.” Swansea University CREW; Poetry Archive
  • Half rhyme: words that nearly rhyme, such as “water” and “weather”. Enjambment: when a sentence runs on past the end of a line or stanza without a pause.
What this poem does with it
  • 20 lines: six tercets, then a final two-line couplet. our count
  • Three becomes two: the uneven three-line stanzas suit a couple who are out of step. The poem ends in a couplet, a “pairing”, just as the couple become a pair again. our reading
  • Sentences flow across stanzas (for example lines 6–7 and 18–19). The long, run-on sentences feel like a slow walk, and the last sentence carries the hands across the stanza break until they “folded”. our reading
  • Sound echoes, not a rhyme scheme: “water” (line 10) half-rhymes with “weather” (line 12), and “light” (line 15) fully rhymes with the poem's last word, “flight”. The couplet itself doesn't rhyme (“other” / “flight”), so the ending is gentle rather than neat. our check
  • First person, past tense: the speaker looks back. A Sheers resource warns that first person heightens emotion, but “we must be wary of a simplistically biographical reading.” Swansea University CREW
A sentence you could use: Sheers writes in uneven three-line stanzas while the couple are “silent and apart”, but ends with a couplet, a pair of lines for a couple who have become a pair again, so the form itself shows the relationship coming back together.
Weather that matches the feelings

The poem begins after a storm: “The clouds had given their all”. The ground is “gulping for breath”. By the end the couple walk in “afternoon light”. The weather clears as the relationship does.

Pathetic fallacy: when the weather or setting reflects people's feelings.
The swans as a symbol

The swans move “in unison” while the couple are apart. “They mate for life” turns them into a model of lasting love. The hands become “a pair of wings”, so the couple end up like the swans.

Symbol: an object that stands for a bigger idea.
Actions, not words

Only one sentence is spoken, and the speaker “didn't reply”. Instead the hands “swum the distance between us”, as if their bodies make up before their minds do. AQA's 2019 mark scheme points to “the use of hands, or swans, or the natural world”.

Personification: giving human actions or feelings to something that is not human.
Surprising comparisons

Swans are “icebergs of white feather”, “boats righting in rough weather” and “porcelain”. Each image mixes strength and danger or fragility: love can survive storms, but it can also break.

Metaphor and simile: describing one thing as if it were another (metaphor), or saying it is “like” another (simile).
Examiners warn: don't just describe the stanza shape. AQA's 2022 report says “There is a risk of comments on poetic construction becoming rather descriptive”. Always say what the form shows about the relationship.

The story in short

The speaker remembers breaking up with a secret lover years ago, both of them silent and crying. Now he hears other people gossiping about her. Nobody knows they were lovers, so he has to grieve in secret.

The message

A secret love brings double pain. You lose the person, and you cannot even grieve in public. The hurt of being betrayed lasts for years.

Why it was written

Byron published the poem in 1816, the year his own marriage collapsed amid scandal and he left England for good. One critical source says Byron later put a false date, 1808, on the poem, and that his later letters linked it to a married woman, Lady Frances Wedderburn Webster, whose name he wanted to protect. Britannica; Gale, Poetry for Students the 1808 date and the Webster link are one source

The title

The title is also the first line, so the poem starts at the moment of breaking up. “We two” stresses that there were only two people in this love: nobody else knew. “Parted” is in the past tense: the love is already over before the poem begins, and the rest of the poem deals with the damage. A speaker remembers standing by a pond with the person he loved, on a cold winter day, when he realised their love had died.

The poem
What it means, in plain English
We stood by a pond that winter day,
And the sun was white, as though chidden of God,
And a few leaves lay on the starving sod;

The setting: a pond in winter. “chidden” = scolded, told off: even the sun seems to be punished by God. “sod” = earth, ground; it is “starving”, so nothing can grow. Everything is grey, white and dead.

Over tedious riddles of years ago;
And some words played between us to and fro
On which lost the more by our love.
Her eyes “rove” (wander) over him as if he were a boring old puzzle (“tedious riddles”). Their words go back and forth like a game, arguing about who “lost the more” by loving. Love has turned into a contest.

Alive enough to have strength to die;
And a grin of bitterness swept thereby
Like an ominous bird a-wing…
A smile should be warm, but hers is “the deadest thing”, with just enough life left to die. A bitter grin crosses her face like a bird flying past (“a-wing” = in flight). “ominous” = warning that something bad is coming.

Years later. “keen” = sharp, painful. He has learned that love lies and “wrings” (twists, squeezes) people with pain. Each new lesson brings back the same picture: her face, the cursed sun, the tree and the pond. The poem ends where it began.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: four neat quatrains that go round in a circle
About the form
  • A quatrain is a stanza (verse) of four lines. An ABBA rhyme scheme means lines 1 and 4 rhyme, and lines 2 and 3 rhyme: the outer lines wrap around the inner pair.
  • A lyric is a short poem expressing one speaker's feelings. Hardy himself warned readers that his poems were often “dramatic or personative”, meaning the “I” may be a character, not Hardy himself. Hardy's Preface to Wessex Poems, 1898 one source
  • Pathetic fallacy is the “poetic practice of attributing human emotion or responses to nature”. The term was coined by the critic John Ruskin. Britannica
What this poem does with it
  • Four quatrains, 16 lines, rhyming ABBA in every stanza (“day” / “grey”, “God” / “sod”). The fourth line of each stanza is set in (indented). our count from the anthology
  • The ABBA pattern closes in on itself: each stanza ends on the same sound family it started with. That suits a speaker who can't escape. our reading
  • The whole poem is circular too: stanza 4 repeats the face, the sun, the tree and the pond with its leaves from stanza 1. He keeps coming back to the same moment. our reading
  • Three stanzas of past, one of present: “Since then,” moves to years later, but the last line takes us straight back to the pond.
  • Not quite a perfect rhyme: “rove” and “love” look alike but don't sound alike. Even “love” fails to match. our reading
A sentence you could use: Hardy's tight ABBA quatrains wrap each stanza back on itself, and the final stanza returns to the same pond, sun and leaves as the first, so the form traps the speaker inside one painful memory he cannot escape.
Nature mirrors the dead love

The “white” sun, “starving sod” and “grey” leaves fallen from the tree show a world with no warmth or life, just like the relationship.

Pathetic fallacy: giving human feelings to nature, so the setting reflects the mood (Britannica).
Death imagery

Her smile is “the deadest thing”; the leaves have “fallen”; the bird is “ominous”. Love is presented as something that dies.

Semantic field: a group of words linked by the same topic, here death and decay.
A curse from above

The sun is “chidden of God” at the start and “God curst” at the end. Hardy suggests the universe, even God, is against the lovers.

Imagery: descriptive language that creates a picture in the reader's mind.
Talking to “you”

“Your eyes”, “your mouth”, “Your face”: he speaks straight to her, but she never answers. It feels like an accusation she can't reply to.

Direct address: speaking straight to a person as “you”. AQA's 2024 mark scheme points to the “direct address in ‘Neutral Tones’”.
Examiners warn: AQA's 2022 report says comments on “poetic construction”, such as the ABBA rhyme scheme, risk “becoming rather descriptive”. Don't just say “it rhymes ABBA”: say what the circular, closed-in shape shows about the speaker's feelings.

He would not call his life special: it is hard, cold work (a waterbutt is a rain-water barrel; he has to break the ice on it). But “Still”, it is he who sends news of “that other world”: the natural world the speaker has lost touch with.
Lines 13–15pouring air and light into an envelope. … tap out messages across the icy miles.
His letters are full of fresh air and light, things you cannot really post. At night they both watch the same TV news, apart but sharing it. In the last line their “souls” send messages to each other, like a telegraph tapping across the cold distance between them.

Learn these word for word. Short quotations score better than long ones.
LINE 2
“the first lapwings return”
Means: he sees the lapwings (farmland birds, also called peewits) coming back as spring approaches. Why it matters: it shows how closely he watches nature, and it is the first thing he wants to share. The birds “return” each year, a hint that the loved one’s letters return too, regularly. LINES 3–4
“his knuckles singing / as they reddened in the warmth.”
Means: coming in from the cold, his hands tingle and turn red. Why it matters: “singing” turns a painful sting into music, as if his body is happy. It suggests the joy he feels as he sits down to write. Technique: metaphor; personification (knuckles that sing); enjambment across stanzas
LINE 5
“It’s not romance, simply how things are.”
Means: this is just ordinary life, not something romantic. Why it matters: a flat, honest line in the middle of the poem. It refuses to make country life pretty. It can also be read as the speaker saying the relationship is not a love affair, which keeps the relationship open. Technique: end-stopped line; plain, conversational tone
LINE 7
“me with my heartful of headlines”
Means: the speaker is filled with news stories from work. Why it matters: “heartful” sounds like “handful”: the speaker’s heart is full, but of headlines, other people’s events, not the speaker’s own life. It contrasts with his seasons and soil. Technique: alliteration (heartful / headlines); an unusual word, made like “handful”
LINE 9
“Is your life more real because you dig and sow?”
Means: is his life more genuine because he works the land? Why it matters: the speaker doubts their own indoor life. The question is never fully answered, which makes the reader weigh up city against country, words against work. Technique: rhetorical question; monosyllables (“dig and sow”: plain words for plain work)
LINE 13
“pouring air and light into an envelope.”
Means: his letters are full of fresh, open-air life. Why it matters: you cannot post air and light, so the image is impossible, almost magical. It shows how much his letters lift the speaker: they let the speaker breathe. Technique: metaphor; hyperbole
LINE 15

Means: their deepest selves stay in touch across the cold distance. Why it matters: “our” finally joins them together. “tap out” suggests Morse code or typing: a steady, hidden connection. “icy miles” admits the distance is real and cold. Technique: metaphor; change of pronoun (he / me / you → our); final line as resolution

The form: five tercets, like a letter
About the form
  • A tercet is a stanza of three lines. This poem has five of them (15 lines), with no rhyme scheme and no regular rhythm. our count from the anthology
  • Without rhyme or a fixed beat, the poem sounds like natural speech, or like a letter written to someone you know well. our reading
What this poem does with it
  • Sentences run across stanzas: lines 3–4 (“his knuckles singing” / “as they reddened”), 6–7 and 12–13 all run on into the next stanza. The stanzas are like separate houses, but the sentences cross the gaps between them, just as the letters cross the miles. our reading
  • Pronouns move together: stanza 1 is “he” and “me”; from line 6 it is “You”, talking straight to him; the last line is “our”. The grammar travels from two separate people to one shared feeling.
  • Structure as two worlds: lines about him (digging, ice, snow) alternate with lines about the speaker (headlines, screen). The poem keeps swapping between country and city, the way letters go back and forth. our reading
  • Why it matters: the form is a correspondence in miniature. Separate stanzas show the distance; the run-on sentences and the final “our” show the connection winning.
A sentence you could use: Dooley’s five unrhymed tercets are separated like the two houses, but her sentences run on across the gaps between stanzas, so the form itself shows the letters crossing the distance until the final “our” joins the two lives.
Contrast: country and city

His world is physical and natural: “digging his garden, planting potatoes”, “breaking ice on a waterbutt”. The speaker’s is indoor and made of words: “heartful of headlines”, “feeding words onto a blank screen.” Setting them side by side raises the question at the heart of the poem: whose life is “more real”?

Juxtaposition: placing two things side by side to highlight the contrast between them.
Imagery of nature and seasons

Lapwings returning, “seeing the seasons / turning”, ice and snow: the man lives by nature’s calendar. AQA’s specimen mark scheme points to the “use of imagery of nature to present parent” in this poem. Nature here stands for something honest and renewing that the letters carry to the city.

Imagery: descriptive language that creates a picture in the reader’s mind (and can appeal to the other senses).
Cold and warmth

The poem is full of cold (“in the cold”, “snow”, “icy miles”) but also warmth: knuckles that redden “in the warmth”. The physical cold stands for the distance; the warmth stands for the feeling that crosses it. our reading

Semantic field: a group of words linked to the same topic (here, cold weather) spread through a text.
Communication

Letters, “headlines”, a “blank screen”, “the same news”, “messages”: the poem is about ways of sending words. The news on TV is shared but impersonal; the letters and the souls’ “messages” are personal. The most real message is the one sent with love.

The story in short

A young man is moving into his first home. His mother comes to help him measure the rooms with a tape measure. As he climbs higher, the tape stretches between them.

The message

Growing up means moving away from a parent. The bond still holds, but it gets stretched thin, and the child has to risk going it alone.

Why it was written

Armitage based it on a real day: his mum helped him measure a rented house when he left home. He calls it “a rite of passage poem.” Oak National Academy video Armitage's own words; one source

The title

The poem has no real title. Armitage says it “doesn't have a title because it's taken from a sequence of poems called ‘Book of Matches.’” Armitage's own words; one source So we call it by its first words. That makes the opening “Mother,” a direct address: the whole poem is spoken to her. And the first idea we meet is distance. the last two sentences are our reading (Oak National Academy video; AQA anthology)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker is a young man talking straight to his mother (“You”). He has just got his own place, and she has come round to help. It is written in the present tense, so we feel we are there.
  • Lines 1–2He tells her that measuring anything longer than a hand-span (“a single span”) needs two people. It sounds like a practical rule, but it also hints that he still needs her.
  • Lines 3–4She helps him measure windows, curtain boxes (pelmets) and doors. The empty house seems enormous: its walls are “acres” and its floors are “prairies”.
  • Lines 5–8She holds the end of the tape (“the zero-end”). He takes the reel, calls the numbers back to her, then goes upstairs. The tape keeps unrolling, as if it is measuring the years between them. Then two one-word sentences: “Anchor. Kite.”
  • Lines 9–11Alone upstairs, he moves through the empty bedrooms like an astronaut (“space-walk”). He climbs the ladder into the loft. The tape is stretched as far as it will go: “breaking point”. Something “has to give;”.
  • Lines 12–15Two floors down, his mother is still holding on to the very end of the tape. He reaches for a hatch that opens onto the sky. The poem ends with two choices: he might fall, or he might fly.
The turning point (line 9): after the first two stanzas (8 lines, the length of a sonnet's first part), the son goes on alone. “I space-walk” starts the stanza with “I”, not “You”. Mother and son are now apart, joined only by the tape. How it ends: On an open question. The last line, “to fall or fly.”, gives two opposite futures and does not choose. Independence is exciting and frightening at the same time. The tone: It starts practical and almost jokey, with lists of things to measure. It becomes tense as the tape stretches, and ends hopeful but nervous, poised at the edge of the sky.

2The poem, section by section

Key words to quote
What it means, in plain English
Lines 1–2Mother, any distance … a second pair of hands.
A span is the distance across a stretched hand. Anything longer needs two people to measure it. On the surface it's a practical rule. Underneath, it says he still can't manage alone: he needs her “hands”.
Lines 3–4You come to help me measure … the prairies of the floors.
A list of things to measure: windows, pelmets (boxes that hide curtain rails), doors. Then the house grows huge. Acres and prairies (vast open grasslands in North America) are exaggerations: to a young man on his own, the house feels as big as a wilderness. The rhyme “doors / floors” makes this opening neat and steady.
Lines 5–8You at the zero-end, me with the spool of tape … unreeling … Anchor. Kite.
She holds the start of the tape; he holds the reel and calls out the measurements “back to base” (like a mission talking to control). He climbs the stairs and the tape keeps unrolling, measuring the years between them as well as the metres. The stanza ends with two single words. Who is the anchor and who is the kite? Lines 9–11I space-walk through the empty bedrooms … to breaking point, where something … has to give;
Now it's “I”. The empty rooms are like outer space and he floats through them like an astronaut on a spacewalk. The form: a sonnet that stretches to 15 lines
About the form
  • A sonnet is traditionally a 14-line poem, usually in iambic pentameter (lines of ten syllables with a da-DUM beat). Britannica; Poetry Archive
  • Sonnets were traditionally love poems, and they often have a volta (turn): a shift in thought partway through. Britannica; Poetry Archive
  • The Poetry Archive says that calling a poem a sonnet means it “stands in relation to the long tradition of sonnets”, not that it follows every rule. Poetry Archive one source
What this poem does with it
  • It comes from the first part of Book of Matches (1993), which Armitage's publisher describes as “a series of sonnets”, each meant to have “the urgency of a struck match”. Faber blurb (via bookseller); Oak National Academy one source each
  • But this one has 15 lines, not 14, and no regular rhyme scheme. Oak National Academy says it “does not strictly follow the sonnet form but does use many of its conventions”, such as the closing rhyming couplet. our count; Oak National Academy
  • Why 15? The extra line pushes past the sonnet's limit, just as the son pushes past the end of the tape. The form reaches “breaking point” too. our reading
  • Where it turns: the first 8 lines are “You” and “me” together. Line 9 starts with “I”, alone. That is where a traditional sonnet turns. our reading
  • Line lengths: lines get long and run on (enjambment) as the tape unreels, then line 11 shrinks to three words. The shape of the lines acts out stretching and snapping. our reading
  • A love poem, but a mother's: a sonnet is the classic form for romantic love; Armitage uses it for a son's love for his mother. our reading

The story in short

A grown man remembers his father ploughing a field with a horse. As a small boy he stumbled along behind, admiring him and wanting to be just like him. Now the roles have flipped: his old father stumbles behind him.

The message

Children look up to their parents, but time turns the relationship round. The child who followed becomes the one who leads, and the parent becomes the follower.

Why it was written

Heaney grew up on his family’s farm in County Derry. His father, Patrick, was “a farmer and cattle dealer”. Heaney did not become a farmer: he became a poet. Britannica; Heaney estate

The title

A follower is someone who walks behind, or who copies a leader. The boy literally follows his father up the field, and he wants to follow in his footsteps by becoming a farmer. Line 19 picks up the word: “All I ever did was follow”. Then the last stanza flips it: the old father is now the follower. A critic calls this “the double meaning of the title”. one critic + our reading (English Association (Peter Cash); AQA anthology)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker is Heaney as an adult, looking back on his childhood on the family farm. He speaks in the first person (“I”, “My father”). Most of the poem is in the past tense; the last stanza jumps to “today”.
  • Lines 1–4His father ploughs a field with a horse. His shoulders are rounded and strong, like a ship’s sail filled with wind. The horse pulls hard when the father clicks his tongue.
  • Lines 5–7He is “An expert.” He sets up the plough’s parts, the “wing” and the shining steel “sock” (the pointed blade). The turned earth (“sod”) rolls over in one piece, without breaking.
  • Lines 8–12At the end of the field (the “headrig”), one tug on the reins turns the sweating “team” (the horse pulling the plough) round. His eye narrows as he aims the plough, “Mapping the furrow exactly.”
  • Lines 13–16The boy stumbles along behind in his father’s “hob-nailed wake” (the path left by his heavy boots), and sometimes falls. Sometimes his father carries him on his back, rising and dipping with each heavy step.
  • Lines 17–20The boy wanted to grow up and plough like his father. But all he ever did was follow along in his father’s “broad shadow”.
  • Lines 21–24He admits he was a nuisance, tripping, falling and chattering (“Yapping”). “But today” it is his father who keeps stumbling behind him, and “will not go away.”
The turning point (line 22): “But today”. After five stanzas in the past, the poem jumps to the present, and the roles are reversed. How it ends: the father, once strong and skilled, is now old and stumbling, and the grown son leads. “will not go away.” is ambiguous: it can sound irritated (an old parent who needs care and is a burden), or loving (his father stays in his mind and memory for good). The tone: admiring and affectionate for most of the poem, with gentle self-mockery about the clumsy boy. The final lines are harder to read; AQA’s 2023 mark scheme calls the poem’s tone “more ambiguous”.

2The poem, section by section

Highlighted words are the ones worth quoting in the exam. The poem is still in copyright, so read the full text in your anthology, or in AQA's free anthology PDF. Below, each section is explained in plain English, with the key words to quote.
Key words to quote
What it means, in plain English
Lines 1–4worked with a horse-plough, … shoulders globed like a full sail
The opening line puts the father first, doing hard, skilled work. His shoulders are “globed” (rounded, swelling) like a sail full of wind: big, powerful and smooth. The horse strains forward at the click of his tongue, so even the animal obeys him.
Lines 5–8An expert. … The sod rolled over without breaking.
Two short words, a full stop: the boy’s verdict on his dad. Heaney then uses the real farming words (“wing”, “sock”, “headrig”) as if proud to know them. The earth turns over in one neat strip: perfect work.
Lines 9–12the sweating team turned round … Mapping the furrow exactly.
One pull on the reins (“a single pluck”) turns the horse and plough at the end of the field. The father narrows his eye and aims, like a surveyor drawing a map. “exactly.” ends the stanza: he never makes a mistake.
Lines 13–16I stumbled in his hob-nailed wake, … Dipping and rising to his plod.
Now the boy appears, and he is the opposite of his father: he stumbles and falls on the shiny turned earth. A “wake” is the trail behind a ship, which carries on the sail image. Sometimes the father carries him piggyback, rocking with each slow step, like a boat on waves.
Lines 17–20I wanted to grow up and plough, … In his broad shadow round the farm.
The boy’s dream: to copy his father’s ploughing pose, closing one eye and stiffening his arm. But he could only follow, always in his father’s shadow. The shadow shows how big the father seemed, and how small the boy felt.
Lines 21–24I was a nuisance, tripping, falling, … But today … my father who keeps stumbling
The boy was a pest who kept tripping and chattering. Then the time jumps to “today”: the father, now old, is the one who “keeps stumbling” behind his son, and “will not go away”. The roles have swapped completely.

3Key quotations to learn

Learn these word for word. Short quotations score better than long ones.
LINE 2
“His shoulders globed like a full sail strung”
Means: his father’s shoulders are rounded and strong, like a sail filled with wind. Why it matters: the boy sees his father as huge and powerful, and he moves smoothly, like a ship. It shows deep admiration. Technique: simile; sibilance (repeated “s” sounds: shoulders, sail, strung)
LINE 5
“An expert.”
Means: his father is a master at ploughing. Why it matters: a short, blunt sentence, like a child’s final judgement. It sums up the boy’s hero-worship in two words. Technique: minor sentence (a sentence without a verb) and caesura (a pause mid-line)
LINE 12
“Mapping the furrow exactly.”
Means: he plans and ploughs a perfectly straight line. Why it matters: words of precision (“Mapping”, “exactly”) make ploughing sound like a science or an art. The father has total control. Technique: metaphor (ploughing as map-making); semantic field of skill
LINE 13
“I stumbled in his hob-nailed wake,”
Means: the boy trips along behind in the path his father’s heavy boots leave. Why it matters: “stumbled” is the opposite of the father’s skill. A “wake” is the trail behind a ship, so the father is the great sailing ship and the boy is bobbing behind. Technique: metaphor; contrast between father and son
LINE 15
“Sometimes he rode me on his back”
Means: sometimes his father carried him piggyback while ploughing. Why it matters: a rare moment of tenderness: the strong father carries the weak child. At the end, the burden is reversed. Technique: imagery of carrying (foreshadowing the role reversal)
LINE 17
“I wanted to grow up and plough,”
Means: his childhood dream was to be a farmer like his father. Why it matters: this links straight to AQA’s 2025 focus, “feelings about growing up”. The past tense “wanted” hints that the dream never came true: Heaney became a poet. Technique: past tense; first person
LINES 19–20
“All I ever did was follow / In his broad shadow”
Means: he never matched his father; he only trailed behind him. Why it matters: echoes the title. The “broad shadow” shows how much the father dominated the boy’s world, and how small and overshadowed the boy felt. Technique: metaphor; enjambment (the sentence runs on into the next line, like the boy trailing on)
LINES 22–23
“But today / It is my father who keeps stumbling”
Means: now, in the present, his old father is the one tripping along behind him. Why it matters: the turning point. “stumbling” repeats the boy’s word from line 13, so father and son have swapped places. Growing up has reversed the relationship. Technique: volta (turn); role reversal; change from past to present tense

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: neat quatrains, like straight furrows
About the form
  • A quatrain is a stanza of four lines. “Follower” has six of them, and each “follows an abab rhyme scheme”: lines 1 and 3 rhyme, and lines 2 and 4 rhyme. Poetry for Students (Gale); our check
  • The lines have roughly four beats each. One critic says they can “almost” be read as iambic tetrameter (four da-DUM beats), but Heaney does not follow a strict metre. Poetry for Students (Gale) one source
  • A critic says Heaney “writes in ballad quatrains” to show that this role reversal happens to everyone. Ballads are old, simple story-poems, often from country life. English Association (Peter Cash) one critic
What this poem does with it
  • Half-rhymes: many rhymes are close but not exact: “plough” / “furrow”, “wake” / “back”, “falling” / “stumbling”. A few are full rhymes, like “sod” / “plod”. The pattern is regular but slightly rough, like hand-ploughed earth. our check + our reading
  • Regular stanzas, like furrows: six even blocks, one after another, mirror the neat, straight lines the father ploughs. The form is as controlled as he is. our reading
  • Running on: lines and even stanzas flow into each other: “a single pluck / Of reins” runs from stanza 2 into stanza 3, like the plough moving on without stopping. our reading
  • The turn comes late: five stanzas of memory, then “But today” in the last stanza. Saving the twist for the end makes the reversal land as a shock.
  • Why it matters: the steady form is a picture of the father’s skill; the last stanza uses the same form to show him stumbling. The pattern stays the same, but the people in it have swapped places.
A sentence you could use: Heaney’s six regular quatrains, with their steady ABAB rhyme, mirror the neat furrows of his father’s ploughing; but the final stanza’s turn, “But today”, uses that same pattern to show the roles reversed, so the form itself shows how time changes the relationship.
Imagery of the sea

The father’s shoulders are “like a full sail”, and the boy stumbles in his “wake” (the trail behind a ship). The father is a great ship moving powerfully through the land; the boy is tossed about behind. AQA’s mark schemes point to the “natural world in ‘Follower’”.

Extended metaphor: one comparison carried on through several lines or a whole poem.
The language of skill

Words like “An expert.”, “Mapping” and “exactly.”, and the specialist farm words “wing”, “sock” and “headrig”, show the father’s mastery. Heaney seems proud to know the proper words.

Semantic field: a group of words linked to the same topic (here, skill and precision) spread through a text.
Father against son

The father is smooth and in control; the boy “stumbled”, “Fell”, was “tripping, falling”. Setting the two side by side makes the father seem even greater and the boy even smaller.

Juxtaposition: placing two things side by side to highlight the contrast between them.
The role reversal

“But today” jumps from past to present. The boy’s word “stumbled” comes back as the father “keeps stumbling”. The same word, used for the other person, shows the relationship has turned round completely.

Volta: a turning point in a poem, where the mood or argument changes.
Examiners warn: in 2022 the question was about “the power of love” (romantic love). AQA’s report said choices such as Follower, Mother, any distance and Eden Rock “did not usually work as well and may have been selected because the students knew them well so wrote about them without considering their appropriateness.” Use Follower for family and growing-up questions, not romantic ones.

The story in short

A father remembers a day eighteen years ago. He watched his young son play his first game of football, then walk away from him towards the school. The boy looked small and lost, and the memory still hurts.

The message

Watching your child walk away is painful, but it is how a child becomes their own person. A parent’s love is shown by letting them go.

Why it was written

Day Lewis wrote the poem for his eldest son, Sean one source. He knew schools from both sides: he boarded at school from 13, and later worked as a schoolteacher. Agenda poetry journal; Poetry Archive; Old Shirburnian Society

The title

Walking away is what the boy literally does in line 7: “You walking away from me towards the school”. The phrase comes back in line 19, but now it is a general truth about everyone: “How selfhood begins with a walking away,”. So the title starts as one small moment and ends as a lesson about life. The word “away” also ends lines 5, 11 and 19, like an echo of the boy leaving. our reading (AQA anthology)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker is a father, talking to his son as “you”. (One source says Day Lewis wrote it for his eldest son.) He looks back to a day “eighteen years ago”. The poem moves from memory (stanzas 1–2) to thinking about what it means (stanzas 3–4).
  • Lines 1–5Nearly eighteen years ago to the day, on a sunny autumn day, the father watched his son play his “first game of football”. Afterwards the boy drifted off, like a satellite pulled out of its orbit.
  • Lines 6–10The father can still see him walking away towards the school behind a group of boys. He looked like a baby bird let out too early into the wild, walking like someone who “finds no path”.
  • Lines 11–15The unsure little figure drifted away like a seed blown from its plant. The father feels it says something he can’t quite put into words: about how nature gives and takes, and how hard experiences shape an unformed child, like fire hardening clay.
  • Lines 16–18He admits he has had worse partings, but none that still “Gnaws at my mind” like this. Perhaps the moment roughly shows something only God could show perfectly.
  • Lines 19–20The lesson: you become your own person (“selfhood”) by walking away, and a parent proves their love by letting go.
The turning point (line 11): “That hesitant figure”. The first two stanzas describe what he saw; from stanza 3 the father starts to ask what the moment means. How it ends: with a calm, wise saying: “love is proved in the letting go.” The pain is not gone, but the father understands it. Letting go is not a lack of love; it is the proof of love. The tone: tender, sad and thoughtful. AQA’s 2017 mark scheme speaks of the “sadness of the parent in ‘Walking Away’”. By the end the sadness turns into acceptance.

LINES 4–5
“like a satellite / Wrenched from its orbit”
Means: the boy leaves his father’s side like a satellite torn out of its path round a planet. Why it matters: the father is the planet the child used to circle. “Wrenched” is violent: the parting feels sudden and painful, even though the boy is only “drifting” off. Technique: simile; violent verb “Wrenched” set against gentle “drifting”
LINE 8
“the pathos of a half-fledged thing set free”
Means: the boy looked pitiful, like a young bird let out before its feathers have grown. Why it matters: “half-fledged” shows the father thinks his son is not ready. “set free” sounds positive, but the next words are “Into a wilderness”: freedom is also danger. Technique: metaphor (bird imagery); oxymoron-like contrast of freedom and vulnerability
LINE 10
“Who finds no path where the path should be.”
Means: the boy walks as if he is lost, with no clear way forward. Why it matters: it shows the child’s uncertainty, and the father’s worry. The repeated “path” makes the missing guidance stand out: the father can’t walk it for him. Technique: repetition; symbolism (the path of life)
LINE 12
“Like a winged seed loosened from its parent stem,”
Means: the boy floats away like a sycamore seed falling from its tree. Why it matters: the seed must leave the tree to grow into a new tree. So leaving is natural and necessary, even if it hurts. “parent” makes the link to the father clear. Technique: simile; nature imagery
LINE 15
“Ordeals which fire one’s irresolute clay.”
Means: hard experiences shape and strengthen a person who is still soft and unformed. Why it matters: clay is hardened in a kiln’s fire to make something lasting. The father accepts that his son must face hard times to become strong. Technique: metaphor (pottery)
LINES 16–17
“none that so / Gnaws at my mind still.”
Means: no other goodbye still troubles him as much as this one. Why it matters: “Gnaws” suggests an animal chewing: a slow, constant pain that eighteen years have not healed. It shows how deeply a parent feels a small moment. Technique: metaphor; enjambment (the line breaks before “Gnaws”, so the word hits hard)
LINE 20
“And love is proved in the letting go.”
Means: a parent shows real love by allowing a child to leave. Why it matters: the poem’s final lesson, said like a proverb. It turns sadness into acceptance: letting go is not losing love, it proves it. Technique: aphorism (a short, wise saying); alliteration of “l”

4How the poet writes it (methods)

The form: four neat stanzas holding back strong feelings
About the form
  • The poem has 20 lines in four stanzas of five lines each. A five-line stanza is sometimes called a quintain. our count
  • Each stanza rhymes ABACA: lines 1, 3 and 5 rhyme, and lines 2 and 4 do not. For example, “see” / “free” / “be.” in stanza 2. our check of the anthology text
  • A regular, repeated pattern like this can make a poem feel controlled and calm. our reading
What this poem does with it
  • Control over feeling: the father is in pain, but he speaks in a careful, ordered pattern. The form shows an adult trying to make sense of a hurt, not just pouring it out. our reading
  • “away” keeps coming back: the rhyme word “away” ends lines 5, 11 and 19. The boy keeps leaving, all through the poem, just as the memory keeps returning. our reading
  • Running on: stanza 1 does not stop at its end: the boy goes “drifting away” straight on into stanza 2. The sentence drifts across the gap, like the boy drifting off. our reading
  • Memory, then meaning: stanzas 1–2 describe the scene; stanzas 3–4 think about it. The poem ends on a rhyming, proverb-like line (“show” / “go.”), so the lesson lands with a sense of completion.
  • Why it matters: the steady form lets the father turn one painful memory into a calm truth about all parents and children.
A sentence you could use: Day Lewis uses four regular five-line stanzas with an ABACA rhyme, so the father’s pain is held in a calm, controlled shape; the rhyme word “away” returns again and again, just as the memory of his son leaving still “Gnaws” at him.
Images of leaving orbit and flight

The son is a “satellite” “Wrenched from its orbit”, then a “half-fledged thing set free”. The father is the centre the child used to circle; now the child is flying off into a “wilderness”.

Simile: a comparison using “like” or “as” (here, “like a satellite”).
Nature imagery

Autumn “leaves just turning”, a young bird, a “winged seed” blown from its “parent stem”: nature makes the parting feel natural, part of a cycle. AQA’s mark scheme for the Nov 2020 Walking Away question lists “imagery of the natural world” as a method worth comparing (its examples are other poems).

Imagery: descriptive language that creates a picture in the reader’s mind.
The fire and the clay

“Ordeals which fire one’s irresolute clay.” Clay is soft until it is fired in a kiln. The father accepts that hard experiences, like that lonely walk, are what shape his son into a strong, finished person.

Metaphor: describing something as if it were something else, without “like” or “as”.
Speaking to “you”, then to everyone

The father speaks straight to his son (“I watched you play”), which makes it personal. The ending switches to general truths with no “I” or “you”: “love is proved in the letting go.” One memory becomes a lesson for every parent.

Direct address: when a poem speaks straight to a person, using “you”.

The story in short

A grown-up son sees his parents waiting for him across a stream. They are young again, dressed just as he remembers, setting out a picnic with a place for him. They wave and call him over. In the last line he says it is not what he expected.

The message

Love between parents and child outlasts time, and perhaps even death. Critics such as the Poetry Archive, John Mole and Vahni Capildeo read the parents as dead, calling to him across the stream. So many readers take the crossing as the speaker’s own death, gentler than he feared. our reading

Why it was written

Causley’s father died when Causley was seven, from the effects of the First World War. His mother raised him, and he cared for her in later life. The Poetry Archive calls the poem “the moving elegy to his parents” (an elegy is a poem mourning the dead). Poetry Archive; Charles Causley Trust

The title

Eden Rock sounds like a real local landmark, a rock by a stream. But Eden is also the Garden of Eden: in the Bible, the “earthly paradise” where Adam and Eve lived. So the title hints that the place beyond the rock may be a kind of heaven. our reading We could not find a reliable source saying whether Eden Rock is a real place. (Britannica (“Garden of Eden”); our reading)

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.

  • Lines 17–19Calmly, they wave him over from the other bank. He hears them call: they show him the path across, and say that crossing is easier than he thinks.

The stream between them now matters: he has to cross it to join them. How it ends: the last line is cut off from the rest by a gap, just as the speaker is cut off from his parents by the stream. It is open to more than one reading. Critics including the Poetry Archive (“elegy to his parents”), John Mole and Vahni Capildeo read the parents as dead. If the crossing is death, the line means dying, or rejoining his parents, is gentler and more ordinary than he feared. You could also read it as surprise at how clearly the memory returns. our reading The tone: calm, tender and full of small, loving detail (the Thermos, the sauce bottle, the blue cups). Under the calm there is a quiet ache: the parents are young forever, the speaker is not, and a stream lies between them.

The parents wave him over from the far side of the stream. They call out to show him the path and to reassure him that crossing is easy. If the stream divides the living from the dead, they are gently inviting him to join them.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: tidy stanzas, then a gap, then one line
About the form
  • A quatrain is a stanza of four lines. “Eden Rock” has four quatrains (lines 1–16), then a three-line stanza (lines 17–19), then one line on its own (line 20). Some revision guides say five quatrains, but that is wrong for AQA’s printed text. AQA anthology (our count)
  • Half-rhyme means words that nearly rhyme but not quite. Most rhymes here are half-rhymes, in an alternating pattern: “Rock” / “Jack”, “suit” / “feet”, “bank” / “think.”. Only “screw” / “blue” is a full rhyme. our check
  • The lines are roughly the same length (about ten syllables), but there is no strict beat. Causley drew on folk songs, hymns “and above all, ballads”, so he often wrote in simple, traditional shapes. Poetry Archive
What this poem does with it
  • Calm, even stanzas: the four regular quatrains match the calm, orderly picnic. Everything is neat and in its place, like the plates on the cloth. our reading
  • The pattern breaks: when the parents call to him (lines 17–19) the stanza shrinks to three lines. Something is missing, just as the family is missing one member on their side of the stream. our reading
  • The gap before the last line: line 20 is cut off by white space, like the stream between the son and his parents. He has not yet crossed. our reading
  • Almost-rhymes: the half-rhymes are close but never quite match, like a family nearly, but not quite, reunited. The last line half-rhymes with line 18 (“is!” / “this.”), so it still belongs to the poem. our reading
  • Why it matters: the shape tells the story. Order and closeness first, then a break, then the speaker alone on his side of the gap.

Sauce bottle”, “the tin cups painted blue.” These details make the parents feel real and show how carefully he has kept them in his memory. Imagery: words that help you see, hear or feel a scene in your mind.
Light and nature

His mother’s hair is “the colour of wheat” and “takes on the light.”; the sky shines “as if lit by three suns.” The light grows stronger and stranger, turning a family picnic into something like a vision of heaven.

The stream between them

The parents are on “the other bank.” and he must cross “the drifted stream.” A river you cross to reach the dead is an old idea: in Greek myth, Charon ferried the souls of the dead over the River Styx. The stream may be the line between life and death. Britannica (Charon) the link is our reading

The father is “twenty-five”, the mother “twenty-three”, the dog “Still two years old”. The poem is in the present tense, so the past seems to be happening now. Only the last line, “I had not thought”, shifts tense, as the old speaker steps back.

A model comparison paragraph:

The story in short

A grown-up daughter speaks to her mother. Using old photos and her mum’s stories, she imagines her mother as a fun-loving teenager in Glasgow, ten years before the daughter was born. Then she remembers her own childhood with her.

The message

Before she was a mum, your mother was a young woman with her own life, freedom and fun. Having a child changed that. The daughter loves her, admires her, and is a little jealous of the girl she can never meet.

Why it was written

Duffy calls it “essentially a kind of love poem addressed to my mother” and says it is “entirely autobiographical”. The images “come from old photographs of my mother.” Sheer Poetry, via University of Glasgow Duffy’s words, quoted by the University of Glasgow; one source

The title

“Mine” is a word lovers use, and Duffy calls this “a kind of love poem” to her mother. Duffy’s words, quoted by the University of Glasgow; one source But here it is a child claiming a parent, as if saying that her mother belongs to her. The title phrase comes back in line 9 and again as the very last words of the poem, so the poem keeps returning to the time before the daughter owned her. The University of Glasgow calls this “a cyclical structure”. the ideas about lovers and ownership are our reading (Duffy’s words as quoted in a University of Glasgow guide; University of Glasgow teaching resource; AQA anthology)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupThe speaker is a grown-up daughter talking straight to her mother (“you”). She is not there in the scenes she describes: they happened before she was born. Most of it is in the present tense, so the past feels as if it is happening now.
  • Lines 1–2She places herself “ten years away” from her mother, who is laughing on a street corner with her friends (“your pals”), named as Maggie McGeeney and Jean Duff.
  • Lines 3–5The three girls double up with laughter and “shriek at the pavement.” The mother’s polka-dot dress blows round her legs, and the daughter adds one word: “Marilyn.” (the film star Marilyn Monroe).
  • Lines 6–8“I’m not here yet.” At the dance hall, the young woman is not thinking about a baby at all. She is dreaming of exciting, film-like futures: “the fizzy, movie tomorrows”.
  • Lines 8–10The daughter is sure her mother danced well. Back home, the mother’s own mum (“your Ma”) waits at the entrance to the flats (the close) to give her a beating (a hiding) for being late. The girl thinks the fun was “worth it.”
  • Lines 11–12Now the daughter’s birth arrives: her “loud, possessive yell”. She asks if the ten years before it were the best ones, “eh?” She remembers playing with her mother’s old red high heels, now “relics”.
  • Lines 13–15In the present, she pictures the young version of her mother (“your ghost”) hurrying across George Square in Glasgow. She sees her under a tree with lights and teases her about love bites: “sweetheart?”
  • Lines 16–20A childhood memory: walking home from church (“Mass”), her mother teaches her dance steps (“Cha cha cha!”). Even then, the child wanted the bold, flirty girl her mother used to be. That girl lives on, dancing and laughing, “before you were mine.”
The turning point (line 11): stanza 3 jumps forward to the daughter’s birth, her “loud, possessive yell”. Stanzas 1–2 are the mother’s life without her; stanzas 3–4 mix the daughter’s own memories with that lost past. How it ends: The last line returns to the title phrase. The daughter cannot have the young woman, but in her imagination that girl lasts for ever: “you sparkle and waltz and laugh”. The ending is joyful and a little sad at the same time. The tone: Warm, chatty and teasing (“eh?”, “sweetheart?”), full of glamour and fun. AQA’s 2023 mark scheme points to “the sense of joy and celebration” in the poem. Underneath there is longing, and some envy, for the girl the daughter never knew.

LINE 5

Means: her mother looked like the film star Marilyn Monroe. Technique: a one-word sentence and an allusion (a reference to something famous). It turns an ordinary street into a film set, and the mother into a glamorous star.
LINE 6

LINE 11

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: four neat stanzas, a chatty voice, and time that won’t stay still
About the form
  • The poem has four stanzas of five lines each (quintains). The University of Glasgow says this “gives the poem a clear and controlled shape”. University of Glasgow; our count
  • There is no regular rhyme or rhythm: it is close to free verse. The same source says this “gives the poem a natural and conversational tone, like someone remembering and reflecting.” University of Glasgow; our count one source
  • It is written in the second person: the speaker talks to “you”, her mother. AQA’s 2017 mark scheme names the “second person in ‘Before You Were Mine’” as a method worth writing about. AQA mark scheme 2017
What this poem does with it
  • Three time zones: the poem “shifts between past and present, from the time before the speaker was born, her own childhood, and the present day.” University of Glasgow Stanzas 1–2 are the mother’s youth; stanza 3 brings in the daughter’s birth and childhood; “and now” jumps to the present; stanza 4 goes back to childhood and then to the youth again.
  • Present tense for the past: “you laugh”, “your Ma stands”. The mother’s youth is told as if it is happening now, so the daughter seems to be watching a film of it. our reading
  • A circle: the title words appear in line 9 and as the final words. The poem cannot move forward to the present; it keeps going back to the girl “before”. our reading
  • Talking voice: questions and slang (“eh?”, “sweetheart?”, “You reckon”) make it sound like a daughter teasing her mum. The neat stanzas hold this chat in a careful shape, like photos in an album. our reading

The story in short

A child climbs all over their grandfather, from his shoes to the top of his head. The whole poem pretends the grandfather is a mountain, and the child is a climber going up it with no ropes.

The message

Getting to know someone you love is an adventure. Up close, the child learns their grandfather's body and his life story, and ends feeling safe and close to his “good heart.”

Why it was written

Waterhouse lived in Northumberland and worked as a teacher and writer. The poet Jo Shapcott, quoted by his publisher, said his poems “bring together the landscape, the mind and the fragile connections between people”: here a person literally becomes a landscape. Jo Shapcott, quoted by The Rialto (his publisher) one source

The title

You climb mountains, not people. The odd verb tells us at once that this is an extended metaphor: the grandfather is the mountain. “My” makes it personal and loving. The title also hints at looking up to him: he is huge to a small child. (our reading, from the AQA anthology text)

1What happens, step by step

Follow along in the poem. The line numbers match the AQA anthology.
  • The setupA child speaks in the first person (“I”) and the present tense, as if the climb is happening now. The grandfather never speaks; apart from a smile and a moving pupil, he stays still, like a mountain.
  • Lines 1–4The child decides to climb “free”, with no safety equipment. They start at his old, cracked shoes (brogues) and scramble up his trousers, grabbing the cloth.
  • Lines 5–12At his shirt they move sideways along his belt to his hand. His nails are broken but easy to grip. His skin feels “like warm ice”. On his arm the child finds an old scar and steps “gently” over the stitch marks.
  • Lines 13–17The child rests on his strong shoulder, careful not to look down, because “climbing has its dangers”. Then up the loose skin of his neck to his smiling mouth.
  • Lines 18–23Across his cheek to look into his brown eyes. Over his wrinkled forehead to his white hair, high up “at this altitude”, reaching for the top (“the summit”).

The turning point (line 13): halfway up, at the shoulder, the child stops to rest and admits “climbing has its dangers”. Before this, the climb sounds easy; from here the poem admits risk, and by the top the child is “gasping for breath”. our reading How it ends: At the summit the climb stops. The child simply lies still, feeling the grandfather's heat and heartbeat. The adventure ends in calm, safety and love. The tone: Playful and adventurous at first, then careful and respectful (“gently”), and finally peaceful and full of love. Key words to quote
What it means, in plain English
Lines 1–4do it free, without a rope or net. … an easy scramble
To free-climb means to climb “without using aids for support” Merriam-Webster. The child chooses no rope and no net: brave, or trusting that Grandad is safe. They begin at his old shoes (brogues), “dusty and cracked”, and scramble up his trousers, gripping the woven cloth.
Lines 5–8traverse along his belt … earth-stained hand
The shirt hangs over like a rock overhang, so the climber goes sideways. To traverse is to cross a slope at an angle Merriam-Webster. The line break after “I change” makes the line change direction too. His hand is stained with soil: he is a man who works outdoors, maybe a gardener or farmer. His broken nails “give good purchase”, a climbing term for a good grip.
Lines 9–12like warm ice … the glassy ridge of a scar
His finger skin is smooth and thick, “like warm ice”: an impossible mix of hot and cold. A shiny scar on his arm becomes a mountain ridge. The child places their feet gently in the old stitch marks: careful not to hurt him, and aware he has been hurt before.
Lines 13–17At his still firm shoulder … for climbing has its dangers
His shoulder is “still firm”: he is old, but still strong. The child rests in its shade and does not look down. The poem admits danger for the first time. Then up the loose skin of his neck to his smiling mouth, where the climber stops to “drink”, as a climber drinks at a mountain stream.
Lines 18–23the screed cheek … reaching for the summit
The cheek is called “screed”, like scree: loose stones on a mountain slope Merriam-Webster the link to scree is our reading. The child looks into his brown eyes and watches a pupil move: up close, the mountain is alive. The wrinkles on his forehead are “well-spaced / and easy” footholds. His white hair is like snow on a peak, “at this altitude” (height).
Lines 24–27clouds and birds circle … slow pulse of his good heart.
At the top, out of breath, the child can only lie there. They see sky, clouds and birds, as from a real peak. But the last things are human: his heat and his heartbeat. The final word is “heart”: the real goal of the climb was to reach him.

3Key quotations to learn

Learn these word for word. Short quotations score better than long ones.
LINE 1
“do it free, without a rope or net.”
Means: the child chooses to climb with no safety gear. Why it matters: the line opens in the present tense (“I decide”), which puts us in the moment. Climbing without a rope shows bravery, but also total trust: the grandfather will keep the child safe.
LINE 7
“earth-stained hand”
Means: his hand is marked with soil. Why it matters: it tells us his life story in two words: he has worked outdoors with the land. It also links him to nature, like the mountain he becomes. Technique: compound adjective (two words joined with a hyphen)
LINES 9–10
“smooth and thick / like warm ice”
Means: his skin feels smooth and solid, like ice, yet it is warm and alive. Technique: simile and oxymoron (“warm ice”: two opposite ideas together). He is solid like a mountain, but alive and warm.
LINE 11
“the glassy ridge of a scar”
Means: a shiny raised scar looks like a mountain ridge. Why it matters: the climb uncovers his past pain. The child places their feet “gently”, showing care and respect for what he has been through.
LINE 13
“At his still firm shoulder, I rest for a while”
Means: the child rests on his strong shoulder. Why it matters: “still” hints he is old, but strong enough to hold the child. AQA's 2025 mark scheme points to “the influence of strength on growing up” in this poem.
LINE 23
“reaching for the summit,”
Means: stretching up to the very top of his head. Technique: extended metaphor: the head is the mountain top. Reaching the summit is a big achievement; getting to know Grandad fully takes effort.
LINES 26–27
“knowing the slow pulse of his good heart.”
Means: the child feels his steady heartbeat and senses his kindness. Why it matters: the poem ends on the heart, not the head: the goal was love. “slow” suggests calm and safety, but also age. “good” is a simple, child-like judgement of his whole character.

4How the poet writes it (methods)

This is AO2: how the poet's choices create meaning.
The form: one long free-verse climb
About the form
  • Free verse is “poetry organized to the cadences of speech and image patterns rather than according to a regular metrical scheme”: no fixed rhythm and no rhyme scheme. Britannica
  • Enjambment is “the continuation of the sense of a phrase beyond the end of a line of verse”: the sentence runs over the line break. Britannica
  • An extended metaphor is “the repeated use of the same metaphorical idea throughout a text”. Oak National Academy
What this poem does with it
  • One unbroken stanza of 27 lines. There is no break, just as a climber can't stop halfway up a cliff. The poem is one long climb from shoes to heart. our count our reading
  • Bottom to top: the order of the poem follows the body, feet first (“brogues”, line 2), shoulder in the middle (line 13), head and “summit” near the end (line 23). The structure is the route. our reading
  • Enjambment for movement: “I change direction” (lines 5–6) is split across the line break, so the sentence changes direction as the line does. Long run-on sentences feel like constant climbing; the full stops are like resting points. our reading
  • Present tense, first person: “I decide”, “I discover”, “I rest”. We climb with the child, step by step, as it happens.
  • Another reading: Oak National Academy suggests “The single, unbroken stanza could reflect the unending process of grieving for a loved one.” This treats the poem as a memory of a grandfather who has died. one source (Oak National Academy) Our reading: the poem does not say he has died. It is in the present tense, and his heart is still beating at the end. our reading
A sentence you could use: Waterhouse writes one long, unbroken stanza that climbs from the grandfather's “old brogues” to his “summit”, so the structure itself becomes the climb, ending not at his head but at “his good heart”.
The grandfather as a mountain

Every detail of his body becomes part of a mountain: a “ridge”, a “summit”, “altitude”. Climbing words fill the poem: “scramble”, “traverse”, “purchase”. This makes him seem huge, strong and worth admiring, and it makes getting to know him a slow, careful adventure. AQA's specimen mark scheme names the “Use of metaphor in ‘Climbing My Grandfather’”.

Opposites side by side

“warm ice”, “an easy scramble”. The grandfather is both hard and soft, old and strong, a mountain and a living man. These contrasts show how complicated a person is when you look closely. our reading

Oxymoron: two opposite words placed together, like “warm ice”.

The Farmer's Bride and Porphyria's Lover

“Compare how poets present strong feelings in romantic relationships in ‘The Farmer’s Bride’ and in one other poem from ‘Love and relationships’.” [30 marks]
AQA, November 2021, paper 8702/1P, Question 01
Why “Question 01”? In November 2021 the poetry question was set on a paper numbered 8702/1P, where Love and relationships was Question 01. In your exam the same kind of question is Paper 2, Question 25, worth the same 30 marks. This answer was written to match AQA's Level 6 descriptors. It has not been marked by AQA, and no one can guarantee a mark, but every part is designed to hit a top-level requirement. About 700 words: a realistic length for 45 minutes.

Step 1: the 5-minute plan

  • Pick the partner: the question is about strong feelings in romantic relationships, so choose Porphyria's Lover: another man whose desire becomes control. The November 2021 mark scheme names it first, and the examiners said it was an effective choice: “Porphyria’s Lover [students engaged with this, especially well alongside The Farmer’s Bride]”. Family poems such as Mother, any distance and Walking Away were “not really appropriate choices”.
  • Big idea (thesis): in both poems a man's desire turns into control of a woman who cannot speak for herself. Browning's speaker kills to own Porphyria and ends calm; Mew's farmer has locked his wife away, but his feelings build with nowhere to go.
  • Comparison points: 1. love as owning (“chose”, “mine”); 2. the similes and images that turn women into animals or objects; 3. first-person voices and silent women; 4. the endings: mounting tension against terrible calm. Then gender as context, and a judgement.

Step 2: the answer, with what earns the marks

Introduction Both Mew and Browning present strong feelings in romantic relationships as dangerous when only one person holds them: in each poem a man's desire turns into control of a woman who cannot speak for herself. Browning's lover acts on his feelings, killing Porphyria to keep her forever, and ends calm and satisfied. Mew's farmer has already locked his wife away, yet his feelings have no outlet and build towards breaking point. This makes The Farmer's Bride the more disturbing poem, because its control is ordinary and still going on.
AO1
  • Answers the question in the first sentence, using its key words, “strong feelings in romantic relationships”.
  • Sets up a similarity and a difference, so the whole essay is a comparison.
  • Ends with a judgement (“more disturbing”) to prove later.
Mark scheme (Nov 2021): “comparison of negative effects of strong feelings, eg ‘Porphyria’s Lover’ or ‘Neutral Tones’”. Level 6: “It takes a conceptualised approach to the full task”. Examiners (2020): students who began with “a thesis statement … tended to be more successful with AO1.”
Paragraph 1 · love as owning Both speakers treat love as ownership. Mew's farmer begins “I chose a maid,”: the verb gives all the choice to him, and “maid” makes her a type, not a person. He admits she was “Too young maybe”, but the careless “maybe” shows how little this troubles him, because there was more to do “At harvest-time than bide and woo.” Browning's speaker is more extreme: the moment he believes “Porphyria worshipped me”, he decides “That moment she was mine, mine, fair,”. The repeated “mine” sounds obsessive, as if he must say it twice to believe it. Both men confuse loving with having, but Browning's speaker turns possession into murder.

  • Short, precise quotations, built into sentences.
  • Zooms in on single words (“chose”, “maybe”, “mine”) and explains what they suggest.
  • Moves between both poems in the same paragraph.
Mark scheme (Nov 2021): “comparison between the ways strong feelings affect how people feel about themselves and/or others”. Level 6: “Judicious use of precise references to support interpretation(s)”.
Paragraph 2 · similes: animals and objects Mew's similes compare the bride to small, hunted creatures: she does the housework “like a mouse”, and the men chased her “flying like a hare”. We pity her fear, yet the images show he sees her as prey. Later the imagery softens: she is “Shy as a leveret”, a young hare, and the stanza ends “To her wild self. But what to me?” He admires her beauty but resents that it is not for him. Browning goes further and turns Porphyria into an object. After her death she is “The smiling rosy little head,” and the pronoun changes: “So glad it has its utmost will,”. “It” replaces “she”, and she becomes a doll he can arrange.

Paragraph 3 · one voice, two silent women Both poems are dramatic monologues, so we hear only the man's version, and both women are silenced. Mew's farmer admits “I’ve hardly heard her speak at all.” The bride's only words come through her eyes: “‘Not near, not near!’ her eyes beseech”. Her fear is obvious, but he never asks its cause. Browning's speaker is less trustworthy. When Porphyria called him, “no voice replied,”: his silence is a way of controlling her. After the murder he insists “I am quite sure she felt no pain.” The words “quite sure” sound as if he is persuading himself. Mew's narrator is limited but honest; Browning's deceives himself.

Paragraph 4 · endings: tension against calm The endings show the sharpest difference. Mew's last stanza builds tension. The bride sleeps “Alone, poor maid.”, and “’Tis but a stair” between them: the short phrase stresses how close she is, and how unreachable. Then his self-control breaks: “Oh! my God! the down,”. The exclamations and repetition of “her eyes, her hair, her hair!” show desire he can no longer hold back, and the poem stops unresolved, leaving a threat in the air. Browning's ending is calm instead: “And all night long we have not stirred,”. His desire is satisfied, and his last line, “And yet God has not said a word!”, suggests he expected heaven's approval. One poem ends in tension that may explode; the other in a terrible peace.

Paragraph 5 · context: gender and control Both poems come from societies in which men held power over women. Browning wrote in 1836, when, under the law of coverture, a wife's legal existence was “suspended” in her husband's. Porphyria, alone with a man at night, risked her reputation: a woman who strayed could “lose her good name”. In 1842 Browning printed the poem under the heading “Madhouse Cells”, marking its speaker as mad. Mew's poem, first printed in 1912 or 1913, shows the same control without madness. Women could not vote, and even after 1857 many couples “were still unable to end a marriage”. The bride is chased, locked in and silenced, and one critic notes the farmer's “inability to recognize his wife's reaction to his patriarchal power”. Mew shows that such power was ordinary.

Conclusion · the judgement Both poets present strong feelings in romantic relationships as destructive when one person controls the other. Browning shows possessive love at its most extreme, but by presenting his speaker as mad he keeps him at a distance. Mew's farmer is no madman: he is an ordinary husband, and his wife's prison is her marriage. That is why The Farmer's Bride is the more troubling poem: Porphyria's story is over, but the bride's fear and the farmer's frustration go on, just one stair apart.

Neutral Tones and Winter Swans

“Compare how poets present powerful feelings about love in ‘Neutral Tones’ and in one other poem from ‘Love and relationships’.” [30 marks]
AQA, June 2024, Question 25

Step 2: the answer, with what earns the marks

Introduction Both Hardy and Sheers present powerful feelings about love through restraint: in each poem a couple stand by cold water in winter, and the landscape carries the feelings they cannot say. However, the poems move in opposite directions. In Neutral Tones, the moment love dies becomes a bitterness the speaker can never escape; in Winter Swans, a couple who begin in silence are drawn back together. Hardy suggests that the most powerful feeling love leaves behind is pain, while Sheers suggests it is a quiet, lasting bond, which makes Neutral Tones the more disturbing poem.

Paragraph 1 · Hardy's drained landscape Hardy paints the end of love in drained colours. The “sun was white, as though chidden of God,”: “chidden” means scolded, so even the sun seems punished and has lost its warmth. The ground is “starving sod;”, and the fallen leaves “were grey.” This pathetic fallacy makes nature as lifeless as the relationship. The title fits: “neutral” tones are colourless, and perhaps the feelings here are so powerful that they have drained the world of colour, leaving only numbness.

Paragraph 2 · love dying in front of him Hardy speaks directly to the woman, which makes the poem feel like an accusation. “Your eyes on me were as eyes that rove”: her gaze wanders, as if he were one of the “tedious riddles of years ago;”, an old puzzle she now finds boring. Their talk is a contest about which of them “lost the more by our love.” Love has become a loss. The paradox that her smile is “the deadest thing” yet “Alive enough to have strength to die;” shows love dying in front of him, and the simile “Like an ominous bird a-wing…” turns her bitter grin into an omen. Paragraph 3 · Sheers: a storm, then the swans Sheers also opens with a bleak landscape after a storm: “The clouds had given their all”, as if they have argued themselves out, like the couple. The personified earth is “gulping for breath at our feet”, suggesting a relationship that is drowning. The couple walk “silent and apart,”: like Hardy's pair, they are together but emotionally distant. But where Hardy's bird is an omen, Sheers's swans are a lesson. They are “tipping in unison.”, the togetherness the couple lacks, and after they have “halved themselves in the dark water,” they rise again “like boats righting in rough weather.” The swans show that love can survive a storm.

Paragraph 4 · the endings: trapped or settled The endings differ most. Hardy's last stanza jumps forward: “Since then,” experience has taught him “keen lessons that love deceives,” and “wrings with wrong,”, where the harsh alliteration sounds like twisting pain. Yet these lessons only bring back the same scene, ending on “And a pond edged with greyish leaves.” The circular structure traps him in one memory: one powerful feeling has become a belief about all love. Sheers ends in movement instead. After “They mate for life”, the speaker admits “I didn't reply”, but their hands answer: they have “swum the distance between us” and end “like a pair of wings settling after flight.” The word “pair” echoes the swans, and “settling” suggests the conflict is over. Feeling is shown through touch, not words.

Paragraph 5 · context linked to ideas Hardy dated the poem 1867, but it was not published until 1898, over thirty years later. He later “rejected the Victorian belief in a benevolent God”, and it is tempting to link this to the “God curst sun,”, though that is a link we make, not something the poem states. Hardy also warned that his poems were often “dramatic or personative”, so the speaker need not be Hardy himself. Sheers's poem comes from Skirrid Hill (2005), named after a hill whose Welsh name comes from a word meaning “divorce or separation”. In that collection, relationships are almost always broken “by death, disagreement, or some other parting”, which makes this couple's reunion stand out. Both poets explore what powerful feelings do to the self: one man is made bitter for life, while the other couple learn that love can last.
