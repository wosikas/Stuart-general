## Storm on the Island — Seamus Heaney (in *Death of a Naturalist*, 1966) — ANCHOR

- **Text checked against:** I found no allowed source with the full text. Poetry Foundation is blocked, and no Poetry Archive, publisher, BL or newspaper text was found. The only full text I opened on an allowed-list domain is in the PMT notes PDF: https://pmt.physicsandmathstutor.com/download/English-Literature/GCSE/Notes/AQA/Poetry/Power-and-Conflict-Anthology/Storm%20On%20The%20Island%20-%20Seamus%20Heaney.pdf. That PDF is revision material, so **every quotation below is SINGLE SOURCE. Check against the anthology.** PMT's text has 19 lines in a single stanza (I counted them).
- **What happens:** An islander speaks for the whole community ("we"). He calmly explains how they have built their houses to survive storms, and says the island has no crops or trees to lose. Then he describes a storm arriving: the sea and wind attack the houses, the people can only sit tight, and he ends by saying it is strange that what they fear is "a huge nothing", meaning empty air.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Nature's power is greater than human preparation. The confidence at the start becomes fear by the end. [power (nature), fear]
  - Isolation: the island has no trees and no company, so the community faces the threat alone. [place/home, fear]
  - Conflict: the storm is described in the language of war (strafing, salvo, bombarded). [conflict/war, power (nature)]
  - Collective identity: a community speaks as "we" and knows how to endure. [identity, place/home]
  - A possible political reading about Northern Ireland exists, but see the AO3 warning below. [conflict]
- **Key quotations (8):** all are from the PMT URL above. All are SINGLE SOURCE and UNVERIFIED against the anthology.
  1. "We are prepared" | declarative opening line: confident and defensive, as if they are ready for an attack | PMT
  2. "we build our houses squat" | adjective: the low, solid houses show human defence and practicality | PMT
  3. "This wizened earth" | personification: the land is old and shrivelled, so it is barren and has nothing to lose | PMT
  4. "can raise a tragic chorus in a gale" (PMT prints "Can raise" at the start of a line) | metaphor from drama: trees would turn the storm into a theatrical lament, but there are no trees | PMT
  5. "spits like a tame cat / Turned savage" | simile plus enjambment: something familiar becomes violent, and the line break mirrors the sudden turn | PMT
  6. "strafes invisibly" | military verb (to strafe is to attack from low-flying aircraft): the wind is an unseen enemy attacker | PMT
  7. "Space is a salvo" | metaphor (a salvo is a burst of gunfire) with sibilance: emptiness itself is the weapon | PMT
  8. "it is a huge nothing that we fear" | oxymoron/paradox ending: the enemy is invisible and abstract, and the poem shifts from confidence to fear | PMT
  - Also useful (PMT): "you know what I mean" (conversational aside to the listener). **CONFLICT:** PMT has "We are bombarded by the empty air". A search-result snippet (allpoetry, not an allowed source, not opened) gave "bombarded with". Check the anthology before quoting this line.
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Blank verse:** I checked this myself against the PMT text. The lines are roughly iambic pentameter (for example, the first line has 10 syllables) and do not rhyme regularly. It is one continuous stanza. The steady line looks controlled and "prepared", but enjambment and caesurae (for example "Blast: you know what I mean") break it up as the storm builds.
  - **Conversational voice / dramatic monologue feel:** the speaker uses "we" and addresses a listener ("as you see", "You might think", "But no:"). The poem sounds like an islander talking to a visitor, which makes the threat feel ordinary and lived-with. PMT calls this a dramatic monologue. That label is SINGLE SOURCE and comes from a revision site.
  - **Semantic field of war:** "strafes", "salvo", "bombarded" and "Exploding". The storm is presented as a military attack, and this is what links the poem to the conflict poems.
  - **Turn ("But no:"):** the poem moves from practical confidence to the sea's betrayal (the "tame cat" turned savage).
  - **Ending on paradox:** "huge nothing" leaves fear of something that cannot be seen or fought. This is the main idea to take into a comparison.
- **Context (AO3):**
  - The collection: *Death of a Naturalist* was Heaney's first collection and came out in 1966. | https://www.seamusheaney.com/death-of-a-naturalist | "The 1966 publication of _Death of a Naturalist_, Seamus Heaney's debut collection, announced the arrival of an exceptional new talent."
  - That *Storm on the Island* is in *Death of a Naturalist*: the only source I opened that says so is PMT, which is SINGLE SOURCE revision material. The estate page above lists only "Digging", "Mid-Term Break" and "Blackberry Picking". | PMT URL above | "Storm on the Island is part of a three-poem segment in the collection 'Death of a Naturalist' which was focused on the Aran Islands"
  - Heaney's background: born on a farm in County Derry, Northern Ireland, in 1939. | https://www.seamusheaney.com/life-and-legacy | "Seamus Heaney was born on 13 April, 1939 in rural County Derry, in Northern Ireland." Britannica adds that he grew up Catholic in a mainly Protestant area: https://www.britannica.com/biography/Seamus-Heaney | "Born April 13, 1939, near Castledawson, County Londonderry, Northern Ireland" (the Britannica summary I saw was paraphrased around this quoted phrase).
  - **Dating warning for the "Troubles" reading:** Britannica dates the Troubles from about 1968, and a 1976 review says Northern Ireland's violence became world news in 1969. *Death of a Naturalist* (1966) came out before both dates. A poem in a 1966 book cannot be "written during the Troubles", although critics may still read it as foreshadowing them.
    - https://www.britannica.com/event/The-Troubles-Northern-Ireland-history | "violent sectarian conflict from about 1968 to 1998 in Northern Ireland"
    - Richard Murphy, "Poetry and Terror", *NYRB*, 1976: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1976/09/30/poetry-and-terror/ | "His first volume, _Death of a Naturalist_, was published ten years ago in 1966." and "Heaney's second volume, _Door into Dark_, appeared in 1969, the year when violence in Northern Ireland became world news."
  - Violent, warlike imagery for nature appears elsewhere in the 1966 book, so Storm's military language fits a pattern in early Heaney. | same NYRB URL | "Bullfrogs are compared to 'mud grenades,' and butter crocks on a pantry shelf to 'large pottery bombs.' Even allowing for the fashion in the Sixties for overemphasizing the toughness and cruelty of nature, you feel that these images are true."
  - Heaney on the Troubles in general, not about this poem: https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/obituary-heaney-the-most-important-irish-poet-since-yeats-1.1510684 | the summarised obituary reported that Heaney "always felt it was impossible for him to take sides". This is a fragment from the obituary; the full sentence was not captured.
  - **GAP: Stormont pun.** I found no reliable source (Heaney interview, critic, publisher, BL or university) for "Storm" in the title being a pun on Stormont. It appears only on school and revision sites. Use it, if at all, as "some readers suggest…", never as fact.
  - **GAP: Heaney interview about this poem.** None was found online. *Stepping Stones* (Dennis O'Driscoll's interviews) may discuss it, but it is not online and was not checked. The Nobel lecture was blocked.
  - **GAP: first periodical publication.** Revision sites claim it first appeared in the *New Statesman* in 1964. I found no reliable source, so it is unverified.
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - power of nature: *Extract from The Prelude* (nature overwhelms a confident human; there the fear is psychological, here it is physical attack) and *Ozymandias* (nature or time outlasts human power).
  - nature as the enemy / conflict: *Exposure* (weather as the real enemy; "nothing happens" against "a huge nothing that we fear"; both use military language for weather) and *Bayonet Charge* (nature in a war setting).
  - fear and isolation: *Exposure* and *The Prelude*.
  - place/home and identity: *The Emigrée* (a remembered home under threat) and *Checking Out Me History* (identity).

## Extract from The Prelude — William Wordsworth (written 1799–1805; published 1850)

- **Text checked against:**
  - 1850 first edition (London: E. Moxon), scanned at the Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/preludeorgrowtho00word (OCR text: https://archive.org/download/preludeorgrowtho00word/preludeorgrowtho00word_djvu.txt). The passage is in Book I, pp. 18–19.
  - Knight's 1896 edition on Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12383/pg12383.txt. Book I, lines 357–400 (Knight's numbering) run from "One summer evening (led by her) I found" to "By day, and were a trouble to my dreams."
  - Both texts agree word for word in the passage.
  - **Which version AQA uses:** AQA's papers confirm only the title. I did not open an AQA page that gives the version or line numbers. The opening line and this 44-line span match the 1850 text. **CONFLICT:** both 1850-based texts read "Within a rocky cave". Knight's footnote says: "Dr. Cradock suggested the reading "rocky cove." Rocky cave is tautological, and Wordsworth would hardly apply the epithet to an ordinary boat-house." Many GCSE copies print "rocky cove" (seen in search snippets only, not verified). **The student should check which word his anthology prints.**
- **What happens:** One summer evening the young Wordsworth takes a boat that isn't his and rows out on a lake, proud and excited, fixing his eyes on a ridge. As he rows, a huge black mountain peak appears from behind the ridge and seems to grow and follow him. Frightened, he rows back and returns the boat. For days afterwards his mind is troubled by "huge and mighty forms" that replace his pleasant images of nature.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Nature's power humbles human pride: the confident rower is made small by the mountain. [power (nature), pride]
  - Guilt and conscience: the "act of stealth" seems to be punished by nature. [guilt, fear]
  - Memory and growing up: an adult remembers how a childhood experience changed his mind. [memory, identity]
  - Fear changes how he sees the world, with lasting effects on his thoughts and dreams. [fear, effects of conflict (internal conflict)]
- **Key quotations (6).** All are verified in both the 1850 Moxon scan and Gutenberg/Knight (URLs above).
  1. "(led by her)" | parenthesis: "her" is Nature (the preceding lines speak of "Nature" and "her aim"), so Nature is guiding him | 1850 / Gutenberg
  2. "an act of stealth / And troubled pleasure" | oxymoron: excitement mixed with guilt from the start | 1850 / Gutenberg
  3. "She was an elfin pinnace" | metaphor: the boat is magical and delicate, showing a child's romantic view and pride | 1850 / Gutenberg
  4. "a huge peak, black and huge" | repetition and dark colour: the sudden scale and menace of the mountain | 1850 / Gutenberg
  5. "Upreared its head" / "Strode after me" | personification: the mountain behaves like a living giant hunting him | 1850 / Gutenberg
  6. "huge and mighty forms, that do not live / Like living men" | the lasting effect: his mind is haunted, and pleasant images are replaced by fear and awe | 1850 / Gutenberg
  - Extra: "Of unknown modes of being" and "no colours of green fields". The tone shifts from "glittering" and "sparkling light" to "darkness".
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Form:** a first-person autobiographical extract in blank verse, from an epic poem. Source: https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Prelude | "an autobiographical epic poem in blank verse by William Wordsworth, published posthumously in 1850." Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter; the first line has 10 syllables. This makes it read like natural, reflective speech.
  - **Structure / shift in tone:** calm and proud (glittering circles, "like a swan") → the peak "Upreared" (volta) → fearful retreat ("With trembling oars") → lasting disturbance. The tone moves from confidence to fear to reflection.
  - **Enjambment and caesura:** for example "I struck and struck again, / And growing still in stature the grim shape". Breathless run-on lines mirror his panic and the peak's growth.
  - **Personification of nature:** the mountain has "purpose of its own" and "measured motion like a living thing". Nature becomes a moral teacher or punisher.
  - **Ending:** it closes on the troubled mind ("were a trouble to my dreams") rather than the event. The real effect is internal, which is why it works as a "conflict" poem.
- **Context (AO3):**
  - Composed 1799–1805 and published in 1850 (Knight's edition). | Gutenberg URL above | "Composed 1799-1805.--Published 1850". Its 1850 Advertisement says: "The following Poem was commenced in the beginning of the year 1799, and completed in the summer of 1805."
  - Britannica gives a longer span of revision and confirms posthumous publication. **CONFLICT with the start date:** Britannica says about 1798, the Advertisement says 1799. | https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Prelude | Britannica says he "first began work on the poem in about 1798" and lists "four distinct manuscript versions (1798–99, 1805–06, 1818–20, and 1832–39)."
  - The poem is addressed to Coleridge. | Gutenberg URL above (1850 Advertisement) | "The Friend, to whom the present Poem is addressed, was the late SAMUEL TAYLOR COLERIDGE"
  - Its subject is how the poet's mind grew. | Britannica (The Prelude) | the poem examines "the narrator's development as a poet, the forces that shaped his imaginative powers, and his spiritual crisis and recovery."
  - Childhood in the Lake District and Romanticism. | https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Wordsworth | "sent off by guardian uncles to a grammar school at Hawkshead, a village in the heart of the Lake District." and "Lyrical Ballads (1798), written with Samuel Taylor Coleridge, helped launch the English Romantic movement." Shortly before the boat episode in Book I, the poem itself says he was "Fostered alike by beauty and by fear" (Gutenberg, Book I). Britannica quotes it as "Foster'd". This is a useful line for AO3 and AO1.
  - Location: Knight's note links the peak to real Lake District fells. | Gutenberg URL above | the "huge peak, black and huge … may he either the summit of Wetherlam, or of Pike o'Blisco." This is a speculative editor's note and does not need to be learnt.
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - power of nature: *Storm on the Island* (nature's attack defeats human confidence) and *Ozymandias* (human pride against something greater).
  - memory and fear: *Kamikaze* (a remembered moment changes a life) and *The Emigrée* (memory of place).
  - pride and guilt: *My Last Duchess* (pride, though human rather than natural power) and *Remains* (guilt that haunts the mind; compare "trouble to my dreams" with Remains' ending).
  - effects of conflict (internal): *Remains* and *War Photographer*.

## Exposure — Wilfred Owen (experiences of winter 1917; final version September 1918; published 1920)

- **Text checked against:**
  - Owen, *Poems* (1920, ed. Sassoon), Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1034/pg1034.txt
  - poets.org (public-domain text): https://poets.org/poem/exposure
  - **CONFLICT: the two texts differ.**
    - 1920: "knife us", "lingering stealth", "Nor ever suns", "To-night, His frost", "gray". It is also divided into parts I and II.
    - poets.org: "knive us", "fingering stealth", "Now ever suns", "To-night, this frost", "grey".
    - The Wilfred Owen Association critique quotes "knive us", which matches the modern text.
    - The AQA anthology wording is unverified. Check "His/this frost" and "fingering/lingering" against the anthology before quoting.
  - Poetry Foundation was blocked.
- **What happens:** Soldiers lie out at night in freezing trenches on the Western Front. They are awake, cold and waiting for an attack that never comes, and the refrain repeats "But nothing happens." The snow and wind are deadlier than the bullets. They half-dream of home but feel shut out from it, and they wonder whether they are dying. By morning the frost has killed some of them, and a burial party finds frozen faces.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Nature (the weather) is the real enemy and more deadly than the human enemy. [power (nature), conflict/war]
  - Boredom, waiting and pointlessness: "What are we doing here?" [effects of conflict]
  - Suffering and death of ordinary soldiers. [loss/grief, effects of conflict]
  - Home and memory are out of reach, and faith is in doubt ("For love of God seems dying"). [place/home, memory]
- **Key quotations (6).** Verified in both texts unless noted.
  1. "Our brains ache, in the merciless iced east winds that knive us" | personification (the wind as an attacker with a knife) and sibilance | poets.org. The 1920 text has "knife us". Williams notes it "echoes 'My heart aches'" from Keats.
  2. "But nothing happens." | refrain (short final line in four stanzas, 1, 3, 4 and 8): monotony, futility and waiting | both
  3. "What are we doing here?" | rhetorical question: doubt about the purpose of the war | both
  4. "Dawn massing in the east her melancholy army" | metaphor: even the dawn is an enemy army attacking them | both
  5. "Less deadly than the air that shudders black with snow" | comparison: the weather is deadlier than "flights of bullets" | both
  6. "All their eyes are ice" | metaphor in the final stanza: the dead are frozen | both
  - Extra: "Is it that we are dying?" and "on us the doors are closed" (shut out of home) are in both texts. "Pale flakes with fingering stealth" is the poets.org reading; 1920 has "lingering".
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Form/structure:** I checked this against poets.org. There are 8 stanzas of 5 lines. Lines 1–4 are long and the 5th is a short half-line. The pattern is ABBA pararhyme or half-rhyme (for example "knive us / nervous" and "silent / salient"), followed by the short final line. The rhyme never quite resolves, which is unsettling. | Williams: "There are some daring half-rhymes - 'knive us/nervous', 'nonchalance/happens' - which come off, as does the short, simple, hanging line at the end of each verse." (https://www.wilfredowen.org.uk/poetry/exposure)
  - **Circular structure:** it ends where it began, with nothing changed. | same URL | "The men appear trapped in a No Man's Land between life and death, and the poem's movement is circular. When it ends, they are exactly where they were in the first verse."
  - **Voice:** "we" and "our" form a collective first-person voice. The speaker speaks for all the soldiers, and the present tense makes the reader share the waiting.
  - **Sound:** sibilance in "Sudden successive flights of bullets streak the silence" imitates the hiss of wind and bullets. Ellipses ("…") in stanza 1 suggest exhaustion and time dragging.
  - **Dream-home section (stanzas 5–6):** a shift to warm imagery ("crusted dark-red jewels", "crickets jingle") that ends with "on us the doors are closed". Home rejects them, which deepens the despair.
- **Context (AO3):**
  - Based on Owen's experience of winter 1917; final version September 1918, weeks before his death. | https://www.wilfredowen.org.uk/poetry/exposure (Merryn Williams, Wilfred Owen Association) | "'Exposure' gives a worm's-eye view of the front line, based on Owen's experiences in the winter of 1917, and passive suffering is what it is all about." and "The final version of this poem belongs to September 1918, a few weeks before Owen was killed, and it is mature and brilliant work."
  - **CONFLICT on the date of writing:** Poetry Archive gives February 1918, while Williams gives the final version as September 1918. Both can be true if he drafted it in early 1918 and revised it later; this is not confirmed. | https://poetryarchive.org/poet/wilfred-owen/ | "'Exposure', written in February 1918, recreates the experience of fighting at the front, and puts the reader right there." An Oxford archive record ("begun December 1917, Scarborough … completed September 1918") appeared only in search snippets. The page was blocked (403), so it is not used.
  - Owen's own letter (4 February 1917) about freezing in the open. Mark Ford's LRB review of his letters quotes it. | https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v46/n18/mark-ford/sunday-best | the letter: "My feet ached until they could ache no more, and so they temporarily died." Ford also says the platoon's water "cans froze, so they had nothing to drink". Only these fragments could be extracted; the full sentence linking the letter to the poem was not captured.
  - Owen's life and death. | https://poetryarchive.org/poet/wilfred-owen/ | "In April 1917 he was blown into the air by a shell and was invalided out with shell-shock and trench fever." / "On November 4th Owen, aged twenty-five, was shot while helping his battalion to cross the Sambre canal." / "The poems that made Wilfred Owen famous were mostly published after his death in action a week before the end of the First World War."
  - Literary influences: Keats, and the song "Keep the Home Fires Burning". | wilfredowen.org.uk URL above | "'Our brains ache' echoes 'My heart aches', the first words of 'Ode to a Nightingale', by Owen's beloved Keats." and "He also has in mind Ivor Novello's song, 'Keep the home fires burning .... though your lads are far away they dream of home'."
  - Published posthumously in *Poems* (1920), edited by Sassoon. This comes from the Gutenberg record (title "Poems", author Wilfred Owen, "Author of introduction, etc.: Siegfried Sassoon"). The 1920 date is SINGLE SOURCE: it came from search-result text and was not confirmed on an opened page.
  - Yeats's objection (useful for AO3): Williams opens with Yeats's comment: "'Passive suffering is not a theme for poetry', wrote Yeats, attempting to justify his distaste for Owen."
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - power of nature: *Storm on the Island* (the weather as military attacker; "nothing happens" against "a huge nothing that we fear") and *The Prelude*.
  - conflict/war and the effects of conflict: *Bayonet Charge* (a soldier's experience; nature in battle) and *Remains* (soldiers' suffering, with a collective "we" at the start).
  - futility and questioning: *The Charge of the Light Brigade* (whether to question orders and the purpose of war; patriotic versus anti-war presentation).
  - loss/grief and home: *Poppies* (home and loss) and *Kamikaze* (the pull of home).
- **Common misreading to avoid:** none named by a reliable source. Williams's point is that the suffering is passive and "nothing happens" (not even a battle), and that the cold is the killer rather than the enemy.
