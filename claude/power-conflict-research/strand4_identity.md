## Poppies — Jane Weir (first published 2009 in the *Guardian* "Exit Wounds" commission — but see date conflict below)

- **Text checked against:** Full text, [PEARSON-QP20] (Edexcel exam booklet, 35 numbered lines). The PDF text extraction does not show stanza breaks; [PEARSON-MS20] says "the poem is written in four stanzas of unequal length". NOT checked against the AQA anthology itself — see wording flag in Gaps.
- **What happens:** A mother remembers the morning her son left home: she pins a poppy on his blazer, fusses over his collar and holds back her tears and the urge to hug him. After he has gone she goes into his empty bedroom, then follows a dove up to the war memorial in the churchyard, where she traces the names and listens for his voice. The poem never says outright that he has died, but the war memorial and Armistice Sunday setting strongly suggest loss.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - A parent's grief and fear when a child goes to war — the "home front" side of conflict. [effects of conflict, loss/grief]
  - Memory: the poem moves between the present, the departure and his childhood. [memory]
  - A mother's identity — the "brave" face she must wear vs the private feeling she suppresses. [identity, fear]
  - Remembrance and national ritual (poppies, war memorial) set against private family love. [patriotism, loss/grief]
- **Key quotations** (all verified in [PEARSON-QP20]):
  1. "spasms of paper red" | metaphor/colour imagery: the poppy's petals twitch like a body in pain — remembrance foreshadows injury.
  2. "Sellotape bandaged around my hand" | military/medical lexis in a domestic act ("bandaged") — home life already invaded by war's language.
  3. "steeled the softening / of my face" | contrast of hard/soft: she forces herself to hide emotion; tension between public bravery and private grief.
  4. "play at / being Eskimos like we did when / you were little" | memory/flashback: tender childhood play contrasts with the adult soldier he has become. (Word as printed in source; teachers may wish to discuss the term.)
  5. "All my words / flattened, rolled, turned into felt" | textile metaphor + tricolon: her language fails; words go soft and silent.
  6. "released a song bird from its cage" | symbolism: letting go of her son / his freedom (or his soul) — ambiguous.
  7. "leaned against it like a wishbone" | simile: fragile, about to snap; a wish (that he is safe) that may not come true.
  8. "your playground voice catching on the wind" | ending on sound/memory: she can only hear the child he was; no closure.
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - Dramatic monologue addressed to "you" (her son) — [PEARSON-MS20]: "It is a mother’s dramatic monologue directly addressed to her son". The one-sided voice means we only ever get her feelings; his silence adds to the sense of loss.
  - Free verse, four stanzas of unequal length ([PEARSON-MS20]: "the poem is written in four stanzas of unequal length and is in free verse"). No fixed rhythm mirrors grief that cannot be ordered or contained.
  - Caesura and enjambment ([PEARSON-MS20]: "caesura is used to disrupt the flow of the poem ... Enjambement is also used to convey the flow of the mother’s emotions"). E.g. mid-line breaks around "Before you left," — her thoughts stop and start like suppressed sobs.
  - Textile/sewing lexis ("bias binding", "felt", "tucks, darts, pleats", "ornamental stitch") — domestic, maternal craft imagery; [PEARSON-MS20] links this to Weir being "a textile designer". Shows how she processes war through the language of home.
  - Non-chronological time ([PEARSON-MS20]: "Three different times are included in the poem: the present, the specific moment and the past ... The poem is in non-chronological order"). Mirrors how grief makes memory loop.
- **Context (AO3):**
  1. Duffy (Poet Laureate) commissioned war poetry, published in the *Guardian* on 25 July 2009, in response to Afghanistan and the Iraq inquiry. | [WOA] | "With the conflict in Afghanistan escalating and the Iraq inquiry pending, poet laureate Carol Ann Duffy commissions war poetry for today" (WOA quoting the Guardian headline; WOA gives "Guardian, 25 July 2009").
  2. *Poppies* was one of the poems in that commission, *Exit Wounds*. | [PEARSON-MS20] | "the poem is from a collection of contemporary poems entitled Exit Wounds and published in 2009. The collection was commissioned by Carol Ann Duffy in response to the growing unrest in Afghanistan and Iraq". **SINGLE SOURCE** for Poppies' inclusion (WOA does not list poets; the Guardian page could not be opened).
  3. Duffy had become Poet Laureate in 2009, the first woman in the role. | [BRIT-DUFFY] | "In 2009–19 she served as the first woman poet laureate of Great Britain."
  4. Weir has a background in textile design, which explains the sewing imagery. | [WEM-WEIR] | "I have extensive experience of working with major archives, specifically in the area of textile design, fashion and costume." Also [PEARSON-MS20]: "Jane Weir is a textile designer and many references to textiles are made in the poem".
  5. Weir was born in Manchester in 1963 and is Anglo-Italian. | [WEM-WEIR] | "Born in Manchester 1963. I m Anglo-Italian I have and English Mother and an Italian Father." [PA-WEIR]: "Jane Weir is an Anglo-Italian writer and designer."
  6. Armistice/Remembrance Sunday and the poppy. | [PEARSON-MS20] | "Armistice Sunday, also known as Remembrance Day, is the Sunday closest to 11 November. The end of the First World War was on 11 November 1918 and Armistice Sunday remembers all those who lost their lives during that war and all others. Poppies are symbolic of remembrance." (IWM page blocked — SINGLE SOURCE in this session.)
  - Author statement: GAP — no interview with Weir about *Poppies* found on an allowed source (claims that she wrote as "the mother of two teenage boys" appear only on revision sites, not used).
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Effects of conflict on those at home / family: **Kamikaze** (family response to a soldier; told by a family member, not the soldier); **War Photographer** (the person left to process others' grief).
  - Loss/grief and memory: **Remains** (memory that won't go away); **The Emigrée** (memory of a lost person/place kept alive in the speaker's mind).
  - Contrast — glorious vs private view of war: **The Charge of the Light Brigade** (public, patriotic commemoration vs one mother's private grief).
- **Common misreading to avoid:** omitted (no allowed source names one).

## The Emigrée — Carol Rumens (1993, *Thinking of Skins*, Bloodaxe)

- **Text checked against:** Full text, [PEARSON-SUPP] p.21, headed "The Émigrée" and dated "Carol Rumens (1993)". 25 lines in three stanzas (lines 1–8, 9–16, 17–25). NOT checked against the AQA anthology — see first-line wording flag in Gaps.
- **What happens:** The speaker left her home country as a child and cannot go back. Although she hears bad news about it (war, tyranny, the language banned), her childhood memory of the city stays bright and full of sunlight. In the final stanza the city "comes to" her and she cares for it, while people in her new city surround her, accuse her and mutter threats.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Memory is more powerful than present reality: her childhood image of home cannot be broken by bad news. [memory, place/home]
  - Identity and language: the home tongue is part of who she is, even if the state bans it. [identity]
  - Exile and not belonging: she is an outsider in her new "free city". [identity, place/home, fear]
  - Conflict and tyranny as the hidden cause — war, tanks, frontiers, the state. [conflict/war, power (human)]
- **Key quotations (ANCHOR set of 8)** (all verified in [PEARSON-SUPP]):
  1. "my memory of it is sunlight-clear" | compound adjective/light imagery: memory is vivid and idealised.
  2. "the bright, filled paperweight" | metaphor: her memory is a sealed, perfect snapshot — beautiful but frozen and enclosed.
  3. "It may be at war, it may be sick with tyrants" | repetition + personification of the country as ill: she admits the reality but dismisses it.
  4. "I am branded by an impression of sunlight" | metaphor "branded": the memory is permanently burned into her identity (positive but also painful).
  5. "as time rolls its tanks" | metaphor/personification: time itself becomes a military invasion; conflict intrudes on memory.
  6. "like a hollow doll, opens and spills a grammar" | simile (like a nesting doll): her childhood language keeps unpacking inside her.
  7. "It lies down in front of me, docile as paper" | personification/simile: the city becomes a vulnerable child or pet she protects — role reversal.
  8. "my shadow falls as evidence of sunlight" | final line, paradox: even her darkness/otherness proves the light of her memory survives; defiant ending.
  Also usable: "They accuse me of being dark in their free city." (line 23) — hostility/irony of "free".
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - First-person voice, past and present tense ("I left it as a child"; "I have no passport") — personal testimony makes exile feel lived, while never naming the country (universalises it).
  - Three stanzas each ending with "sunlight" (lines 8 "sunlight.", 16 "It tastes of sunlight.", 25 "evidence of sunlight.") — checked in [PEARSON-SUPP]. Refrain-like repetition shows memory returning no matter what; light imagery contrasts with darker images of war and "the city / of walls".
  - Opening "There was once a country…" (Pearson text) — fairy-tale formula + ellipsis: the homeland is a story, possibly lost or unreal. (Wording differs in other versions — see Gaps.)
  - Shift in the final stanza: from memory to a threatening present ("They accuse me ... they circle me ... They mutter death") — short clauses and repeated "They" build menace; the city is now protected "behind me".
  - Free verse, no regular rhyme scheme (checked against text) — mixes long flowing lines with abrupt sentences; mirrors a mind moving between idealised past and harsh present.
- **Context (AO3):**
  1. Published 1993 in *Thinking of Skins: New & Selected Poems*, Bloodaxe Books, Newcastle upon Tyne. | [OL-SKINS] | Record gives "Publisher: Bloodaxe Books", "Publish Date: 1993", title "Thinking of skins: new & selected poems". Poem date also given as "Carol Rumens (1993)" in [PEARSON-SUPP]. (That *The Emigrée* is in *Thinking of Skins* specifically: GAP — Open Library has no contents list; the 1993 date is double-sourced only for the poem/collection separately.)
  2. The collection deals with exile and displacement, often in Eastern Europe/Russia. | [OL-SKINS] summary | quoted phrase: "love, separation, death and displacement" (record summary). SINGLE SOURCE.
  3. Rumens was born in 1944 in Forest Hill, South London — she is not herself an émigrée, so the poem is a voice/persona. | [PA-RUMENS] | "Carol Rumens, nee Lumley, was born in Forest Hill, South London." (page shows "B. 1944").
  4. She has a special interest in Central and Eastern European and Russian literature. | [PA-RUMENS] | "She has a particular interest in the literary cultures of Central and Eastern Europe." and "Rumens's engagement with the poetry of Russia has extended to an on-going investment in translation."
  5. She has taught widely, including in Belfast. | [PA-RUMENS] | "She has taught at the University of Kent at Canterbury, Queen's University Belfast, University College Cork, University of Stockholm, and the University of Hull."
  - Author statement: GAP — no Rumens comment on this poem found (her Guardian columns could not be opened). Claims linking the poem to the former Yugoslavia/Soviet bloc appear only on revision sites — not used.
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Identity/place: **Checking Out Me History** (identity under pressure from a dominant culture); **Tissue** (fragile documents/borders vs human identity).
  - Memory: **Poppies** (memory of a lost loved one); **Remains** (memory that dominates the present — but traumatic, not sunlit).
  - Power of tyrants / the state: **Ozymandias** (tyranny that does not last); **London** (people oppressed by those in power).
- **Common misreading to avoid:** omitted (no allowed source names one).

## Kamikaze — Beatrice Garland (collection *The Invention of Fireworks*, 2013 per WJEC — see conflicts)

- **Text checked against:** Full text, [AQA-PAPER-K] (AQA's own exam paper; 42 numbered lines, seven 6-line stanzas). Best-quality verification of the three poems.
- **What happens:** A woman tells her children about her father, a Japanese kamikaze (suicide) pilot in the Second World War. Halfway to his target he looks down at the fishing boats and shoals of fish, remembers his childhood by the sea, and turns back. When he returns, his wife, neighbours and eventually his children treat him as if he no longer exists, and he wonders which was "the better way to die".
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Duty, honour and national expectation vs an individual's choice to live. [patriotism, power (human), pride]
  - The power of nature and memory to change a mind. [power (nature), memory]
  - Effects of conflict on a family across generations — shame and silence. [effects of conflict, identity, guilt]
  - Social death: being rejected is presented as a kind of death. [loss/grief]
- **Key quotations** (all verified in [AQA-PAPER-K]):
  1. "a shaven head / full of powerful incantations" | imagery of ritual/brainwashing: the pilot is filled with propaganda and duty.
  2. "enough fuel for a one-way / journey into history" | enjambment + euphemism: suicide is presented as glory ("history"); the line break enacts the one-way journey.
  3. "little fishing boats / strung out like bunting" | simile: festive, homely image — life and celebration pull him back.
  4. "the dark shoals of fishes / flashing silver" | sibilance/visual imagery: nature is beautiful and alive, contrasting with his mission.
  5. "a tuna, the dark prince, muscular, dangerous" | metaphor/list: nature's power and nobility; perhaps a version of the warrior he was meant to be.
  6. "my mother never spoke again / in his presence" | shift to first person ("my"): the family's punishment is silence.
  7. "they treated him / as though he no longer existed" | social rejection = a living death.
  8. "which had been the better way to die" | final line, rhetorical/reported question: bitter irony — choosing life led to a different death.
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - Narrative with a frame: the daughter's story is reported ("she thought, / recounting it later to her children") in third person, then shifts to the daughter's own words ("my mother", "we children") in stanza 6. [WJEC-K]: "‘Kamikaze’ is a narrative poem mostly told in the third person through the reported speech of the pilot’s daughter." The pilot never speaks for himself — his thoughts are imagined ("he must have"), showing how he is silenced.
  - Regular form: seven stanzas of six lines (checked against [AQA-PAPER-K]); [WJEC-K]: "There is no set rhythm or rhyme." Regular stanza shape vs free rhythm — order of duty vs flow of memory.
  - Very few sentences: [WJEC-K]: "There are only three sentences in the poem with each one marking a significant moment in the action and time." (Checked: sentences end at lines 30, 40 and 42.) The long first sentence (30 lines) flows like his memory/flight; the short final one lands hard.
  - Dashes/parenthesis "– yes, grandfather’s boat –" (line 25) — an oral, storytelling voice: the daughter corrects herself for her children, showing the story passed down generations.
  - Contrast of rich, sensory nature imagery (stanzas 2–5) with the bare, plain language of the last two stanzas — the beauty that saved him vs the coldness of his rejection.
- **Context (AO3):**
  1. Kamikaze pilots were Japanese pilots in WW2 who deliberately crashed into enemy targets. | [BRIT-K] | "Kamikaze, any of the Japanese pilots who in World War II made deliberate suicidal crashes into enemy targets, usually ships."
  2. The word means "divine wind", recalling a typhoon that saved Japan from a Mongol invasion. | [BRIT-K] | "The word kamikaze means 'divine wind,' a reference to a typhoon that fortuitously dispersed a Mongol invasion fleet threatening Japan from the west in 1281."
  3. Kamikaze attacks were most common from October 1944 to the end of the war. | [BRIT-K] | quoted phrase: "most prevalent from the Battle of Leyte Gulf, October 1944, to the end of the war." 
  4. Such deaths were seen as honourable and patriotic. | [WJEC-K] | "It was considered to be an honourable and patriotic way to die and over 3,000 kamikaze pilots died doing this in WW2." SINGLE SOURCE (the "3,000" figure is not confirmed by Britannica text seen).
  5. Garland was born in 1938 and worked as a clinician/teacher; she writes about others' experiences. | [WJEC-K] | "Beatrice Garland is an English poet born in Surrey in 1938." and "Beatrice Garland has said: “I spend a lot of the day listening to other people’s worlds”. In this poem she tells a story, told by someone else about an event and culture that is outside her own experience as a teacher and clinician." SINGLE SOURCE (her own site was down).
  6. Collection and date. | [WJEC-K] | "The poem was written in 2013 and is part of the poetry collection ‘The Invention of Fireworks’." SINGLE SOURCE — see conflicts.
  - Author statement: only the WJEC-quoted line above. A video interview ("Mr Bruff meets Beatrice Garland") is mentioned in [WJEC-K] but is a tutor channel — not used. GAP.
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Patriotism/duty questioned: **The Charge of the Light Brigade** (blind obedience praised vs questioned); **Bayonet Charge** (a soldier's patriotic ideals collapse mid-action).
  - Power of nature: **Storm on the Island**, **Extract from The Prelude** (nature changes a person's outlook).
  - Effects of conflict on families/identity: **Poppies** (family member's view of a soldier); **Remains** (guilt and psychological aftermath).
- **Common misreading to avoid:** Advice-adjacent, sourced: [WJEC-K] itself states "Kamikaze pilots were responsible for the attack on Pearl Harbour in 1941" — this conflicts with [BRIT-K], which dates kamikaze attacks as most prevalent from October 1944. Do NOT use the Pearl Harbor claim.

## Gaps and conflicts

1. **Poppies date — CONFLICT.** Pearson's exam booklet [PEARSON-QP20] prints "Jane Weir (2005)" under the poem, but Pearson's own mark scheme [PEARSON-MS20] says it is from *Exit Wounds* "published in 2009", and [WOA] dates the Duffy commission to the Guardian, 25 July 2009. 2005 is the year of Weir's collection *The Way I Dressed During the Revolution* per [PA-WEIR] — possibly the source of the confusion. Recommend: say "published 2009, commissioned by Carol Ann Duffy". Check the date the AQA anthology prints.
2. **Poppies in Exit Wounds — SINGLE SOURCE** (Pearson mark scheme). The Guardian original and IWM library record ("Exit wounds : with the conflict in Afghanistan escalating...", iwm.org.uk object 1500107000) were blocked. GAP: no Weir interview/author statement on an allowed source.
3. **Poppies wording:** Pearson prints line 26 "and this is where it led me,"; some online versions read "where it has led me" (seen in search snippets only, not verified). Check against the AQA anthology. Stanza breaks not visible in PDF extraction (Pearson MS: four unequal stanzas).
4. **The Emigrée first line — CONFLICT/UNVERIFIED.** Pearson prints "There was once a country…"; many online sources (revision sites, not opened/allowed) give "There once was a country…". The AQA anthology text was not available. Mark the first line "UNVERIFIED — check against anthology" before he memorises it. Also title accent: Pearson uses "The Émigrée" in contents/heading but "The Emigrée" in acknowledgements; AQA paper lists "The Emigrée".
5. **The Emigrée in *Thinking of Skins*:** collection (Bloodaxe, 1993) verified; that the poem is *in* it is not on any allowed page opened. No author statement found (Guardian blocked).
6. **Kamikaze publication/prize — GAP.** WJEC says "written in 2013" (collection date). Claims that *Kamikaze* won/placed in the National Poetry Competition (2006) or that Garland won the NPC in 2001 with 'undressing' are only in search snippets/revision sites; Poetry Society pages returned 403 and the poet's site returned 503. 7. **WJEC error:** the WJEC sheet's Pearl Harbor claim conflicts with Britannica (see Kamikaze section). Minor: WJEC quotes "'til gradually" — the AQA paper prints "till gradually".
8. **Kamikaze line 25** punctuation on the AQA paper begins with a hyphen "- yes, grandfather's boat – safe" (likely typesetting); WJEC prints an en dash. Immaterial for quoting.
