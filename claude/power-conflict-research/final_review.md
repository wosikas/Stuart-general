## FIX

**1. Ozymandias: paraphrase in quotation marks (rule e)** (Key quotations table, "Look on my works" row)
- Current: `He meant "despair at my greatness"; now it reads as a warning that their works will fall too.`
- Replace: `He meant them to despair at his greatness; now it reads as a warning that their works will fall too.`

**2. Poppies: non-verbatim phrase in quotation marks (rule e)** (Big ideas)
- Current: `the "home front" side of conflict;`
- Replace: `the home front side of conflict (the families left at home);`

**3. War Photographer: misreads the last two lines (c)** (What happens)
- In the poem, he stares "at where / he earns his living", and "they" (the readers) "do not care". He is not looking down at the people who do not care.
- Current: `and from the aeroplane he looks down "impassively" at people who "do not care".`
- Replace: `and from the aeroplane he stares "impassively" at where he earns his living, while the readers back home "do not care".`

**4. Exposure: wrong tense, so it misreads the ending (c)** (What happens)
- The poem says "Tonight, His frost will fasten on this mud and us". The deaths and the burial party are predicted, not reported.
- Current: `By morning the frost has killed some of them, and a burial party finds their frozen faces.`
- Replace: `The last stanza predicts that tonight the frost will kill some of them, and a burial party will find their frozen faces.`

**5. Checking Out Me History: false claim about the text (c, d)** (Methods, Irony bullet)
- "1066" and "ole King Cole" are not beside each other. "1066" is paired with "Dick Whittington and he cat". "ole King Cole" comes in the Florence Nightingale stanza. The AQA quotation itself is correct.
- Current: `**Irony:** putting "1066" beside "ole King Cole" treats official history like a nursery rhyme.`
- Replace: `**Irony:** listing "1066" next to "Dick Whittington and he cat", and Florence Nightingale next to "ole King Cole", treats official history like a nursery rhyme.`

**6. The Emigrée: possibilities stated as facts (c)** (What happens)
- The poem says "It may be at war, it may be sick with tyrants" and "It may by now be a lie, banned by the state". These are things that may be true, not news she has received.
- Current: `She hears bad news about it (war, tyrants, her language banned), but her childhood memory stays bright and full of sunlight.`
- Replace: `She hears bad news about it, and it may now be at war, ruled by tyrants, with her language banned, but her childhood memory stays bright and full of sunlight.`

**7. The Emigrée: matching quote gloss (c)** (Key quotations, "It may be at war" row)
- Current: `Repetition: she admits the reality but will not let it change her memory.`
- Replace: `Repetition of "may": she admits things may have gone wrong but will not let it change her memory.`

**8. Kamikaze: quotation attached to the wrong people (c)** (What happens)
- In the poem, it is "the neighbours" who treat him "as though he no longer existed". The children learn "to live as though / he had never returned".
- Current: `When he returns, his wife, the neighbours and finally his children treat him "as though he no longer existed".`
- Replace: `When he returns, his wife never speaks to him again, the neighbours treat him "as though he no longer existed", and finally even his children learn to act as if he had never come back.`

## CARE (judgement calls; optional changes)

**Readability (f)**
1. **AO1, AO2 and AO3 are not explained until "Every past Q26 question", but they appear earlier in "How to use this guide".** Suggest glossing them there: AO1 = your ideas and references; AO2 = the poet's methods; AO3 = context.
2. **Literary terms are used without a definition.**
   - Undefined: sibilance, enjambment, personification, oxymoron, imperative, refrain, half-rhyme, free verse, paradox, alliteration, "Romantic" (which a beginner may read as "romantic").
   - Already defined: blank verse, dramatic monologue, strafe, salvo.
   - Suggest a 10-line glossary near the top.
3. **The doc is long (about 59 KB).** It is well structured, so fine as a reference, but suggest saying "read the three anchor poems first, the rest later".
4. **"Storm on the Island (nature, and your backup)" is unclear.** Suggest: "and the partner to use when the printed poem is Ozymandias or Remains".
5. **"Check the question first" says "If the question is about power, Ozymandias is usually the best partner".** It doesn't say what to do when Ozymandias is the printed poem. Suggest adding "(if Ozymandias is printed, use Storm on the Island or My Last Duchess)".
6. **The Storm exam-history note says to use Storm for nature, fear or conflict questions "rather than political power".** The table, however, recommends Storm with Ozymandias for a power question. That pairing is backed by the specimen and 2018 mark schemes, but the two pieces of advice read as contradictory. Suggest: "rather than questions about political power and control (like London's)".

**Accuracy and wording (a, c, e)**
7. **The heading "Every past Q26 question" is not quite right.** In Nov 2021 and June 2022 it was Q02 on paper 8702/1P. The table does say so.
8. **"A weak second poem was 'the problem in this exam'".** The source has a capital T ("The problem in this exam frequently came from the second poem"). The lower-case version in quotation marks is trivial but not exact. Also, "weak" is the doc's word.
9. **"So this answer is moving from Level 5 into Level 6".** This is an inference, since AQA gives no mark. Suggest "This suggests the answer is moving…".
10. **Bayonet Charge: "usually read as the First World War".** No source in the files says this. verify3 recommended this hedged wording, so it is acceptable.
11. **Storm on the Island: "Some readers see the war language as hinting at conflict in Northern Ireland".** This reading appears only on revision sites; no allowed source makes it. It is hedged well already.
12. **London, "Marks of weakness": '"marks" are scars'.** This is a stretch; "signs" or "traces" is safer.
13. **Tissue, What happens: "She imagines…".** The speaker's gender is never given. Suggest "The speaker imagines…".
14. **Checking Out Me History, Context: "so Agard grew up and went to school under British rule".** This is an inference from his birth in 1949 and independence in 1966. It is reasonable but not stated by a source.
15. **Charge of the Light Brigade: "a falling beat".** The metre is not sourced (verify3 left "dactylic" unverified). It is fine as a description.
16. **War Photographer: "first published in *Standing Female Nude* in 1985".** The collection is confirmed by three sources. That it was the poem's *first* publication is a slight stretch.
17. **Exposure context gives only the Wilfred Owen Association dates.** The Poetry Archive says "written in February 1918", and that conflict isn't mentioned. This is fine for a student, but the doc is not wrong to leave it out.
18. **Kamikaze exam history ("June 2024").** The PMT paper is coded 8702/2R with the footer "IB/G/Jun23". verify4 judged "June 2024" plausible.
19. **Sources, PMT link: https://www.physicsandmathstutor.com/past-papers/gcse-english-literature/aqa-paper-2/** This URL does not appear in any research file. exam_materials.md used pmt.physicsandmathstutor.com/download/English-Literature/GCSE/Past-Papers/AQA/Paper-2/… file links. Suggest swapping in the verified file links or checking that the page opens.
20. **Quotation marks around "some readers suggest" (Storm on the Island context).** This is wording for the student to use, not a quotation. It is OK, but strictly it breaks the doc's own rule that quotation marks mean copied text.
21. **Checking Out Me History summary spells it "Nanny de Maroon"; the anthology has "Nanny de maroon".** It is not in quotation marks, so this is trivial.
22. **Ozymandias, if printed:** remind the student that the paper's spelling may differ from the anthology (2018 printed "shattered", "mocked").
