Note on apostrophes: AQA prints curly apostrophes (’). They are written here as ' for ease of typing; the words are unchanged.

## Ozymandias — Percy Bysshe Shelley (published 11 January 1818, *The Examiner*) — ANCHOR

- **Text checked against:**
  - **AQA's printed text** (the anthology wording): AQA-SQP, Q26: https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/resources/english/AQA-87022-SQP.PDF (full poem printed).
  - poets.org (Academy of American Poets): https://poets.org/poem/ozymandias (full text; matches the AQA wording except that AQA spells "shatter'd", "stamp'd", "mock'd" and poets.org has "shattered", "stamped", "mocked").
  - Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto): https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/ozymandias (full text of the **1818 *Examiner* version**). **FLAG, different wording:** "wrinkled lips" (AQA: "wrinkled lip"); "My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings, / Look on my Works ye Mighty, and despair!" with no inner quotation marks (AQA: "'My name is Ozymandias, king of kings: / Look on my works, ye mighty, and despair!'"); "colossal Wreck" (AQA: "colossal wreck"). **Use the AQA wording in the exam.**
  - Poetry Foundation and Bartleby both returned 403 (blocked), so they were not used.
- **What happens:** The speaker says he met a traveller who had been to an ancient land. The traveller described a huge, broken statue of a king lying in the desert: just two legs standing, and a cracked face with a cruel sneer. On its base the king boasts that other mighty rulers should despair at his works, but nothing is left around it except empty sand.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Human power doesn't last: the king who called himself "king of kings" is now a broken ruin. *[power (human), pride]*
  - Time and nature (the "lone and level sands") are stronger than any ruler. *[power (nature)]*
  - Pride and arrogance: the tyrant's own boast becomes the joke against him. *[pride, power (human)]*
  - Art outlasts power: the sculptor's skill, and the poem itself, survive while the empire has gone. Britannica: "it is the artist's perspective (including Shelley's) that has endured" (see Context). *[memory, power (human)]*
- **Key quotations (8, ANCHOR).** All are verbatim from the AQA text (AQA-SQP); line numbers are AQA's.
  1. "I met a traveller from an antique land" (l.1) | Frame narrative: the story reaches us second-hand, through a speaker and then a traveller, so the king is far away in time and place. "antique" stresses how ancient, and how forgotten, he is. | AQA-SQP; poets.org
  2. "Two vast and trunkless legs of stone" (l.2) | Imagery: "vast" shows his size and ambition, but "trunkless" shows the statue is broken and the body has gone. Power is reduced to fragments. | AQA-SQP
  3. "Half sunk, a shatter'd visage lies" (l.4) | Verbs of collapse ("sunk", "shatter'd", "lies"): the ruler's face, the symbol of his identity, is sinking into the desert. | AQA-SQP (poets.org: "shattered")
  4. "sneer of cold command" (l.5) | Alliteration of hard "c" sounds: it captures the tyrant's cruelty and contempt for his people, which the sculptor recorded. | AQA-SQP; RPO; poets.org
  5. "The hand that mock'd them and the heart that fed" (l.8) | Parallel structure ("The hand… the heart…"). The sculptor's hand "mock'd" (copied, and also ridiculed) the king's passions. Art judges the tyrant. | AQA-SQP (poets.org: "mocked")
  6. "'My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:" (l.10) | Direct speech on the pedestal: an arrogant title that sets himself above all other rulers. Dramatic irony, because the reader can see the ruin. | AQA-SQP (RPO 1818 text: "King of Kings,")
  7. "Look on my works, ye mighty, and despair!'" (l.11) | Imperative and exclamation: he commands other rulers to despair at his greatness. Now the only thing to "look on" is nothing, so the line turns against him. | AQA-SQP (RPO 1818: "Look on my Works ye Mighty, and despair!")
  8. "Nothing beside remains." (l.12) / "The lone and level sands stretch far away." (l.14) | A short blunt sentence with a caesura (a full stop mid-line) cuts off the boast. The final line's alliteration ("lone… level", "boundless and bare" in l.13) and long vowels show endless, empty time and space. Nature has the last word. | AQA-SQP; RPO; poets.org
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Form: a sonnet, 14 lines.** Britannica calls it "Shelley's sonnet" (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ozymandias-poem) and AQA's specimen mark scheme lists "Use of sonnet to explore ideas, possibly dealing with structural elements, such as use / effect of volta, rhyming couplet, line lengthening to stress particular ideas" (AQA-SMS). WHY: the sonnet is a tight, controlled form, used here for a ruler who wanted total control.
  - **Rhyme scheme: ABABACDCEDEFEF** (checked by me against the AQA text: land/stone/sand/frown/command/read/things/fed/appear/kings/despair/decay/bare/away). It fits neither the regular Petrarchan nor the Shakespearean pattern and has **no final rhyming couplet**. WHY (Advice, my interpretation): the broken or irregular pattern can mirror the broken statue and power that does not hold together. *Note: the rhyme scheme is my own check; an online claim that Shelley "blends" sonnet types came only from search snippets of non-allowed sites, so it is not cited.*
  - **Voice and perspective: layered narrators.** "I" (speaker), then the traveller ("Who said:"), then the king's own words on the pedestal. AQA: "use of perspective/person to present ideas about those in power, eg first person in 'Ozymandias'" (AQA-MS23). The June 2023 examiner report praised "consideration of the use of perspective to indicate the passage of time and thereby drawing attention to the 'antique'-ness of his tyrannical rule and all power eventually turning to dust" (AQA-ER23). WHY: each layer pushes the king further into the past.
  - **Irony and the turn:** the boast in ll.10–11 is immediately followed by "Nothing beside remains." AQA lists "use of person perspective to create irony" (AQA-MS18). WHY: the structure itself proves the boast false.
  - **Imagery of sand and time:** AQA lists "ideas about the use of sand as metaphor for the passing of time and impermanence of human constructions" (AQA-MS18). WHY: the poem ends on the desert, not the king, so nature outlasts human power.
- **Context (AO3):**
  1. Publication: first published in *The Examiner* on 11 January 1818, under a pseudonym. | https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ozymandias-poem | "Published on January 11, 1818, in *The Examiner*, the poem offers an ironic—and enduring—commentary on the fleeting nature of power." Pseudonym (two sources): Britannica: "Author: Percy Bysshe Shelley (published under the pseudonym Glirastes)". RPO, https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/ozymandias, says the poem appeared in *The Examiner*, no. 524 (11 January 1818), under the pseudonym "Glirastes".
  2. The Horace Smith contest: Shelley wrote it in friendly competition with Horace Smith, whose sonnet had the same title and was published on 1 February 1818 in the same paper. | https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ozymandias-poem | "Like Shelley's sonnet, Smith's was also titled "Ozymandias" and published in the politically radical *Examiner* on February 1, 1818." **Second source**, RPO: the poem was written in "friendly competition with Horace Smith, whose own sonnet of the same name was published Feb. 1, 1818, also in *The Examiner*."
  3. Ramesses II: "Ozymandias" is the Greek name for the pharaoh Ramesses II. | RPO: "Greek name for the Egyptian king Rameses II (1304-1237 BC)". Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/question/What-inspired-Percy-Bysshe-Shelley-to-write-Ozymandias): the two sonnets were both titled "Ozymandias", the Greek name for Ramses II (fetch summary; the exact wording was not captured). **Spelling and date conflict:** RPO says "Rameses II (1304-1237 BC)"; the Britannica topic page calls him the 13th-century-BCE pharaoh Ramses II (from a summary, not verbatim). Use "Ramesses II" (13th century BC) and avoid exact regnal dates.
  4. The statue in London: a colossal bust of Ramesses II reached the British Museum in 1818. | https://www.britannica.com/question/What-inspired-Percy-Bysshe-Shelley-to-write-Ozymandias | "The statue was among several ancient Egyptian artifacts that had been removed from the country as a result of Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798. The Ramses bust arrived at the British Museum in London in 1818, where it remains." **SINGLE SOURCE** (the British Museum's own page returned 403).
  5. The ancient inscription: the historian Diodorus Siculus recorded an inscription on a statue of Ozymandias. | RPO: Diodorus Siculus documented an inscription reading "King of Kings am I, Osymandias. If anyone would know how great I am and where I lie, let him surpass one of my works." **SINGLE SOURCE** for the wording (Britannica mentions Diodorus only in a paraphrased fetch).
  6. Shelley the radical: he was a radical Romantic who opposed monarchy and authority and was expelled from Oxford over a pamphlet on atheism. | https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ozymandias-poem | "Given Shelley's beliefs, "Ozymandias" is not merely a sonnet about a statue but a political statement by one of English literature's most radical voices." Also from the same page: he rejected "the monarchy, religion, war, and commerce". Britannica biography (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Percy-Bysshe-Shelley): "In March 1811, University College expelled both Shelley and Hogg for refusing to admit Shelley's authorship of a pamphlet called *The Necessity of Atheism*."
  7. Life dates: | https://www.britannica.com/biography/Percy-Bysshe-Shelley | "Percy Bysshe Shelley (born August 4, 1792, Field Place, near Horsham, Sussex, England—died July 8, 1822, at sea off Livorno, Tuscany [Italy])" and "With John Keats and Lord Byron, was a major figure among the second generation of English Romantic writers."
  - **Link to Tissue (author statement):** Imtiaz Dharker said of writing Tissue: "Somewhere in my mind I also had Shelley's poem 'Ozymandias' and its great image of a 'colossal wreck' toppled in the desert." (York Notes interview; see the Tissue section for the source caveat.)
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Power (human) / pride: **My Last Duchess** (another arrogant ruler revealed by his own words; AQA lists "Any valid comparisons dealing with despotism, such as 'My Last Duchess'", AQA-SMS). **London** (abusive power seen from below).
  - Power (nature) beats humans: **Extract from The Prelude**, **Storm on the Island** (both named in AQA-SMS and AQA-MS18).
  - Fragility of human-made things: **Tissue** (Dharker had Ozymandias in mind, see above).
- **Common misreadings to avoid (AQA-ER23, June 2023 examiner report):**
  - "What was less successful was where students attempted to say anything meaningful about Shelley's use of sonnet form, leading to comments such as 'he uses a sonnet because sonnets are love poems which shows Ozymandias loves himself'. This isn't particularly helpful as a line of argument…"
  - Narrating history instead of ideas: "some of the comments on the Duke of Ferrara and Lucrezia, or Rameses II, were not particularly useful in terms of developing a response to the effects of power in a thoughtful or considered way."

## London — William Blake (published 1794 in *Songs of Experience*; drafted 1792 by one source)

- **Text checked against:**
  - **AQA's printed text:** AQA-QP21, Q02: https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/sample-papers-and-mark-schemes/2021/november/AQA-87021P-QP-NOV21.PDF (full poem). **AQA modernises spelling and capitals:** "I wander through each chartered street", "The mind-forged manacles I hear:", "chimney-sweeper's", "church", "soldier's", "palace", "harlot's", "marriage hearse" (all lower case).
  - poets.org: https://poets.org/poem/london (original 1794 spelling: "I wander thro' each charter'd street", "mind-forg'd manacles", "blackning Church", capitals on Chimney-sweeper, Soldier, Palace, Harlot, Marriage).
  - Peter Cash, "William Blake: London", *The Use of English* (English Association): https://englishassociation.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Blake-London-63.2.pdf (a third version: "thro'", "chartered", "mind-forged", with capitals).
  - **Use the AQA wording in the exam.** Poetry Foundation and the Project Gutenberg reader returned 403 or would not reproduce the text, so they were not used.
- **What happens:** The speaker walks through London's streets and sees suffering on every face. He hears misery everywhere: crying men and babies, child chimney sweeps, soldiers, and at night a young prostitute whose curse spreads disease even into marriages. He blames the powerful institutions (church, palace, the "chartered" system) and also people's own "mind-forged" acceptance.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Abuse of power by institutions: Church, State/monarchy ("palace") and commerce/ownership ("chartered") are blamed for the suffering of the poor. *[power (human), conflict]*
  - Suffering is everywhere and everyone is trapped, even the "new-born". *[effects of conflict, loss/grief]*
  - Mental oppression: "mind-forged manacles" suggests people are chained by their own acceptance and fear. *[power (human), fear, identity]*
  - Place as a moral portrait: the city itself shows a corrupt society. *[place/home]*
- **Key quotations (6)**, verbatim from AQA-QP21 (line numbers are AQA's):
  1. "I wander through each chartered street," (l.1) | Repeated "chartered" (also "the chartered Thames", l.2): even the streets and the river are owned and controlled, showing commercial and legal power over everything. | AQA-QP21; poets.org ("thro' each charter'd")
  2. "Marks of weakness, marks of woe." (l.4) | Repetition and alliteration; a pun on "mark" (notice) and "marks" (scars). Suffering is written on every face. | AQA-QP21; poets.org
  3. "The mind-forged manacles I hear:" (l.8) | Metaphor: "manacles" are chains, but they are "mind-forged", so oppression is mental as well as physical. | AQA-QP21 (poets.org: "mind-forg'd")
  4. "Every black'ning church appalls," (l.10) | Imagery: soot-blackened churches. The Church is morally stained by ignoring child chimney sweeps. | AQA-QP21 (poets.org: "Every blackning Church appalls;")
  5. "Runs in blood down palace walls." (l.12) | Metaphor: the soldier's "sigh" turns to blood on the palace, so the monarchy is guilty of soldiers' deaths. | AQA-QP21; poets.org ("Palace")
  6. "And blights with plagues the marriage hearse." (l.16) | Oxymoron, "marriage hearse": marriage (new life) is joined to death. The corruption reaches every part of life; the plosives "blasts… blights" add anger. | AQA-QP21 (poets.org: "Marriage hearse")
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Form: four quatrains with alternate rhyme (ABAB).** Checked by me against the AQA text (street/flow/meet/woe; man/fear/ban/hear; cry/appalls/sigh/walls; hear/curse/tear/hearse). Cash: Blake "intends a sardonic conflict between the alternate rhymes of his ballad quatrains and the grotesque experience which they describe". WHY: a simple song-like form, like a nursery rhyme, makes the horrors more shocking.
  - **Metre: iambic tetrameter.** Cash: "by means of iambic tetrameter, repeatedly placing a stress on the first syllable of the word ['every'], he has conveyed a dynamic sense of the ubiquitous wretchedness". **SINGLE SOURCE.** WHY: the steady, relentless rhythm is like walking, and the misery never stops.
  - **Repetition ("every", "cry", "marks"):** Cash: "By his sixth use of 'every', he has established the comprehensive scale of the misery". AQA lists "use of particular techniques such as repetition, or structural features such as rhyme/stanza formation" (AQA-MS21). WHY: suffering is universal.
  - **Senses shift, from seeing to hearing:** "mark in every face" (sight), then "I hear" in ll.8 and 13. Cash: "It is instructive that this experience of the city should be auditory". WHY: the cries of the city get louder and more urgent.
  - **Ending:** the final stanza builds to the oxymoron "marriage hearse". Cash: "the powerful oxymoron 'marriage hearse' suggests that nobody is immune". WHY: it ends on disease and death infecting the future (the "new-born infant").
- **Context (AO3):**
  1. Publication: in *Songs of Experience*, 1794. | https://www.britannica.com/topic/London-poem-by-Blake | "First published in his collection *Songs of Experience* in 1794." Britannica's biography of Blake (https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Blake) also gives "Songs of Innocence (1789) and Songs of Experience (1794)". **CONFLICT:** Cash (English Association) titles the poem "London (1793)" and says *Songs of Experience* was "completed in 1793". Both can be true (completed 1793, issued 1794). Use **1794** for publication.
  2. Draft date: Blake drafted the poem in his Notebook in summer 1792. | British Library (Chinese-language site), https://www.britishlibrary.cn/en/works/notebook-of-william-blake/ | "William Blake composed his poem 'London' in the summer of 1792." Same page: "In this poem, Blake explores poverty, revolution and the power of the imagination." **SINGLE SOURCE** for 1792.
  3. Revisions in the draft, "dirty" to "chartered" and "German-forged" to "mind-forged": | Cash, englishassociation.ac.uk (URL above) | "research among Blake's manuscripts reveals that he actually changed his adjective from the literal 'dirty' to 'chartered'." and "Research reveals that Blake's original phrase was 'German-forged'". **SINGLE SOURCE** (the British Library's Untold Lives blog post on Blake and London returned 404, so it could not be checked).
  4. "Chartered" and Thomas Paine: Cash links "chartered" to *Rights of Man* (1791). | Cash | "In 1791, Thomas Paine (Rights of Man) wrote: It is a perversion of terms to say that a charter gives rights. It operates by a contrary effect—that of taking rights away."
  5. Blake and London: he was born and died in London and worked as an engraver. | https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Blake | "born Nov. 28, 1757, London, Eng.—died Aug. 12, 1827, London"; described as "an English engraver, artist, poet, and visionary".
  6. Child chimney sweeps (social context): | Cash | "Until The Chimney Sweepers Act of 1834, there was no effective legislation to protect children from this dangerous and dirty work." **SINGLE SOURCE**, and a teacher's commentary, not a historian.
  - **GAP:** no opened source directly links "London" to the French Revolution (the BL page says "revolution" only generally).
- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Power (human) and its abuse: **Ozymandias** (AQA-MS21 names it), **My Last Duchess** ("political power with personal power used to repress", AQA-MS21).
  - Reactions to being controlled: **Checking Out Me History**, **Kamikaze** (AQA-MS21).
  - Place: **The Emigrée**, **Tissue** ("use of places as representation of people", AQA-MS21).
- **Common misreading:** none named by an AQA or other allowed source. (The June 2023 examiner report warned generally that answers which "stayed within the particulars of the poems in 'My Last Duchess', 'Ozymandias' or 'London'" fared less well than those discussing the "responsibilities / abuse of power" (AQA-ER23).)

## My Last Duchess — Robert Browning (first published 1842, *Dramatic Lyrics*)

- **Text checked against:**
  - **AQA's printed text:** AQA-QP23, Q26: https://filestore.aqa.org.uk/sample-papers-and-mark-schemes/2023/june/AQA-87022-QP-JUN23-CR.PDF (full poem, subtitle "Ferrara", 56 numbered lines).
  - RPO (University of Toronto): https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/my-last-duchess (full text; minor differences: "I chuse" where AQA has "I choose"; "Oh, sir" where AQA has "Oh sir"; RPO ends "for me." and AQA ends "for me!").
  - poets.org: https://poets.org/poem/my-last-duchess (selected lines checked).
  - **Use the AQA wording.**
- **What happens:** The Duke of Ferrara shows a visitor a portrait of his dead wife, the "last Duchess". He complains that she smiled at everyone and was pleased by small things as much as by his ancient family name, and he hints that he "gave commands" and her smiles stopped, meaning he had her killed. The visitor is in fact arranging the Duke's next marriage to a Count's daughter, and the Duke ends by showing off another artwork.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Power and control: the Duke controls his wife (even now, behind a curtain) and speaks to control his listener. *[power (human)]*
  - Pride in status: his "nine-hundred-years-old name" matters more to him than his wife. *[pride, identity]*
  - Women as possessions: the Duchess, and the next bride, are treated like art objects and dowries. *[power (human), conflict]*
  - Jealousy and violence hidden under polite speech. *[conflict, guilt]*
- **Key quotations (6)**, verbatim from AQA-QP23 (line numbers are AQA's):
  1. "That's my last Duchess painted on the wall," (l.1) | Possessive "my" and "last" (as if one in a series) show ownership and coldness from the first line. | AQA-QP23; RPO; poets.org
  2. "The curtain I have drawn for you, but I)" (l.10) | Symbol and parenthesis: only the Duke may draw the curtain, so he controls who sees her even after death. | AQA-QP23; RPO
  3. "she liked whate'er / She looked on, and her looks went everywhere." (ll.23–24) | Enjambment and repetition ("looked… looks"): the Duke resents her free, friendly nature, which he sees as disloyalty. | AQA-QP23; RPO
  4. "My gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name" (l.33) | Hyphenated compound adjective: his pride in status and lineage. | AQA-QP23; RPO; poets.org
  5. "This grew; I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together." (ll.45–46) | Caesura (semicolons) and understatement: the chilling hint that he had her killed, told in a few blunt words. | AQA-QP23; RPO
  6. "Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse," (ll.54–55) | Symbol at the ending: the god taming a creature mirrors the Duke "taming" women. He moves straight on to another possession. | AQA-QP23; RPO
  - Extra if needed: "and I choose / Never to stoop." (ll.42–43, AQA-QP23; RPO spells "chuse"), showing pride and refusal to communicate.
- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Dramatic monologue:** Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/topic/My-Last-Duchess: "It is one of Browning's most successful dramatic monologues." Definition (M. H. Abrams, quoted by Baylor University's Armstrong Browning Library, https://blogs.baylor.edu/armstrongbrowning/2012/08/01/robert-browning-and-the-dramatic-monologue/): "A single person, who is clearly not the poet, utters the speech that makes up the whole of the poem, in a specific situation at a critical moment." AQA lists "possible treatment of use of structural/form elements, such as dramatic monologue" (AQA-MS23). WHY: the Duke reveals his cruelty without meaning to; we judge him through his own words.
  - **The silent listener:** the Duke's questions ("Will't please you sit and look at her?" l.5; "Will't please you rise?" l.47, AQA-QP23) show the envoy is present but never speaks. The Baylor page notes that the listener's responses are known only through the Duke's remarks. WHY: the Duke dominates the conversation just as he dominated his wife.
  - **Rhyming couplets in a single 56-line stanza:** Britannica: "poem of 56 lines in rhyming couplets". AQA's text is numbered to 56. Lines are ten syllables (e.g. l.1, my own count), i.e. iambic pentameter, so these are heroic couplets. *The term "heroic couplet / iambic pentameter" is from a Wikipedia search snippet only, so it is NOT CITED; check with a teacher.* WHY (Advice): the strict rhyme mirrors his control, while heavy **enjambment** (e.g. "I call / That piece a wonder", ll.2–3) makes it sound like natural, casual speech, hiding the control.
  - **Hesitations and dashes:** "A heart – how shall I say? – too soon made glad," (l.22); "Somehow – I know not how –" (l.32). WHY: the polished speaker briefly loses fluency when talking about her, showing jealousy and anger.
  - **Ending/structure:** the poem moves from the portrait to the dowry ("no just pretence / Of mine for dowry will be disallowed", ll.50–51) and ends on Neptune. The June 2023 examiner report praised discussion of "the impact of 'the curtain', the image of the statue of Neptune, and Browning's use of dramatic monologue to depict megalomania" (AQA-ER23).
- **Context (AO3):**

## Checking Out Me History — John Agard (text first published in *Half-Caste and Other Poems*, Hodder Children's, 2004, per the Poetry Archive)

- **Text checked against:**
  - Poetry Archive: https://poetryarchive.org/poem/checking-out-me-history/ (full text displayed; publication credit: "From Half-Caste and Other Poems (Hodder Children's, 2004), John Agard 2004, used by permission of the author c/o Caroline Sheldon Literary Agency.").
  - Children's Poetry Archive: https://childrens.poetryarchive.org/poem/checking-out-me-history/ (same credit).
  - **FLAG:** neither page showed any italic or indented stanzas (the fetch tool may not preserve italics). Whether the AQA anthology sets the Toussaint, Nanny and Mary Seacole sections in a different layout is **UNVERIFIED, so check the anthology**. AQA's June 2023 mark scheme spells the title "'Checkin' Out Me History'" (AQA-MS23), while the AQA paper list and the Poetry Archive use "Checking". **Use the anthology spelling.**
- **What happens:** The speaker, from the Caribbean, complains that his British-style schooling taught him about 1066, Lord Nelson, Florence Nightingale and nursery-rhyme characters, but never about Black and Caribbean heroes. In between, he celebrates figures he was not taught about, such as Toussaint L'Ouverture, Nanny de Maroon and Mary Seacole. He ends by declaring he is now finding out his own history and "carving out" his own identity.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Power of education and of whoever controls the story: the colonial curriculum erased his history. *[power (human), conflict]*
  - Identity: reclaiming his culture and history builds his sense of self. *[identity, memory]*
  - Resistance and rebellion against colonial and imposed power. *[conflict, pride]*
  - Anger, turning to pride and hope. *[pride, identity]*
- **Key quotations (6)**, verbatim from the Poetry Archive (short phrases only, in copyright):
  1. "Dem tell me" | Repeated refrain; "Dem" (the authorities/teachers) are vague and faceless. Repetition shows frustration and builds a chant. | Poetry Archive
  2. "Bandage up me eye with me own history" | Metaphor: education "bandaged" his eyes, blinding him. A bandage might seem to help but here it hides; dialect ("me eye") asserts his own voice. | Poetry Archive

  4. "Toussaint de beacon / of de Haitian Revolution" | Metaphor ("beacon"): a guiding light of freedom, set against the dull list of British history. | Poetry Archive
  5. "a healing star / among the wounded" | Metaphor of light and hope for Mary Seacole, contrasted with the British-taught "Florence Nightingale and she lamp". | Poetry Archive
  6. "I carving out me identity" | Final line: the metaphor "carving" (active, sculpting, permanent) shows him taking control. The present-tense dialect form shows it is ongoing. | Poetry Archive
  - Extras: "Dem tell me bout 1066 and all dat" (dismissive "and all dat" mocks the official history); "Dick Whittington and he cat" (nursery figures treated as history); "fire-woman struggle" (Nanny). All verified on the Poetry Archive.
