## Remains — Simon Armitage (published in *The Not Dead*, Pomona, 2008; the poems were first used in a Channel 4 film shown in 2007) — ANCHOR

- **Text checked against:** AQA-N20-QP (full text, 30 lines). The WJEC-R key quotations were also used to cross-check. They differ in a few places (see Gaps).
- **What happens (plain English):** A British soldier tells how he and two others on patrol shot a man running from a bank raid, who may or may not have been armed. The body is dumped in a lorry, and the soldier tries to treat it as "end of story". Back home on leave he cannot stop seeing the man in flashbacks and dreams, and drink and drugs do not help. He ends feeling that the man's death is in his hands for good.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - The guilt does not go away. A split-second decision becomes a memory he lives with for life. *[guilt, memory, effects of conflict]*
  - Moral doubt in modern war: "probably armed, possibly not". The poem never settles whether the killing was justified. *[conflict/war, guilt]*
  - Psychological damage (trauma) after the soldier comes home. The poem moves from the war zone to "home on leave". *[effects of conflict, fear, memory]*
  - The soldier moves from shared blame to being on his own with it. "we" / "all three of us" shifts to "I" and "my". *[identity, guilt, power (human)]*

- **Key quotations (8, ANCHOR)**: all verified word for word against AQA-N20-QP.

| # | Quotation | What it shows / method |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "On another occasion" | Opens in the middle (in medias res), as casual chat. This kind of killing sounds routine, one "occasion" among many. Conversational voice. |
| 2 | "probably armed, possibly not." | The central doubt, repeated in stanza 6 ("Sleep, and he's probably armed, possibly not."). The repetition shows the uncertainty going round and round in his head. |
| 3 | "myself and somebody else and somebody else" | Repeating "somebody else" spreads the blame. He does not name the others. Shared responsibility as a shield. |
| 4 | "I see every round as it rips through his life –" | A switch to the present tense, with violent alliteration ("round … rips"). The killing is relived as if it is happening now. |
| 5 | "tosses his guts back into his body" | A brutal, casual verb ("tosses") shows how soldiers become hardened to death and treat the dead man as an object. |
| 6 | "End of story, except not really." | The turning point (volta) of the poem. Blunt slang is undercut by "except not really": the story does not end. It is where the poem moves from the war zone to home and memory. |
| 7 | "dug in behind enemy lines," | A military metaphor. The memory is like an enemy soldier dug into a trench inside his mind, which he cannot remove. The war has followed him home. |
| 8 | "his bloody life in my bloody hands." | The last line. "bloody" is both literal (blood) and a swear-word of frustration. "my … hands" takes personal responsibility. WJEC-R notes an echo of Lady Macbeth (see Context/readings). |

  Extra lines you could use (verified, AQA-N20-QP): "His blood-shadow stays on the street"; "he bursts again through the doors of the bank"; "And the drink and the drugs won't flush him out –"; "he's here in my head when I close my eyes"; "near to the knuckle, here and now".

- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Form: dramatic monologue / spoken testimony.** WJEC-R: "The poem is a dramatic monologue from the point of view of a soldier on active duty." Slang ("legs it", "my mates", "letting fly") keeps it sounding like a real soldier talking. This fits its origin in a real soldier's spoken testimony. *(WJEC-R: "Armitage uses colloquial phrases such as 'legs it', 'Well', 'So we've hit', 'tosses his guts' to create an informal tone as if we are part of a direct conversation with the narrator.")*
  - **Structure: 7 quatrains and a final couplet (30 lines).** I checked this against the AQA-N20-QP text: lines are numbered to 30, the stanzas are 4+4+4+4+4+4+4+2. WJEC-R: "There are 7 stanzas of 4 lines and a final stanza of 2 lines to perhaps suggest the continuous suffering of the soldier." The short final couplet makes the ending feel cut off and unresolved.
  - **Shift in time and place halfway through.** Stanzas 1–4 are the shooting in the war zone. From "End of story, except not really." (line 17) it moves to patrols "week after week", then "home on leave". The second half is all memory, which shows the effects of conflict lasting longer than the event. WJEC-R: "The poem moves from a feeling of collective responsibility in the first half to the narrator's individual feeling of guilt in the second half."
  - **Present tense and repetition.** "I see … I see"; "Sleep, and … Dream, and …"; the repeated "probably armed, possibly not". The flashbacks keep coming back, so the poem's form acts out trauma.
  - **No regular rhyme or metre; heavy alliteration and enjambment.** Examples: "sun-stunned, sand-smothered land"; lines running over stanza breaks ("I swear // I see …", "But I blink // and he bursts …"). WJEC-R: "The lack of rhyme and rhythm emphasise the seriousness of the situation and the soldier's suffering." The run-on stanzas suggest memories he cannot hold back.

- **Context (AO3):**
  1. *The Not Dead* was a Channel 4 film first, then a book. | https://www.simonarmitage.com/works/the-not-dead/ (Armitage's official site) | "The poems in this book were originally aired on a Channel 4 documentary film of the same name, shown in the summer of 2007.   - **Reading (WJEC-R):** the final line "has overtones of Lady Macbeth's actions in Shakespeare's play, where she is desperately trying to rub an imaginary spot of blood off her hands. In both cases, the blood is used as a metaphor for the guilt which cannot be removed."

- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Guilt / memory: **War Photographer** (a haunted man reliving images at home; WJEC-WP lists Remains as a link: "Both poems focus on war, conflicting feelings, trauma, guilt, memories and desensitivity") and **Kamikaze** (memory, life after a war decision).
  - Effects of conflict on the individual soldier: **Bayonet Charge** (one soldier's terror in the moment, compared with Remains' terror afterwards) and **Exposure**. AQA-J17-MS pairs Bayonet Charge with Remains: "Any valid comparison of the effects of war on the individual, such as 'Remains' or 'Exposure'".
  - Power / the individual vs the state: **The Charge of the Light Brigade** (soldiers following orders, glory vs guilt). AQA-J17-MS: "first person in 'Remains', third person in 'the Charge of the Light Brigade'".
  - Place/home: **Poppies** (the war comes home, from a mother's side).

## The Charge of the Light Brigade — Alfred, Lord Tennyson (written 2 Dec 1854; first published in *The Examiner*, 9 Dec 1854)

- **Text checked against:** https://poets.org/poem/charge-light-brigade (Academy of American Poets) and https://poetryarchive.org/poem/charge-light-brigade/. Both give the same 6-stanza text with "dismay'd", "Some one had blunder'd" and "Honor the charge they made!". The only difference I saw: Poetry Archive has no full stop after "Rode the six hundred" at the end of stanza 3. *The spelling "Honor" vs "Honour" and the punctuation in the AQA anthology could not be checked: no AQA paper I opened prints this poem. Check against the anthology.* The poem is out of copyright, so full lines may be quoted.
- **What happens:** In the Crimean War, about 600 British cavalrymen are ordered to charge down a valley at Russian guns. The order is a mistake ("Some one had blunder'd") but they obey without question. They are fired on from all sides, fight their way in, and only some ride back. The poet ends by telling the reader to honour them.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Duty and obedience over questioning: soldiers follow orders even into death. *[conflict/war, patriotism, power (human)]*
  - Glory and heroism: the poem celebrates the men's courage and demands they are remembered. *[pride, patriotism]*
  - A quiet criticism of the commanders: "Some one had blunder'd". *[power (human), conflict/war]*
  - Loss on a huge scale: "All that was left of them". *[loss/grief, effects of conflict]*

- **Key quotations (6):** all verbatim from poets.org; the same text is on Poetry Archive.

| # | Quotation | What it shows / method |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward," | Repetition and a galloping rhythm (stressed–unstressed–unstressed). It copies the horses' hooves and pushes the reader forward, like the charge. |
| 2 | "Into the valley of Death / Rode the six hundred." | A refrain, with Death personified and capitalised. It may echo the Bible (Psalm 23's "valley of the shadow of death", my observation; not sourced). The men ride knowingly towards death. |
| 3 | "Not tho' the soldier knew / Some one had blunder'd:" | The only open hint of criticism: a mistake by an unnamed officer. The vague "Some one" protects the commanders. |
| 4 | "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die:" | Anaphora (the repeated "Theirs") and a tight rhyme. Soldiers' duty is obedience, not thought. This can be read as praise or as quiet protest. |
| 5 | "Into the jaws of Death, / Into the mouth of Hell" | Metaphor: the battlefield as a monster swallowing the men. Repeated (reversed) in stanza 5 with "Came thro' the jaws of Death, / Back from the mouth of Hell,". |
| 6 | "Honor the charge they made! / Honor the Light Brigade, / Noble six hundred!" | Imperatives and exclamations. The poem turns into a public command to remember them: a patriotic, memorial ending. |

- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Narrative poem in third person.** The speaker tells the story from outside ("they", "the six hundred"). AQA-J17-MS refers to "third person in 'the Charge of the Light Brigade'" as a contrast with first-person poems. The men are seen as a heroic group, not as individuals. This is unlike Remains and Bayonet Charge.
  - **Rhythm / metre:** from the text, the lines are short with a falling beat: "HALF a league, HALF a league" (one stress then two unstressed, i.e. dactyls). This creates the galloping horse rhythm. *The label "dactylic dimeter" comes from my scansion only. No allowed source I opened names the metre.*
  - **Structure: six stanzas of uneven length** (I counted 8, 9, 9, 12, 11, 6 lines on the poets.org text). The longest stanza (4) is the fighting at the guns. The short final stanza is the call to "Honor". The refrain "six hundred" changes: "Rode the six hundred" becomes "Not the six hundred" and "Left of six hundred", which tracks the losses.
  - **Repetition and mirroring:** "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them" (stanza 3) becomes "Cannon behind them" (stanza 5). This shows they are surrounded going in and coming out.
  - **Rhyme:** repeated rhymes on "-under'd" (blunder'd / thunder'd / wonder'd / sunder'd / hundred) bind the "blunder" to the noise and the dead. In 1854 reviewers objected to this rhyme (see Context 4).

- **Context (AO3):**
  1. The charge took place on 25 October 1854 at Balaklava in the Crimean War. A misunderstood order sent the Light Brigade at the wrong guns. | https://collection.nam.ac.uk/detail.php?acc=1962-11-4-3 (National Army Museum, "Order that launched the Charge of the Light Brigade, 1854") | "The Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaklava on 25 October 1854 has come to symbolize both the bravery of the British soldier and the incompetence of the Army commanders during the Crimean War (1854-1856)." and "Disastrously, Airey's orders were misunderstood, some of the blame resting with Captain Nolan, who took the message to the Light Brigade." The order read: "Lord Raglan wishes the cavalry to advance rapidly to the front, follow the enemy & try to prevent the enemy carrying away the guns."
  2. Casualties. | NAM (same page) | "In one of the most notorious military blunders in history, over 260 men out of the 673 who attacked the Russian guns were killed or wounded." and "The casualties sustained, along with the loss of 475 horses, meant that the Light Brigade was almost wiped out as an operational force." Also https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/battle-balaklava: "Around 260 men of the Light Brigade's 673 were killed or wounded, and 475 horses were lost." **CONFLICT:** Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Balaklava) says "Out of more than 660 men who had embarked on the charge, 113 were killed, 134 were wounded, and another 45 were captured; some 375 horses had also been killed or were destroyed afterward." Poetry Archive (https://poetryarchive.org/poet/alfred-tennyson/) says "Of the six hundred and seventy three men who charged down 'The Valley of Death' only a hundred and ninety five survived unwounded". Suggest using NAM's "about 260 of 673 killed or wounded". The poem's "six hundred" is a round number.
  3. Tennyson was Poet Laureate and wrote it straight after reading *The Times*. | https://poetryarchive.org/poet/alfred-tennyson/ | "he was made Poet Laureate in 1850 following the death of Wordsworth" and he "wrote his poem in commemoration of their courage only a few minutes after reading an account in _The Times_". Britannica (https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alfred-Lord-Tennyson): "poet laureate (1850-1892)".
  4. Publication in a newspaper, then revisions. | https://popularvictorianpoetry.wordpress.com/topics/tennysons-charge-of-the-light-brigade-and-newspaper-poetry/ (University of Victoria course site, footer "JR/Engl550/Fall2013/UVic") | "Tennyson published his poetic response in the _Examiner_, on 9 December 1854." It was responding to "The _Times_ on 13 November 1854 that reported the tragic deaths of nearly 300 soldiers who charged the wrong way during the battle at Balaklava." The companion page (https://popularvictorianpoetry.wordpress.com/an-anthology-of-popular-victorian-poetry/alfred-lord-tennysons-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-examiner-1854/) says the Examiner text included "someone had blundered" and named Captain Nolan. Both were removed for the *Maud* volume (1855), then Tennyson "changed the ending back to the original one published in The Examiner and restored the line 'someone had blundered.'" *University course pages, so treat as secondary.* The exact date written (2 December 1854) was in search snippets only, from Wikipedia citing Shannon & Ricks 1985. **UNVERIFIED** from an opened allowed page.
  5. The poem was printed for the troops. | UVic newspaper-poetry page above | "An army chaplain in 1855 requested prints of the poem to distribute amongst the troops in the Crimea...Tennyson had 2,000 quarto pamphlets of his poem printed for distribution to the soldiers." **SINGLE SOURCE.**
  - Russell of *The Times* (NAM, https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/battle-balaklava): William Howard Russell's famous phrase is quoted as "They dashed on towards that thin red streak tipped with steel." *(This describes the infantry's "thin red line" earlier in the same battle, not the Light Brigade. Don't confuse them.)*
  - Tennyson's own 1890 recording survives (Poetry Archive, recording "courtesy of the British Library Sound Archive"; poet page: "A remarkable recording made in 1890, these poems and eight others were recorded on a set of twenty three soft wax cylinders").
  - **AQA angle (AQA-J17-MS AO3):** "Any treatment of the relationship between the individual soldiers and those for whom they fought, e.g. comparison with 'The Charge of the Light Brigade' or 'Exposure'". AQA-J19-MS: "possible comparison of the effects of war on attitudes, such as 'The Charge of the Light Brigade' or 'Kamikaze'"; "any ideas about patriotism and duty".

- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Patriotism and duty vs reality: **Bayonet Charge** (Hughes deflates "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera"; Tennyson celebrates honour) and **Exposure**.
  - Power of leaders over ordinary men: **Remains** (orders and their after-effects) and **Kamikaze** (duty to nation).
  - Memory / how war is recorded: **War Photographer** (one poet honours the dead in print; the other shows readers who soon forget).

- **Common misreading to avoid:** none found in an allowed source.

## Bayonet Charge — Ted Hughes (in *The Hawk in the Rain*, 1957 — see Gaps)

- **Text checked against:** AQA-J17-QP (full text, 23 lines, Q26). Short phrases only below.
- **What happens:** A First World War soldier (implied by the "khaki", rifle and bayonet) suddenly finds himself running across a field under fire towards a hedge. Halfway through he almost stops, wondering why he is doing this at all. A wounded hare in the furrows shows the horror, and he charges on. Patriotism and ideals vanish, leaving only terror.
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Patriotic ideals collapse under real combat. *[patriotism, conflict/war, fear]*
  - The individual as a small part of a huge war machine he does not understand. *[power (human), identity]*
  - Nature is harmed by war (the hare). *[power (nature), effects of conflict]*
  - Raw fear and instinct take over from thought. *[fear]*

- **Key quotations (6):** all verified verbatim against AQA-J17-QP.

| # | Quotation | What it shows / method |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Suddenly he awoke and was running – raw" | In medias res. "awoke" suggests he has woken to reality (or from a dream of glory). "raw" means inexperienced and exposed. |
| 2 | "Bullets smacking the belly out of the air –" | Violent onomatopoeia ("smacking") and personification: even the air is wounded. |
| 3 | "The patriotic tear that had brimmed in his eye" | Patriotism is past ("had brimmed"). The next line turns it into "Sweating like molten iron": ideals become physical pain. |
| 4 | "In what cold clockwork of the stars and the nations" | Metaphor. Wars and fate are an uncaring machine, and he is just "the hand pointing that second". It reduces the soldier to a cog in the machine. |
| 5 | "Threw up a yellow hare that rolled like a flame" | The hare is the one bright, natural image, and it is destroyed. The simile "like a flame" links nature to fire and suffering. |
| 6 | "King, honour, human dignity, etcetera" | A list ending in the dismissive "etcetera". The grand ideals "Dropped like luxuries", thrown off as useless. |
| + | "His terror's touchy dynamite." | Last line: fear is now an explosive. He has become a weapon. |

- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Third-person narrative focused on one unnamed soldier ("he").** From the text. This makes him an everyman. *Note: AQA-N20-MS says "first person in 'Bayonet Charge'", which contradicts the printed text ("he"). AQA-J17-MS uses first person for Remains instead. Treat "first person" as an AQA slip and describe Bayonet Charge as third person.*
  - **Structure: three stanzas (8, 7 and 8 lines; 23 lines total, counted on AQA-J17-QP), each a different stage.** Stanza 1 is action. Stanza 2 is time freezing as he questions ("his foot hung like / Statuary in mid-stride"). Stanza 3 returns to action. The slowed middle stanza is where thought briefly breaks into instinct.
  - **Enjambment, dashes and caesura with irregular line lengths and no regular rhyme** (checked against the text: lines end "raw", "heavy", "hedge", "hearing", "air –"). This mimics stumbling, breathless running and panic.
  - **Violent, physical imagery and similes:** "numb as a smashed arm", "molten iron", "shot-slashed furrows". The body and the land are both damaged.
  - **Ending:** the closing image ("touchy dynamite") gives no glory and no resolution, only fear about to explode.

- **Context (AO3):**
  1. Hughes's dates and Poet Laureateship. | https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ted-Hughes | "born August 17, 1930, Mytholmroyd, Yorkshire, England—died October 28, 1998, London"; "Hughes served as poet laureate of Britain from 1984 to 1998."
  2. *The Hawk in the Rain* was his first book (1957). | Britannica (same) | the article says his first volume of verse, *The Hawk in the Rain*, was published in 1957 (paraphrase of the fetched page). **That Bayonet Charge is in this volume is NOT confirmed by an opened allowed page (GAP).**
  3. Hughes's father fought in the First World War, at Gallipoli. | https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/engp04/chapter/ted-hughes/ (Indian UGC university e-text, Dr Anjana Sankar) | "Hughes's father William Hughes, a carpenter and later a shopkeeper, had served in the First World War." and "his father, who fought in the Gallipoli peninsula in April 1915 who was one of the seventeen survivors in the battalion". **SINGLE SOURCE.** Wikipedia cites the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography (Keith Sagar) for this, but I could not open it (paywalled).
  4. War is a theme in his first book. | INFLIBNET (same) | "_The Hawk in the Rain_ anticipates six poems on war". Bayonet Charge is not named in the quoted sentence.
  5. Characteristic style. | Britannica | his "most characteristic verse is without sentimentality, emphasizing the cunning and savagery of animal life in harsh, sometimes disjunctive lines." (Useful for the hare and the violent verbs.)
  - **AQA's angle (AQA-J17-MS, Q26 "effects of war in 'Bayonet Charge'"), AO3:** "Ideas about the realisation of the horror / reality of war"; "Any treatment of the use of individual experience to deal with wider / universal experience".
  - **Author statement:** GAP. The Paris Review interview returned 403.

- **Best comparison partners (Advice):**
  - Patriotism/duty: **The Charge of the Light Brigade** (opposite attitudes to honour) and **Kamikaze**.
  - Fear and the individual soldier: **Remains** (AQA-J17-MS lists it) and **Exposure** (WWI, nature hostile).
  - Power of nature in war: **Exposure** and **Storm on the Island**. AQA-J19-MS: "description of the natural world in 'Kamikaze' or 'Bayonet Charge'".

- **Common misreading to avoid:** none from an allowed source. See the AQA "first person" slip above: don't call it a first-person poem.

## War Photographer — Carol Ann Duffy (from *Standing Female Nude*, Anvil, 1985)

- **Text checked against:** AQA-J19-QP (full text, 24 lines) and https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/war-photographer/ (full text). **Variants:** AQA has "darkroom" and "black-and-white", while the Scottish Poetry Library has "dark room" and "black and white". Use the AQA spellings (exam board text) and check against the anthology.
- **What happens:** A war photographer is back in England, developing his films alone in his darkroom. As a picture of a dying man appears, he remembers the man's wife crying. His hands shake now, though they did not at the scene. His editor will print only a few photos in a Sunday magazine. Readers will feel briefly sad and then forget, and he flies off to the next war knowing "they do not care".
- **Big ideas (AO1):**
  - Remembering and bearing witness: the photographer carries other people's suffering. *[memory, effects of conflict, guilt]*
  - The comfortable public doesn't care: readers' sympathy is brief. *[conflict/war, place/home, power (human)]*
  - Contrast between safe "Rural England" and war zones. *[place/home]*
  - A job, and a duty: he must record events rather than help. *[identity, guilt]*

- **Key quotations (6):** all verified verbatim against AQA-J19-QP.

| # | Quotation | What it shows / method |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "with spools of suffering set out in ordered rows." | Sibilance and a metaphor: films hold "suffering". "ordered rows" also suggests war graves. It shows order imposed on chaos. |
| 2 | "a priest preparing to intone a Mass." | A religious simile/extended metaphor (darkroom as church). His work is solemn, like a funeral rite for the dead. |
| 3 | "Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh. All flesh is grass." | A list of real war zones, with full stops creating caesura (like a roll-call). "All flesh is grass" is a Biblical echo (Isaiah; my identification, not sourced): all life is short. |
| 4 | "which did not tremble then / though seem to now." | Delayed trauma. He was steady on the job but shaky at home, as memory catches up. |
| 5 | "A hundred agonies in black-and-white" | Metonymy: photos are "agonies". The editor picks "five or six", which shows suffering being filtered for the news. |
| 6 | "The reader's eyeballs prick / with tears between the bath and pre-lunch beers." | Rhyme and bathos (a sudden drop from serious to trivial). Readers' sympathy is squeezed between comforts. This is satire of public indifference. |
| + | "he earns his living and they do not care." | Last line. "they" is ambiguous (readers? both sides?), and the line ends on cold indifference. |

- **Methods (AO2):**
  - **Third-person viewpoint.** WJEC-WP: "The poem is written in third person to reflect the detachment a war photographer must feel when doing the job." The narrator keeps the same distance from the photographer that he keeps from his subjects.
  - **Regular form: four six-line stanzas with a consistent rhyme pattern.** WJEC-WP: "There are four stanzas with six lines each, suggesting regularity and the routine nature of his job. Each stanza has a similar and consistent rhyme scheme (ABBCDD)". I checked stanza 1 against the AQA text (alone / rows / glows / he / Mass / grass = A B B C D D), and later stanzas follow the same pattern with some half-rhymes (then / again). WJEC-WP: "The strict, controlled structure contrasts the chaos, confusion and lack of structure in war."
  - **Contrast of places:** "Rural England" where "ordinary pain" can be fixed by weather, against "fields which don't explode beneath the feet / of running children" (verified AQA-J19-QP). This opposes the safe home and the war zone.
  - **Development of a photo as structure.** Stanza 3, "Something is happening", "a half-formed ghost": the image appearing on paper mirrors the memory returning, so memory is staged in real time.
  - **Ending:** a shift to the "aeroplane" and the cold adverb "impassively". AQA-J19-MS AO2: "presentation of perspective, such as contrast between 'hands', 'eyes', 'aeroplane'".
